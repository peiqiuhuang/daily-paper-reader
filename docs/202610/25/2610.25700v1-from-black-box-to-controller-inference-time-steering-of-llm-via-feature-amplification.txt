# From Black Box to Controller: Inference-Time Steering of LLM via Feature Amplification 

## Keqi Chen, Zekai Sun, Yuhua Wen, Tianyi Tian, Xinyang Huang, Huijun Lian, Yingming Gao, Ya Li 

Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications Beijing, China ckqqqq,sunzekai,yuhuawen,tian tianyi,xinyanghuang,lianhuijunlybl,yingming.gao,yli01@bupt.edu.cn 

Abstract 

Inference-Time steering is crucial for deploying Large Lan-guage Models (LLMs) in real-world applications, where they must flexibly adjust behavior preference. For instance, the model should dynamically adjust its caution level based on the risk of the scenario. This requirement introduces a challenge: modulating the intensity of LLM preferences. Prompt engineering can require task-specific prompt design, while fine-tuning requires additional optimization and data. These requirements can make repeated behavioral adjust-ments costly. To address this, we introduce NeuroSteer , a plug-and-play framework that enables flexible, quantitative adjustment of LLM preferences at inference time. NeuroSteer employs a Sparse Autoencoder (SAE) as its controller. Com-paring neuron activation frequencies between positive and negative examples isolates “feature neurons” that correspond to target preferences such as politeness and positive senti-ment. During inference, the activations of selected SAE fea-tures are amplified in the latent representations to steer the output distribution. Users can adjust the strength of this steer-ing on a per-scenario or per-user basis, granting the LLM fine-grained, quantifiable trait modulation. Our experiments show that NeuroSteer can alter sentiment, toxicity, stance, and politeness at inference time. It achieves higher target-direction steering scores than the compared baselines in sev-eral settings, with task- and strength-dependent trade-offs in generation quality. The anonymous demo is accessible. 1.

## Introduction 

As LLMs are deployed widely, there is an increasing ex-pectation for LLMs to adapt their preferences across various scenarios. For instance, LLMs should exercise greater cau-tion when answering financial questions compared to open-domain conversations. Such adjustments are often dynamic. For instance, LLMs are required to increase their ’caution level’ to refuse risk-sensitive questions in some cases. But excessive caution leads to LLMs’ unnecessary refusals and over-refusal, which can result in poor user experience. Con-sequently, scenario-aware adjustments of LLMs’ caution levels are crucial. Similarly, adaptive tuning of other trait dimensions - such as toxicity control and politeness adjust-ment - proves critical for task-specific adaptation. Current solutions for such adaptive tuning include full fine-tuning (Devlin 2018), LoRA (Hu et al. 2021), prompt-

However, these meth-ods require substantial resources, including prompt adjust-ments, datasets, retraining for specific tasks, etc., which makes them less flexible. These approaches do not by them-selves provide a common scalar control for adjusting the strength of a target behavior at inference time. Activation engineering provides new solutions. Stud-ies (Jiang et al. 2024; Hernandez, Li, and Andreas 2024; Park, Choe, and Veitch 2024) show that LM encodes knowl-edge along linear directions in hidden activations, enabling latent edits without weight changes. To adjust model output, activation engineering methods (Elhage et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024; Panickssery et al. 2024) use the steering vector to shift LM activation to a linear direction to change LM output with some promise, but they also raise a question: Do LLMs pos-sess representations of traits like ’emotion’ and ’politeness’? If so, could we achieve quantitative control over an LLM’s trait preferences by amplifying or suppressing representa-arXiv:2610.25700v1 [cs.AI] 19 Oct 2026 tions? Previous Studies (Gurnee and Tegmark 2024) show that LLM activation does not have a single linear dimension to represent traits. The trait is composed of multiple low-dimensional features, such as simple logic and tokens. The combination of these low-dimensional vectors consists of abstract and high-level traits, such as answer rejection or positive emotion. SAE provides a solution capable of capturing and inter-vening in these low-dimensional features. The SAE is an unsupervised method with the original goal of discovering interpretable features. It is trained by reconstructing the ac-tivation vectors of the LLM, which can break down high-dimensional and coupling activations into more fundamen-tal, understandable units. Sparse features provide candidate directions for more targeted interventions. However, sparsity does not guarantee feature independence, and amplifying a feature can still have off-target effects. This allows it to serve as an ”amplifier” of LLM features. Building upon the above exploration, we propose Neu-roSteer, which modulates the degree of targeted behavior preferences in LLMs at inference time. We use SAE as LM’s controller to regulate these features. As shown in Figure 1, the SAE learns to map the high-dimensional features into sparse and basic features. To steer the LM toward a target trait, we activate and combine SAE neurons correspond-ing to basic target features. By amplifying or suppressing these features, LLMs can achieve macro-regulation of out-put preference. During inference, the residual addition of these features adjusts the probability distribution on LLMs’ next-token predictions, steering the LM output toward the desired trait. To select SAE neurons representing target features, we use a frequency filter on comparative data pairs. Neurons that are frequently activated in positive samples but not in negative ones are key to positive (target) traits. Neurons ac-tivated in both samples represent noise and are not activated for steering. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: We develop NeuroSteer, an LLM control framework that introduces the SAE features regulation for inference-time steering. We evaluate NeuroSteer on sentiment, toxicity, stance, and politeness steering tasks. It improves several steering metrics relative to the compared baselines, while exposing trade-offs between steering strength and genera-tion quality. Deeper insights are gained into how concepts are represented in LM by layer, and how amplifying and combining basic features affects the LM’s behavior. 

## Related Work 

The work of NeuroSteer is inspired by previous work on activation engineering and sparse autoencoders. 

Activation Engineering 

Although LM’s activations are often seen as black boxes, studies show they develop semantically meaningful inter-nal structures . Jiang et al. (2024) and Li et al. (2024) sug-gest that semantic concepts are generally encoded linearly in the hidden representation space of LMs. For instance, con-cepts such as sentiment polarity (Tigges et al. 2023), time, and space (Gurnee and Tegmark 2024) can be represented through linear directions in hidden states. Following the idea of linear representations, approaches like Panickssery et al. (2024) compute mean hidden state difference activations along the vector connecting the means of the true and false distributions. As shown in Table 1, these methods employ the mean difference vector to im-prove the truthfulness of LM. Similarly, methods such as LM-steer (Dong et al. 2023; Tigges et al. 2023) steer the LM latent state with steering vectors using SVD decom-position. Most activation engineering methods, such as ITI and CAA (Yunfan et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2025; Stickland et al. 2024) calculate mean differences between positive and negative samples, applying a single steering vector directly on LM hidden states. Despite their potential, activation en-gineering inside LLM faces two critical challenges rooted in the fundamental properties of LM representations. (1) A single steering vector restricts the scope and capability of controlling complex LM behavior. (2) The dense, highly su-perimposed nature of LM hidden layers causes unintended noise during interventions, and calculating the mean differ-ence vector introduces more noise, leading to undesired out-puts. Direct activation editing can therefore introduce off-target effects. As shown by Elhage et al. (2022), individ-ual neurons rarely encode single features – GPT-2’s 768-dimensional neurons must encode over 10 5 distinct fea-tures (Gurnee et al. 2024). This indicates that activating a single LLM neuron can affect a large number of distinct fea-tures in its representation, which introduces noise (Stickland et al. 2024). The mean single difference vector approach limits intervention flexibility. Representing composite be-haviors with a single direction may also limit intervention flexibility.

Sparse Autoencoders 

SAE offers potential for enabling finer-grained, controllable, and interpretable intervention methods. SAEs learn by re-constructing activation vectors between LLM layers through autoencoders, projecting the dense internal activations of LLMs into a sparse representational space to achieve dis-entanglement (Templeton et al. 2024). SAE imposes spar-sity constraints on high-dimensional latent spaces (typically 10-100 times larger than LLM embeddings). This encour-ages sparse activation patterns that can correspond to distinct Figure 2: An overview of the NeuroSteer architecture. The feature amplification includes steps: 1 ⃝ SAE Feature Collections :The SAE collects neuron activation frequencies of positive and negative samples; 2⃝ Feature Filtering : The SAE extracts target neurons and denoises based on frequency differences; 3 ⃝ Inference-time Feature Amplifying : The SAE activates target neuron combinations and decodes their effects into the GPT hidden space, which is also shown in Figure 1. semantic features, without guaranteeing statistical indepen-dence. For example, in GPT-2 SAEs, specific neurons fire exclusively for concepts like ’email addresses’ or ’chemical formulas’ (Kissane et al. 2024). The SAE architecture nat-urally separates superimposed features: Given an encoded representation on LM, the encoder produces sparse features, where only a small subset of neurons are activated, while the decoder reconstructs the original encoded representation. The overcomplete basis provides candidate features for tar-geted interventions. Feature overlap and unintended effects can nevertheless remain and must be evaluated empirically. 

## Method 

NeuroSteer introduces the SAE as a controller. During for-ward propagation, the SAE amplifies a set of selected sparse features, thereby applying a preference steering effect on the LLM’s output. 

Method Overview 

As illustrated in Figure 2, NeuroSteer controls LLM prefer-ence degree through trained sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and target positive/negative data sampling. This method first re-quires SAE pretraining. Training on any pretrained corpora like OpenWebText enables SAEs to learn mappings between token activations and fundamental features. Building on pre-trained SAEs, we then perform forward propagation to filter target features for specific behavior preference. In detail, we activate the LLM with positive/negative data pairs, recording SAE activation frequencies, computing inter-sample neu-ronal activation differences, and ultimately selecting target features. During inference, NeuroSteer amplifies these tar-get features by a certain ratio to achieve real-time steering of the generation process and modulation of model prefer-ences. 

SAE Training 

The SAE is an unsupervised autoencoder method. It en-codes the residual activations of each layer in the LLM us-ing an autoencoder, mapping them to a high-dimensional sparse space, and then reconstructs the activation vectors through the decoder. The SAE has two losses: MSE loss 

measures the difference between the reconstructed activa-tion and the original activation, and the other is the L1 loss for SAE sparsity. We follow the training setting of pre-vious works (Kissane et al. 2024; Templeton et al. 2024) to pretrain the SAE. Once the SAE is trained, quantifi-able adjustments for each specific task can be achieved in a high-dimensional sparse space through forward propaga-tion, without requiring any gradient updates. 

Data Pairs 

NeuroSteer’s feature collection relies on a small batch of positive-negative sample pairs. As illustrated in the fig-ures 2, the sentiment steering task employs opposite text pairs which are easily accessible in classification datasets. Activation sampling of positive and negative examples cap-tures feature distinctions between target preferences. Such data suit the modulation of base models lacking prompt-following capabilities such as gpt-2. Data pair samples can also be obtained through prompt-mask sampling. For instance, the common response to ’Please tell me about Microsoft’s revenue’ differs from the cautious version ’Please tell me about Microsoft’s revenue, but don’t provide sensitive data on private dataset.’ By sam-pling answer activations from these paired prompts, the SAE collects steering target feature differentiae for adjusting the degree of caution.

Transfer to Other Tasks: 

In conclusion, this section demonstrates NeuroSteer’s out-put steering capability through emotional polarity reversal in LLM, transforming positive sentiment to negative. The steering mechanism extends to toxicity mitigation via data pairs of toxic and non-toxic samples. Further generalization enables stance modification and politeness adjustment, as empirically validated in our experiments. 

## Experiment 

The SAE learns and pre-trains based on fundamental fea-tures, making it a cross-task controller. Task-specific feature selection uses labeled examples without updating the base model weights. We evaluate four steering tasks: Sentiment Steering , Detoxify , Stance Steering , and Politeness Steer-ing .All experiments follow similar data and evaluation frame-works: first, extracting the former part of the prompt with different polarities. These prompts tend to induce corre-sponding polarized outputs. For instance, when given a prompt with negative sentiment in the first sentence, the model is more likely to generate a continuation with simi-larly negative sentiment in the latter sentence. After gener-ating the target polarity steering texts, a classifier is used to classify the polarity of all generated texts. The proportion of generated text with the target polarity steering is then cal-culated. This proportion serves as a measure of the steering capability of the method, with a higher change indicating stronger steering ability.

The sentiment steering and detoxification tasks have pro-vided baselines, making them suitable for cross-method comparison. The stance and politeness steering tasks are in-novative tasks that have been scarcely explored in previous papers. So we reproduce baseline results for our method on these two tasks. 

Sentiment Steering: In this task, we calculate and select the steering neurons on 3452 data samples from Stanford Sen-timent Treebank (SST5, Socher et al. 2013) for sentiment steering. SST5 samples are classified for positive or nega-tive sentiment and are used to compute positive and nega-tive frequency differences for targeted neuron selection. We assess the model for sentiment evaluation using the multi-class sentiment analysis dataset (Shrivastava n.d.), an out-of-domain dataset with 2500 prompts from an Open-Web sentiment classifier (Hartmann et al. 2023).

Steering Baselines 

Trained methods include DAPT (Gururangan et al. 2020) and PromptT5 (Raffel et al. 2020), which pretrain the LM on a non-toxic subset of OpenWebText (filtered via the Per-spective API), and PPLM (Dathathri et al. 2019), which uses gradients from sentiment and toxicity classifiers to up-date the LM’s hidden representations. Additionally, LM-Steer (Han et al. 2024) freezes the GPT layers and adds a fine-tuning layer. Plug-and-Play methods include GeDi (Krause et al. 2020), which uses bayesian rules for class-conditioned generation; MuCoLa (Kumar, Paria, and Tsvetkov 2022), which formulates text generation as an optimization problem based on classifier scores; and DExperts (Liu et al. 2021), which offsets the LM logits using the difference between positive and negative label classifier scores. Activation engineering method includes LM Steering ,which uses the mean single difference vector to steer LLM on target behaviors. Due to the lack of relevant baselines on stance and polite-ness experiments, we provided GPT-2 with full fine-tuning 

and LoRA as baselines in stance and politeness steering ex-periments. 

Evaluation metrics 

Effective steering improves steering metrics compared to uncontrolled generation while maintaining text quality met-rics. 

Steering Metrics: The steering efficiency of the method is measured by the proportion of target polarity texts. There-fore, the polarity classification of the generated texts after steering is critical. In the sentiment steering task, we use an outstanding sentiment classifier (Hartmann et al. 2023) for sentiment classification. In the detoxification task, the Google Perspective API is employed to assess the toxic-ity polarity of the controlled generations. For the innovative tasks, we carefully design two prompts to instruct the Qwen-based evaluator (Bai et al. 2023) to classify the generated text for stance and politeness polarity. 

Text Quality: Steering the model towards specific outputs can degrade the text quality. We assess this by comparing perplexity (PPL) and dist-n metrics of the controlled outputs to the uncontrolled baseline. Lower PPL indicates greater likelihood under the evaluation model, while higher dist-n indicates greater lexical diversity. These metrics capture flu-ency and lexical diversity, but do not by themselves establish factual accuracy, coherence, or task success.

Computing Efficiency 

In the reported timing setup, NeuroSteer required less task-specific preparation time than LoRA. In tests, NeuroSteer (CPU-only, Intel Xeon) processed 3,000 samples in 14% of the time required by LoRA (GPU-accelerated, NVIDIA RTX 4090). It achieves comparable performance using 600 samples, reported as 30% of the data used in the correspond-ing comparison. Because the methods use different hardware and preparation procedures, this observation does not estab-lish a hardware-matched speedup or an end-to-end inference latency improvement. The pretrained SAE also has an up-front training cost. 

Ablation Steering 

Figure 3: The overview of the steering experiment across layers. (a) shows that greener outputs correspond to better sentiment reversal performance, while (b) demonstrates that greener results maintain higher text quality. The parameter values inside the dashed-line box balance behavior change and text quality preservation. Since different layers of LM represent distinct semantic information, the choice of layer to steer impacts the inter-vention effect. To understand the semantic representation across LM layers, we expanded the grid search in the sen-timent control experiment to examine the behavior of SAE neurons across different GPT layers. We varied both the layer (0–11) and the Top-K value of target SAE neurons, sorting them based on activation frequency differences (K), with higher activity in the target data and lower activity in the source data. Figure 3 shows layer-dependent steering behavior across the 12 GPT-2 layers. Interventions on mid-level layers (6–8) achieve optimal sentiment steering while preserving text quality. Surface layers (0–2) lead to repetitive negative to-kens (e.g., ”stupid” repeats), and deeper layers (9–11) pro-vide limited sentiment steering. These observations char-acterize the tested interventions; they do not establish that deeper layers lack sentiment representations. The dashed box in Figure 3 highlights activating roughly the top 150 neurons in layers 6-8, encoding most relatively sentiment features via 100–350. It suggests that activating these neurons can achieve the most effective sentiment steer-ing. Conclusion 

NeuroSteer is a plug-and-play framework that steers LM be-havior by efficiently activating targeted SAE neurons, sup-porting adjustable interventions without updating the base model weights once an SAE is available. Our work recon-ceptualizes LLMs activation from black boxes into SAE adjustable controllers for behavioral modulation. It demon-strates potential across multiple application scenarios. For example, in educational QA, it may adjust response styles according to users’ academic phases (e.g., elementary, mid-dle, and high school levels), achieving context-aware com-munication. In conclusion, our method provides a potential solution for building quantifiable, context-aware, and flex-ible adaptive AI systems. The reported results have limita-tions. Steering strength can reduce fluency, baseline model sizes and timing hardware differ, and the compositional and caution-level examples are exploratory. Broader evaluation is needed to assess robustness, factual accuracy, and off-target effects.
