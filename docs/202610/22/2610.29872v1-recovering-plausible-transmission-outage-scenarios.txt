Abstract —Expansion planning for extreme weather needs out-age scenarios that specify which transmission branches fail. However, public outage records often only report customers without power by county, making it unclear how to exploit this information to create outage scenarios. We propose a method to recover the sets of outaged branches that most closely match regional load shed data. This gives us sets of plausible outages for a given hazard event. We formulate a bilevel problem with a load shedding DC optimal power flow in the lower level. We relax the binary branch states to differentiate the lower level problem, replace it with its sensitivity matrix, and use gradient descent to update the upper level. We treat the relaxed branch states as probabilities and perform randomized rounding to restore integrality. On three test systems with synthetic outages, the recovered sets reduce the load shed error to 0.02–4.5%. On the California Test System with storm data from December 2025, the error falls to 23%. These scenarios give expansion planning a reliable source of outage data tied to observed hazards. 

Index Terms —grid resilience, outage scenarios, DC optimal power flow, implicit differentiation, randomized rounding. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# EXTREME weather is the leading cause of large power outages in the United States [1], [2]. Hurricanes Ike (2008), Sandy (2012), and Ida (2021) each destroyed major transmission infrastructure and left millions of customers without power [1], [3]. The frequency and intensity of these events have increased substantially in the last decade alone [1], [4], and planners are evaluating investments such as line hardening [3], undergrounding [5], and transmission expansion to limit their impact [6]. Studies of these investments represent a hazard by an outage scenario, which is a set of transmission branches that become out of service during the event. The conclusions of such a study depend on whether its outage scenarios reflect how real hazards damage the grid. The EAGLE-I dataset [7] records the number of customers without power in each U.S. county every 15 minutes, and

the Storm Events Database of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) [8] records the counties that each storm affected. Synthetic grids, realistic but not real grid models [9]–[13], only provide geolocated buses, generators, branches, and limits. These public sources do not identify the branches that failed during a historical event. Utilities record detailed outage information through systems such as NERC’s Transmission Availability Data System [14], but detailed component-level outage records are generally not publicly available [15]. Without fine-grained outage records, it is unclear how planners should build outage scenarios from these past events. We model this ambiguity to generate mul-tiple plausible outage scenarios that planners can incorporate into their expansion planning models. NERC Reliability Standard TPL-001 requires transmission planners to evaluate selected extreme events specified as losses of network elements rather than as outcomes of hazards that a region experienced [16]. Although hazards such as wildfires and severe weather are listed as possible initiating conditions, the modeled events are defined in terms of network component failures rather than derived from a failure model specific to each hazard. St¨ urmer et al. [3] combine wind fields of histor-ical hurricanes with fragility curves for transmission lines to sample line failures in a synthetic Texas grid. This approach captures the physics of each hazard and requires a hazard model and fragility data for every component type. Statistical models predict customer outages from weather, environmental, and infrastructure data [4], [17], [18]. These models are calibrated to observed outages, and their predictions stay at the regional resolution of the outage data. Line outage identification methods locate outaged lines from phasor angle measurements [19], [20], which observe the network state at many buses. County outage data provide only one aggregate value per county. A recent review has holistically examined uncertainty in capacity expansion planning and found that current scenario generation may fail to provide ensembles of projections that properly account for future unseen risks [21]. We propose a method to recover the sets of outaged branches that most closely match regional load shed data. Many different sets of branch outages produce nearly the same regional load shed, so the method returns a ranked collection arXiv:2610.29872v1 [eess.SY] 21 Oct 2026 of outage scenarios for each event. We call these the plausible 

outage scenarios for the event. We formulate the recovery as a bilevel problem. The upper level chooses binary branch states to minimize the squared error between simulated and observed regional load shed. The lower level is a DC optimal power flow (OPF) that minimizes load shed for the topology chosen by the upper level. Network interdiction models have the same structure, with an upper level that chooses branch outages to maximize the load shed of a DC OPF [22]. Our upper level instead matches an observed pattern of load shed. An overview of the methodology is shown in Figure 1. The simplest alternative is to replace the lower level problem with its optimality conditions and solve the result as a single mixed integer program [23]. This reformulation needs a binary variable for every complementarity condition of the lower level problem in addition to one for each candidate branch, and the California Test System alone has 4,313 candidate branches. A greedy search that trips one branch at a time needs a DC OPF solve for every remaining candidate at every step. We instead use the sensitivities of the lower level problem to move all candidate branches at once. Recent work [24] uses a similar approach to solve a different bilevel expansion planning problem. Our upper level problem chooses branch states to match observed regional load shed, which requires (i) relaxing binary branch states so that the lower level problem is differentiable and (ii) a rounding rule that distinguishes low loading from a true line outage. We replace the lower level problem with its load shed sensitivity matrix, which we compute by implicit differentiation of the DC OPF optimality conditions [24], [25], and use gradient descent to update the upper level. We then restore integrality by using randomized rounding [24], [26], setting the outage probability of each branch based on how far the searched moved it from initialization. Repeating the search from several randomized starting points produces the ranked collection of plausible outage scenarios. The contributions of this paper are as follows.  

> •

We formulate the recovery of transmission outage sce-narios from regional load shed data as a bilevel problem with a load shedding DC OPF in the lower level.  

> •

We develop a solution method for outage recovery that re-laxes the branch states by scaling both branch susceptance and branch capacity, computes load shed sensitivities by implicit differentiation, starts each branch at its own critical loading, and samples outage scenarios according to how far the search moved each branch.  

> •

We show on three test systems with synthetic outages (RTS-GMLC, IEEE 300, WECC 240) and on the Cali-fornia Test System (CATS) with observed outages from a December 2025 storm that many distinct outage scenarios fit the same regional data, which suggests that planning studies should use a collection of scenarios for each event. Section II states the problem and our assumptions. Sec-tion III describes the solution method. Section IV reports results on synthetic outages, and Section V applies the method to a storm in California. Section VI concludes and outlines future work. II. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

We consider a single hazard event and the state of the grid at one point in time during it. Regional load shed observations and the geographic extent of the hazard are observed, but not which specific transmission branches that failed. We seek the sets of branches whose outages can explain the observed load shed. This section defines the data that the method uses, states our assumptions, and formulates the recovery as a bilevel problem. The upper level chooses which candidate branches trip. The lower level dispatches generation and sheds load on the resulting topology, subject to the DC power flow model, generator limits, and branch flow limits.

A. Data 

The California Test System (CATS) [10] is a geolocated synthetic model of the California grid with 8,870 buses (Table II). We assign each bus to a county using its coor-dinates and county boundaries from the California Open Data Portal [38]. This gives 59 regions, which are the 58 counties and one region for the buses in neighboring states. Del Norte County contains no CATS buses. A storm crossed the central California coast on December 24, 2025 and left thousands of customers without power around Santa Cruz and Monterey [39], [40]. We take the num-ber of customers without power in each county at 8:30 AM from EAGLE-I [7]. We convert customers to load shed by multiplying by 41.6 kW per customer, which is the sum of the average monthly consumption per customer in the residential, commercial, and industrial sectors (503, 5,398, and 25,073 kWh) from 2024 sales data of the U.S. Energy Information Administration for California [41], divided by the 744 hours in the month. The storm regions are the 32 counties with NOAA storm events on that day [8], and the risk set contains 4,313 lines. Outages appear in 33 counties and total 14.86 p.u., or 12.37 p.u. after we cap each county at its modeled demand. The error with no outages is E0 = 27 .02 .Twelve of the 33 counties with outages, including Sonoma and Santa Cruz, are not storm regions and account for 6.30 of E0.

C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We presented a method that recovers plausible transmission outage scenarios from regional load shed data. It turns public county outage records into branch outage scenarios that a planning model can use directly. On three test systems with synthetic outages, the best outage scenarios leave 0.02% to 4.52% of E0 unexplained, and rounding the relaxed solution outperforms rounding a shuffled or unoptimized vector in every case but one. On CATS with observed outages from a December 2025 storm, the best outage scenario leaves 23.12% of E0 unexplained and concentrates in the counties with the largest observed load shed. Our results indicate that regional load shed constrains the outage scenario only loosely. The best outage scenarios match the regional data closely while sharing few lines with the true outages, and several distinct scenarios reach similar error. A planning study should therefore draw from the ranked collection of outage scenarios for each event and not treat one scenario as the true outage. The assumptions bound these results, and future work will relax them.
