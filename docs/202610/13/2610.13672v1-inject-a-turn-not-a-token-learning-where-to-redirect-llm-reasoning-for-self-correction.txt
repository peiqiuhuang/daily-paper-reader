ABSTRACT 

Large language models struggle to reliably identify and correct errors in their own reasoning. Unlike prior training-based approaches that fine-tune an LLM to generate revisions or feedback, we learn where to intervene in failed rollouts and determine how to redirect them. To choose where to intervene, we train a 130K-parameter MLP head on a frozen encoder using only final-answer correctness. For redirection, we find that under a fixed generation budget, the tested inline injections fail to reliably redirect reasoning, whereas presenting the same instruction in a new user turn changes the model’s course. The resulting correction framework, UPTURN (User-Prompt TURN injection at a learned position), requires no LLM fine-tuning: it retains a selected prefix, injects an instruction as a new user turn, and regenerates the suffix. Across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UP-TURN improves average accuracy in every setting over a 32-rollout baseline. It outperforms self-correction and reflection-token injection baselines under matched correction budgets, transfers across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond without retraining, and complements GRPO and self-correction methods.

Large language models (LLMs) reason autoregressively , so a single mistake can derail subsequent reasoning, making effective correction important. Yet, given only its own rollout, a model may struggle to determine whether it has erred, and intrinsic self-correction without external feedback can even degrade reasoning accuracy (Huang et al., 2024). This motivates learning a correction signal from data. Existing methods do so by fine-tuning the reasoner to produce a revised answer (Kumar et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2024) or by training an external critic or verifier to provide step-level feedback or scores (Lightman et al., 2024; Paul et al., 2024). Although effective, these methods require substantial correction data and typically optimize an LLM-scale component. This raises a question: "Does correction really require training an entire LLM?" Repeated sampling shows that LLMs can solve far more problems than in a single attempt, with pass@ k increasing as k

Yet individual rollouts can still fail: because autoregressive generation conditions each step on the preceding reasoning, even a single mistake can steer the subsequent trajectory away from a correct solution. A failed rollout need not involve many independent errors. Correction may therefore involve redirecting the model toward a solution it can already reach, rather than teaching it to reason de novo . We thus frame correction around two questions: where to intervene in a failed rollout and how to redirect it. We study these questions in a fixed-budget prefix-regeneration setting: after selecting a cut, we retain the prefix and regenerate only the suffix under the original response-token limit. Answering the where 

question requires selecting a single cut position in a failed rollout rather than generating a revised answer or written feedback. We train a 130K-parameter MLP head on a frozen Qwen3-0.6B encoder to favor cut positions that more often lead to correct answers upon regeneration. To address how 

to redirect the rollout, we consider token injection. Reflection tokens such as “wait” mark high-entropy forks where a reasoning path can branch in new directions (Wang et al., 2025), suggesting that they might steer a rollout. Indeed, s1 (Muennighoff et al., 2025) improves accuracy by appending 

“wait” when the model attempts to stop, extending its reasoning. This suggests a simple recipe: learn where to intervene, inject a reflection token, and let the model continue. However, our experiments reveal a critical limitation of inline injection under the fixed-budget protocol. Across the tested reflection tokens ( “wait” , “hmm” ), explicit instructions ( “Solve with a different way.” ), and even a task-irrelevant word ( “Turtle” ), inline injection fails to reliably redirect the rollout. The key difference is conversational structure : the same instruction can redirect reasoning when delivered as a new user turn (Figure 1, left), whereas inline delivery does not reliably do so. These results suggest that the model responds more effectively to an instruction delivered in a user turn than to the same text inserted into its own continuation. We refer to this structural transition from an assistant continuation to a new user turn as a turn boundary .Putting these two pieces together, we propose UPTURN (User-Prompt TURN injection at a learned position), a correction framework that requires no LLM fine-tuning. UPTURN keeps the reasoner and the 0.6B encoder frozen and trains a position policy, implemented as a 130K-parameter MLP head, to select where a fixed instruction is injected through a turn boundary. A separately trained 66K-parameter gate identifies which rollouts to correct at inference. Rather than identify the error or generate a fix, the position policy chooses where to redirect the rollout, while the frozen reasoner performs the correction itself. Averaged across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UPTURN raises accuracy from 37.1% to 55.3% (Figure 1, right). It outperforms self-correction and reflection-token injection baselines under matched correction budgets, as well as heuristic cuts and inline injection. Our contributions are threefold: • We show that conversational structure matters for correction: the tested inline injections fail to reliably redirect reasoning, whereas the same instruction delivered through a turn boundary can. • We learn cut positions with a 130K-parameter head on a frozen encoder, using only regenerated rollouts’ final-answer correctness, without step-level annotations or reasoner fine-tuning. • We demonstrate consistent accuracy gains across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, transfer across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond without retraining, and complementary gains on top of GRPO and existing self-correction methods. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Prompting-based self-correction. Prompting-based methods improve outputs without updating model weights, typically by having the model critique or reflect on its own attempt and then revise it (Madaan et al., 2023). Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), for example, generates a verbal reflection on the previous attempt and uses it to guide a fresh attempt. These methods rely on the reasoner to diagnose its own errors, which can be unreliable for reasoning (Huang et al., 2024; Tie et al., 2025). UPTURN asks the reasoner for neither a critique nor a rewrite: a learned position policy selects where to intervene, and a fixed user-turn instruction redirects the existing rollout. 

Training-based self-correction. Training-based methods learn corrective signals from data, some by fine-tuning the reasoner to revise its own answers (Qu et al., 2024); SCoRe (Kumar et al., 2025), for example, trains the model with RL to produce a revised second attempt conditioned on its first. 2Preprint. Others train a separate model to localize errors, such as a critic whose step-level feedback guides the reasoner’s revision (Paul et al., 2024) or a process reward model that scores each step (Lightman et al., 2024). These approaches typically train an LLM-scale component using correction data, such as revision pairs or step-level annotations. UPTURN instead trains lightweight heads to select which rollouts to correct and where to intervene, rather than generating revisions, critiques, or step-level scores, while keeping the reasoner frozen. 

Reflection-token injection. Several methods improve inference-time performance by injecting or promoting reflection tokens in a frozen model’s reasoning. They append a token such as “wait” 

when the model attempts to stop (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Ringel et al., 2025), or encourage reflection tokens during generation on a predetermined schedule through direct insertion or logit upweighting (Zhang et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2026). These methods steer an ongoing generation from within the model’s own response, whereas we study post-hoc correction of completed rollouts flagged as likely incorrect. In this setting, we find that how the text is delivered matters: under a matched generation budget, the tested inline injections fail to reliably redirect reasoning even at learned cut positions (§6.5), whereas the same instruction delivered as a separate user turn does.

Mathematical reasoning with LLMs. In the standard math setup, the model answers each problem with a chain-of-thought rollout o (Wei et al., 2022) in a single assistant turn via Eq. Group Relative Policy Optimization. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) is a policy-gradient method widely used to post-train LLM reasoners. Unlike PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), it removes the learned value network: for each input, it samples G responses and forms advantages by centering and scaling their rewards with the group mean and standard deviation. It then applies PPO’s clipped surrogate objective, typically with a KL penalty to a reference policy. We adapt this group-relative construction to train the position policy, with the modifications described in Sec. 5.2. 

4 MOTIVATION 

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) has enabled LLMs to produce high-quality mathematical solutions. Prior work (Wang et al., 2025) shows that RLVR updates concentrate on high-entropy “forking” tokens (e.g., Wait , Alternatively ), with smaller updates at the more numerous low-entropy tokens. These high-entropy tokens correspond to decision points where the reasoning can branch. This observation motivates the following question for reasoning correction: If RLVR updates concen-trate on such decision points during training, could intervening at analogous points during inference help correct a failed reasoning trace? 4.1 RECOVERY DEPENDS ON THE RETAINED PREFIX 

Failed traces may contain useful prefixes that can lead to correct answers. Restarting from scratch discards these prefixes, whereas regeneration from a suitable cut preserves them. To test this, we collected failed Qwen3-4B (Thinking on) traces on AIME 2026 and extracted ten prefixes at equally spaced token positions from each trace. We sample 16 such continuations per prefix without any injected text and define the continuation success rate V of each prefix as the fraction of continuations with correct final answers. As a reference, V0 denotes the success rate when the model generates from the question alone, with no retained prefix. In the traces shown in Figure 2a, continuation success rates are high at earlier prefixes but drop sharply and remain low at later ones. This suggests that the choice of retained prefix can substantially affect the model’s ability to recover. The sharp decline may mark a branching point beyond which recovery becomes difficult. This motivates cutting before the transition to preserve a prefix from which the model can still recover. 4.2 INLINE VS . M ULTI -TURN INJECTION 

The previous subsection motivates where to intervene: before a potential branching point marked by a sharp drop in continuation success. We now ask how to redirect reasoning at a given cut position. Using the recovery protocol in Sec. 4.1, we compare injection variants at the sampled cut positions. At each position t, we retain the prefix o≤t, discard the remaining suffix, and append the injected text c within the same assistant turn before regeneration. We test reflection tokens such as Wait 

and Hmm , unrelated words such as turtle and banana , and longer instructions such as “Solve with a different way.” . For each rollout and cut position, only c varies across the inline variants. Inline injection is expressed as 

Cin = [ USER : q] ∥ [ASST : o≤t, c ], (3) where c = ( c1, . . . , c m) denotes the injected text. Across the tested texts, inline injection yields similar continuation success rates. Surprisingly, these rates remain close to those obtained by regenerating from the prefix without injection (Figures 2b and 2c). We next deliver the same text c through the chat template as a new user turn. We close the current assistant turn, insert a user turn containing c, and open a new assistant turn for regeneration: 

Cmt = [ USER : q] ∥ [ASST : o≤t] ∥ [USER : c] ∥ [ASST : ·]. (4) This multi-turn format yields substantially higher continuation success rates than inline injection. 4Preprint. These results show that the injection format matters beyond instruction wording. Both contexts use the same frozen model pθ and injected text c, but differ in their conditioning format. In Cin , the short 

c (m ≪ t) follows a long sequence of assistant tokens and may therefore be interpreted as part of the ongoing reasoning rather than as a revision signal. In Cmt , the chat template instead presents the same 

c as an external instruction in a new user turn, which may help redirect the continuation. Figure 6 in Appendix A illustrates this contrast: inline continuation stays on the original path, whereas a new user turn redirects reasoning.

The reasoner pθ (e.g., Qwen3-4B) produces both the original and revised rollouts, while the position policy πϕ selects the cut. Given a question q and a failed rollout o, a frozen Qwen3-0.6B causal encoder E produces a hidden state ht at each token ot. Because the encoder is causal, each candidate’s score fϕ(ht) depends only on the question and retained prefix o≤t, not on the discarded suffix. To reduce the cost of evaluating regeneration rewards at every token, we restrict cuts to paragraph boundaries ( \n\n ), natural breaks between reasoning steps. Let T (o) denote the token positions at the ends of these boundaries. Appendix H shows that next-token entropy is elevated at paragraph boundaries, supporting these candidate cuts. We also measure end-to-end runtime, including gating and position-selection overhead (Appendix E). 6.2 MAIN RESULTS 

Table 1 compares UPTURN@16 with Base@32 across six benchmarks and four reasoners spanning model families and sizes from 4B to 32B. Despite Base receiving 32 full rollouts, UPTURN improves average accuracy on every benchmark and pass rate in all but one setting. This consistency supports the effectiveness of its correction mechanism. Gains are pronounced on AIME, whereas accuracy gains are smallest on MATH500 for every reasoner, where Base accuracy is already high. The largest improvements occur on DeepSeek-R1-Qwen3-8B, reaching +47 .2 points in average accu-racy on AMC23 and +53 .3 points in pass rate on AIME24. These gains highlight the effectiveness of redirecting existing rollouts under a constrained generation budget, where DeepSeek-R1-Qwen3-8B’s long reasoning traces are frequently truncated. The benefits also persist on QwQ-32B: average accuracy improves on all six benchmarks, while pass rate improves on five and remains unchanged on HMMT. Results on two additional RL-finetuned reasoners show the same trend (Appendix N). 7 CONCLUSION 

We frame reasoning correction as redirecting an existing trajectory rather than retraining the reasoner. UPTURN builds on the observation that inline text is absorbed into the assistant’s reasoning, whereas the same instruction delivered across a turn boundary can redirect it. A lightweight gate flags likely failures, a 130K-parameter head on a frozen encoder selects a cut that preserves a useful prefix, and the frozen reasoner regenerates from a fixed instruction injected as a new user turn. Across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UPTURN consistently improves average accuracy and outperforms self-correction and reflection-token injection baselines under matched correction budgets, as well as heuristic cuts and inline injection. It transfers across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond without retraining, and provides additional gains on top of GRPO and existing self-correction methods. When the required capability is already present, a small learned intervention can thus recover a better reasoning path without retraining the reasoner; extension to code and tool use remains open. 9Preprint. B LIMITATIONS 

UPTURN is a first step toward correcting reasoning by redirection rather than retraining, and several aspects remain open. Our study focuses on reasoning tasks whose outcomes can be checked automatically, and on a single, deliberately minimal form of intervention. Richer interventions, and settings where success is harder to verify, may call for different choices of what to inject and where. We leave these extensions to future work. 

C IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Position policy architecture. The position head fϕ operates on the 1024-dimensional last-layer hidden states of the frozen Qwen3-0.6B encoder. It applies LayerNorm to the input, followed by a linear layer 1024 → 128 , a GELU activation, and a linear layer 128 → 1 that produces the logit of each candidate position, totaling 133,377 parameters (about 130K), the only trainable parameters of the position policy. The encoder receives exactly the token sequence the reasoner produced, the chat-templated prompt tokens concatenated with the rollout tokens, with no re-tokenization, as all our reasoners share the encoder’s 151,643-token Qwen vocabulary. The delimiter \n\n is a single token in this tokenizer, and a failed rollout contains 11.3 candidate positions on average (median 5). 

Training details. We train on DAPO-17K, a 17K-prompt subset of the DAPO-Math training pool. With dynamic sampling (at most 8 generation batches per step), each of the 30 training steps collects 

B=64 prompts containing at least one failed rollout, so training consumes 1,920 kept prompts in total. The head is updated once per step, sweeping all failed rollouts of the batch in mini-batches of 32 for one epoch, so the behavior policy πϕold is simply the policy that generated the step’s data. Rollouts and regenerations use identical sampling settings. When a cut position falls inside an unclosed <think> 

block, we append </think> before injection so that the assistant turn remains well-formed, at both training and inference. Table 6 lists all hyperparameters. 

Inference settings. The gate threshold is γ=0 .4; Appendix D shows that results are robust to it. Each flagged rollout is corrected once and replaced by its regeneration, with no iterative rounds. The cut position is sampled from πϕ(· | q, o ).

Data separation. All evaluation benchmarks (AIME24/25/26, AMC23, HMMT, MATH500) are held-out test sets and do not overlap with the DAPO training pool. The gate’s 1,197-prompt pool (Appendix D) is an independently sampled, topic-balanced subset of the same DAPO training pool. 13 Preprint.

H PARAGRAPH BOUNDARIES ARE FORKING POINTS 

UPTURN restricts candidate cuts to \n\n boundaries. To check that this does not miss where a trace actually forks, we compute the next-token entropy at every position of the incorrect rollouts from the 16-rollout pool of Table 1 on AIME24/25, using each reasoner itself.
