# From Copying to Composition: How Language Models Develop In-Context Learning 

Minglai Yang 1* Xinyu Guo 2 Renxiong Wang 1 Sunjie Hou 1 Daniel Yue Zhang 1 Yunzhong He 11Scale AI 2University of Arizona 

minglai.yang@scale.com 

Abstract 

In-context learning (ICL) allows language mod-els to adapt their behavior from instructions and demonstrations in a prompt, but accuracy alone does not reveal how contextual information con-trols their predictions. We study the acquisition and trainability of ICL with a controlled diag-nostic that separates copying a nearby demon-stration from integrating information across the prompt. Across 17 models, about a third pre-fer the copied answer, a few are undecided, and the rest prefer the rule-consistent answer, which goes with higher accuracy and with con-trol by the requested value rather than the clos-est conflicting example. Checkpoint analy-ses show that models begin prioritizing task-relevant prompt tokens over conflicting exam-ples long before their accuracy improves be-yond chance level. In matched training experi-ments, synthetic documents that define endings and then query them shift models toward com-positional responses on the controlled diagnos-tic, whereas fine-tuning on definition exercises built from rewritten real documents improves accuracy when prompts reassign label mean-ings. The results suggest that ICL is not ac-quired as a single, uniform capability. Instead, prompt control, answer preference, construc-tion without candidates, and transfer can de-velop along distinct trajectories. 

1 Introduction 

Language models are increasingly expected to adapt at inference time, from instructions, demon-strations, and temporary conventions in the prompt rather than from parameter updates. This ability is commonly described as in-context learning (ICL). We know much less about how this capacity is ac-quired during training than about how well trained models display it. Understanding ICL acquisition requires tracking how models shift their reliance on

contextual information throughout training. Know-ing when accuracy improves is not enough. The same answer can result from different uses of the context: a model may reuse the output pat-tern of a demonstration similar to the query, or integrate mappings distributed across the prompt and apply them to a new combination. In ordinary ICL evaluations these behaviors predict the same output, so correctness shows that the context was useful but not how it was used. Studying the acqui-sition of ICL therefore needs a diagnostic in which different ways of using the prompt make different predictions. These observations motivate a developmental question that final-answer accuracy alone cannot resolve: how does a model’s use of different sources of contextual information change during training? We track four aspects of ICL that, as we show, develop on different schedules: which prompt source controls the answer, which answer the model prefers, whether it can construct that answer without candidates, and whether a trained change transfers to other evaluations. To make these aspects distinguishable, we con-struct a controlled task inspired by work on com-positional generalization and made-up-word tests (Hupkes et al., 2020; Berko, 1958). Each prompt defines endings for two binary attributes, demon-strates three of the four possible combinations on made-up words, and asks for the missing combina-tion on a new word (Figure 1). Combining the two prompt-defined mappings yields the rule-consistent (correct) answer, whereas reusing the conflicting ending from the demonstrated combination that shares the first requested attribute yields a distinct copied answer. Because the assignments change in every prompt, the task cannot be solved using a fixed word–ending mapping. We measure the copied answer’s probability normalized over these two candidates, denoting it by P (copy) . 2 Related work 

In-context learning and composition. Accounts of in-context learning include copying through induction-like mechanisms (Olsson et al., 2022; Bietti et al., 2023; Reddy, 2024; Akyürek et al., 2024), inference over a latent task, and an update during the forward pass (Xie et al., 2022; von Os-wald et al., 2023). ICL behavior varies with train-ing distribution and stage (Chan et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2023). Controlled tasks show strategies be-tween repetition and exact rule use (Niu et al., 2025; Bhattamishra et al., 2024), and models can recog-nize a task without learning it (Yang et al., 2025b; Kim, 2025). Our task connects this issue to com-positional generalization and made-up-word tests (Hupkes et al., 2020; Lake and Baroni, 2018; Kim and Linzen, 2020; Lake and Baroni, 2023; Berko, 1958; Weissweiler et al., 2023), and to work on prompt-defined labels (Wei et al., 2023a,b). Mor-phology offers a long-studied case of the same con-trast between applying a rule and analogizing to stored forms (Pinker and Prince, 1988; Albright and Hayes, 2003), and recent work asks which of the two language models do (Hofmann et al., 2025; Anh et al., 2024; Ismayilzada et al., 2025). Tok-enization also shapes what counts as a morpheme for a model (Sennrich et al., 2016; Rust et al., 2021), which our Quechua results bear out. We make copy-ing a similar example and applying the supplied mappings predict different answers, then compare their scores and intervene on the supporting prompt tokens. Attention weights provide incomplete ex-planations of model behavior (Jain and Wallace, 2019; Serrano and Smith, 2019). Our interventions directly test which prompt tokens change the can-didate scores. 

Mechanisms of context use. Mechanistic stud-ies locate parts of in-context behavior in attention heads. Induction and retrieval heads copy from the context (Olsson et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024), a copy-suppression head counteracts them (Mc-Dougall et al., 2023), token-level and concept-level copying travel separate routes (Feucht et al., 2025), and a set of demonstrations can be summarized into a task or function vector (Hendel et al., 2023; Todd et al., 2024). Our interventions follow this tradi-tion of patching and ablation (Geiger et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022; Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023), but we ask a different question: which prompt span controls the answer at each stage of training. 

Development during training. Checkpoint stud-ies usually measure abilities by accuracy gains (Chen et al., 2024; Evanson et al., 2023; Tigges et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2024). Work on combined abilities finds that they emerge after their compo-nents (Liu et al., 2026), while other studies track reasoning, arithmetic, grammar, and internal com-ponents across training (Ye et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026; Chang and Bergen, 2022; Pan et al., 2026). We instead track copied-versus-correct scores and prompt-token dependence at every checkpoint. We extend work that localizes or modifies mechanisms for context use (Yin et al., 2024; Prakash et al., 2024) to the training trajectory. 

3 Task and measurements 

Task construction. Figure 1a shows one prompt. Each prompt assigns a fresh ending to each of four feature values, shows three of the four value combi-nations, and asks for the missing combination on a new stem. In the example, applying the definitions gives wug-or-nak , whereas copying the closest ex-ample, big one zud = zud-or-um , gives wug-or-um .The words, endings, and mappings are sampled anew for every prompt. Models can read the query without construct-ing the missing answer. 5 Prompt control changes before composition 

The checkpoints confirm what the cross-model con-trast predicts: control of the answer changes before the model can construct the missing form. 

Control switches first. Early checkpoints are governed by the closest ending. Blocking that end-ing changes the copied-versus-correct margin far more than blocking the requested value. The bal-ance reverses between 336B and 630B tokens (Fig-ure 6; also Figure 1b). At the end of this interval the two candidates receive similar probability and four-choice accuracy has yet to reach chance: prompt control has shifted before the correct answer be-comes reliable. 

Accuracy follows. Accuracy improves later. The fitted four-choice trajectory crosses chance at about 1.5T tokens on the original items and 1.8T on the re-test items (Table 1; Appendix J), well after the two blocking effects cross. The parts of the task are already in place much earlier: the model can copy the requested stem, recover each feature mapping, and generate combinations shown in the prompt hundreds of billions of tokens before it ranks the missing combination first. The long middle stage therefore has every part of the task in place, with composition of those parts the last piece to arrive. Exact generation of the missing form arrives last, at about 3.6T tokens (Table 1): parts, then control, then selection, then generation. 

Other training series. The ordering recurs in runs from other labs and recipes. Of six-teen released series, Marin-8B, Comma-v0.1-1T, SmolLM3-3B, and OLMo-3-7B end above the ac-curacy criterion, Apertus is partial, and eleven stay below it. OLMo-3 and Comma satisfy every or-dering criterion, shifting hundreds of billions of tokens before their first above-chance checkpoints. SmolLM3 also shifts before accuracy rises, with the closest-example effect later regaining strength. Within the OLMo family, OLMo-2-13B shifts early but briefly reverses, and the older OLMo-7B-0424 run changes sign repeatedly and turns negative for good only after its fitted accuracy onset. Runs that fail to compose never sustain control by the re-quested value and keep the copied answer. In every run that reaches composition, D first turns nega-tive by the time accuracy crosses chance, and no below-chance run sustains the switch (Appendix E; Table 6). 6 Training teaches what it practices 

The checkpoint results show control of the answer passing from the closest example to the requested value. We next ask which training signal produces this change. Synthetic documents that define end-ings and then query them reverse which prompt source controls the answer and let models produce the missing form. Exposure to definitions and a penalty on the copy-favoring heads do neither, and definition exercises built from rewritten real docu-ments instead teach models to follow the labels a prompt assigns. 

6.1 Practice, not exposure or suppression, moves control Exposure to definitions. Definitions embedded in natural text are not the active ingredient. Af-ter 1B additional tokens on SmolLM2-1.7B (Ap-pendix M), continued pretraining on mined pas-sages that define and later use a term, on absent-term variants, or on dictionary-prefixed text leaves 

D positive and exact generation at the plain-continuation level. All four conditions remain con-trolled by the closest ending (Figure 16). 

Penalizing copying. Penalizing the copy-favoring heads changes the balance between the two candidates without creating the missing answer. At SmolLM2-360M, adding to plain continuation a penalty on attention from the answer position to the closest ending, applied to ten heads selected by direct logit attribution for favoring the copied an-swer, drives those heads’ attention to that ending to near zero. 6.2 Synthetic documents reverse copying and transfer what they practice Synthetic documents. The synthetic documents re-verse both the preferred answer and which prompt source controls it. These documents have the di-agnostic’s own dictionary-and-query form: each defines endings for a small feature system, shows some combinations, leaves others unshown, and writes out their answers, with nonce stems, mark-ers, and templates drawn from pools disjoint from the evaluation inventories and no code systems (Appendix L). The suffix diagnostic shares the training format, so the code and Quechua ver-sions serve as transfer probes. Replacing 3 % of a continued-pretraining stream with these docu-ments moves SmolLM2-1.7B from copy-dominant to query-dominant and lifts exact generation from near zero to near ceiling (Figure 7; Figure 16). By 50M tokens P (copy) has fallen from .82 to near zero and exact generation has risen to .56 ; by the end of training exact generation is near ceiling, whereas plain continuation stays copy-dominant. The reversal costs nothing measurable on gen-eral text: held-out losses move by less than .001 at both scales while D moves by more than twenty nats.

Definition exercises teach a different use of the prompt. A language model rewrote real passages into short exercises that de-fine a label, term, or rule and then pose a query answerable only from that definition (verified ex-amples in Table 8). Unlike passive exposure to defi-nitions in natural text, every exercise requires an an-swer determined by a local definition. Fine-tuning SmolLM2-1.7B on 57M tokens of exercises is com-pared with the same schedule on the original docu-ments, and both are evaluated on in-context classifi-cation whose class names are replaced by made-up symbols (made-up labels) or exchanged with one another (exchanged labels) (Wei et al., 2023a), so that accuracy requires following the prompt’s as-signment. Made-up-label accuracy rises by 17 .6

points on TREC, 17 .2 on DBpedia, and 3.7 on Yahoo (Figure 1c), and exchanged-label accuracy rises on all three. On SmolLM3-3B the gains hold on TREC and Yahoo, and no counted evaluation loses. The exercises contain neither these datasets nor their labels. The transfer is specific to the prac-ticed use of the prompt: a relational-reasoning suite that also draws definitions from the prompt (bAbI-1, bAbI-15, or CLUTRR) moves down by a few points and farther tasks move in both directions, so the exercises teach following prompt-assigned la-bels rather than prompt-dependent tasks in general. (Appendix N; Figure 17). The morphology diagnostic is untouched by the exercises: P (copy) is unchanged and four-choice accuracy and exact generation stay near floor (Ap-pendix N). The exercises make label following reusable across datasets and leave the morphology task in place. 

The interventions separate suppression, con-struction, and transfer. Suppression moves the margin without producing the missing form. Train-ing on the synthetic documents turns D negative and lifts exact generation across model families, update methods, and encodings, and its transfer follows the practiced form. Definition exercises improve prompt-defined label use elsewhere while leaving the diagnostic unchanged. What mattered was which use of the prompt the training text re-peatedly rewarded.

What the all-shown control shows. In the all-shown control, documents in which every queried combination is already shown on another stem cross at the same checkpoint and reach comparable exact generation at 360M. The tie places the cause in what both conditions share, repeated resolution of queries through temporary mappings, and rules out rehearsal of the absent cell (Appendix L). Developmental checkpoints should be read ac-cordingly: a wrong answer can mark a substan-tive transition if its score now depends on the query rather than the closest example, and a cor-rect multiple-choice answer need not establish con-struction if the form cannot be generated without candidates. 

8 Conclusion 

On a diagnostic that separates copying from compo-sition, in-context learning develops as control shifts among competing prompt cues. The requested value takes control of candidate scores before the correct answer wins. Suppressing copying moves the margin, practice at resolving queries writes the missing form, and training transfers the use of the prompt it repeatedly requires. Tracking control, preference, construction, and transfer shows what pretraining teaches beyond what accuracy reveals. Across models, checkpoint series, and training ex-periments, these four aspects develop on different schedules, so in-context learning is acquired as sev-eral capabilities, each on its own timetable. Limitations 

Our diagnostic is a single controlled task that sepa-rates copying from composition, and the code and Quechua versions carry the contrast into two fur-ther encodings. In-context learning covers many other behaviors, and the four measures used here give a way to ask whether they develop in the same order. The training experiments use models up to 8B parameters and synthetic documents that share the diagnostic’s format, so the transfer they pro-duce is the format-bound transfer the design pre-dicts. Larger models and more varied training data are the next tests of this account. The selected attention heads carry part of the behavior, so the localization rests on the prompt-span interventions. The pre-registered training block, transfer evalua-tions, and analysis-plan record are in Appendices L, N, and P. 

Ethical Considerations 

The Southern Quechua paradigms used in the mor-phology version of the task come from published linguistic resources (Adelaar and Muysken, 2004; Kirov et al., 2018; Batsuren et al., 2022). The mined documents come from public collec-tions of web, scientific, mathematical, encyclope-dia, forum, and legal text. We applied no addi-tional screening for personal or offensive content beyond the source collections’ own filtering, so these sources may contain such material. A Task construction and conventions 

Generator. Every prompt draws fresh stems, words, endings and held-out combination, so no fixed stem–ending mapping solves the task. Asuffix-scheme stem concatenates one of 36 ini-tial strings, one of 9 vowels and one of 22 final strings. No stem repeats within a prompt, and the queried stem never appears in the demonstrations. Endings are drawn without replacement from 30 strings ( il, nak, or, um, esh, oth, ka, tu, ri, mo, lin, dor, sa, ne, po, ta, ku, zi, fen, gor, lu, wa, ex, an, ir, on, ul, ek, ip, ab ), and line order is shuffled. Each prompt draws one first-feature pair from small /big , old /new , red /blue and hot /cold , and one second-feature pair from one /many , here /there 

and mine /yours . The code scheme keeps the first-feature pairs, takes the second from circle /square ,

north /south or left /right , and uses 20 two-character symbols with the header “Codebook:” and the sep-arator “=>”. Four fixed prompt sets serve all models and checkpoints, two (320 items) for the initial analyses and two for the re-test on new items (Appendix J).

> In this language: one glon = glon-lu many pliv = pliv-dor one fooz = fooz-lu big many swoov = swoov-an-dor many bouz = bouz-dor small one kraix = kraix-ek-lu small one plut = plut-ek-lu big stoosh = stoosh-an big one lask = lask-an-lu big one twix = twix-an-lu big crood = crood-an small berb = berb-ek small plaind = plaind-ek big many doot = doot-an-dor small many glev =

The four scored continuations, each pre-ceded by a space, are glev-ek-dor (correct), 

glev-ek-lu (the copy, sharing the first ending 

-ek ), glev-an-dor and glev-an-lu . These are the candidates for four-choice accuracy and the “ranks first” columns. 

Vocabulary. “Correct” is the rule-consistent can-didate of Equation 2 in Section 3, and “copied” is the candidate that repeats the closest example’s sec-ond ending. A generation is labeled by the first word of its first line, split on hyphens. One ending counts as truncated and a form outside the four com-binations as malformed. Anything else is correct, copied, or another shown combination. “Closest ending” and “requested value” name the blocked spans. 

Intervention conventions. Layers are decoder blocks counted from zero. Activation patching replaces a block’s output at the position of the requested second-feature value while the prompt is processed, before generation. The model then takes the most probable token at each step for up to eight new tokens. A blocked span covers every tokenizer token that overlaps the named string or strings, and blocking the closest ending covers the second-ending string in both lines of the closest example. 

Tokenization. All 30 endings are separate tokens after a hyphen in Pythia and Gemma (Biderman et al., 2023; Gemma Team et al., 2025), most are in OLMo-2, and Qwen merges some hyphens with their endings (Yang et al., 2025a; Team OLMo et al., 2024). An ending can span several tokens, so the four forms can differ in token count. We score the next ending with the correct preceding tokens supplied only where hyphen and ending stay separate (scoring checks in Appendix D).

F Code and Quechua versions 

Two further versions test whether the model order-ing of Section 4 depends on the invented suffixes or on tokenization (Figure 12). One uses code words and symbols in place of endings. The other uses Southern Quechua noun paradigms, scored with each suffix as its own token and in standard spelling. 

Code scheme. The six tested models keep the suffix-scheme ordering and score better. P (copy) 

is .55 , .38 , .17 , .06 , .01 and .00 for Pythia-1.4B, Pythia-2.8B, OLMo-2-7B, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-8B (Figure 12a). Blocking here covers the closest example’s whole line, so its size is not comparable with suffix-ending block-ing. In OLMo-2-7B the requested-value effect first exceeds the closest-example effect at 168B, inside the interval of the fitted four-choice crossing, 143B [115, 178]. The two events coincide at this reso-lution, whereas in the suffix scheme the blocking turn came first (Figure 12b).

que noun lemmas we keep the 242 vowel-final, lower-case ASCII lemmas whose cells are exact concatenations of lemma, possessor and case (consonant-final lemmas would need epenthesis), minus function words, loans and proper names. Possessor endings are -y, -yki, -n, -nchik, -nku ,and case endings -man, -manta, -pi, -wan, -kama, -paq, -rayku . Excluding prefix-related pairs ( y/yki ,

n/nchik , n/nku , man /manta ) leaves 7 possessor pairs and 20 case pairs. Each prompt draws one pair from each pool, and the closest example shares the possessor. Segmented forms look like 

tanta-y-pi , standard spelling like tantaypi .

G Activation patching 

Under the patch of Section 3, never-shown shares in the low-copy group rise as P (copy) falls: .26 for OLMo-2-7B, .33 for OLMo-2-13B, .39 for Qwen3-1.7B, .65 for Qwen2.5-7B and .91 for Qwen3-8B. .08 (Figure 13). Counting only the requested end-ing, regardless of stem or other ending, Pythia’s never-shown shares rise to .11 , .31 and .19 while the low-copy models move by at most .03 (supple-ment table). High-copy models can produce the requested ending without building the full form. 

Why this position and layer? Distributed align-ment search (Wu et al., 2023) on the final Pythia-1.4B fits an 8-dimensional rotated subspace at the requested-value word, the last stem token and “=”. Only the requested-value word responds, most strongly after layer 4, where the greedy ending changes on most items (an approximation) for the first feature and about half for the second. Transfer is .97 on the three fitted word pairs but .04 on the held-out pair. The layer-4 success rate rises from 

.28 at step 2k (about 4B tokens) to .95 at step 143k (300B). Layer 4, the Pythia-1.4B optimum, is used as the shared early layer for every model, although per-model sweeps peak at layers 0, 1, 9, 11 and 14 (Appendix N). Both conditions in Figure 13 start from a shown base form, and the never-shown base-form con-dition was not run. Position-by-position patch-ing retains most of the change in m through the first .33 of layers in Pythia-1.4B and .53 –.64 in low-copy models. This depth measurement re-places a planned between-group comparison at the requested-value word that could not be evaluated as specified (Appendix P).

I Fine-tuning a copying model 

We ask whether direct fine-tuning of Pythia-1.4B reproduces the pretraining order, D changing sign before accuracy rises, and whether the all-shown result of Section 6 also holds under fine-tuning. 

Design. We fully fine-tune released Pythia-1.4B for 2,000 steps (learning rate 10 −5, warm-up, co-sine decay, batch size 32). Training uses its own prompt template, evaluation the standard one. Two seeds target the correct held-out form. A third run queries only shown combinations, mirroring the all-shown condition of Section 6. We predicted that the bootstrap interval for D would fall below zero at least 250 steps before the Wilson lower bound for four-choice accuracy exceeded .25 , in both tar-get runs, and that neither event would occur in the all-shown run. 

Outcome. Both target runs cross both thresholds at the first evaluation, step 250 (supplement fig-ure). On a 10-step grid, accuracy clears chance at step 10 or 20 and passes .9 by step 50, while D

turns negative at step 20 in both runs. The closest-ending effect falls from 5.3 to about 3 nats and then fluctuates between +2 .3 and +7 .6, while the requested-value effect grows from 2 to more than 

20 . The all-shown run also learns the held-out form. At step 250 it has P (copy) = .83 , a closest-ending effect near 31 nats and D = +21 . By step 500, D

is negative and correct-ranks-first is .50 , rising to 

.70 –.78 from step 1,000 with closest-ending and requested-value effects near 10 and 30 nats. Nei-ther part of the predicted pretraining order is repro-duced. The requested-value effect grows while the closest-ending effect is still present, instead of after it disappears. 

J Re-test on new items 

We repeat the tests of Sections 4 and 5 on 320 new prompts per model, generated after those analy-ses were fixed (Table 7). The re-test covers new prompts from the same generator, not indepen-dently trained models. 

Criteria. P (copy) must be at least .70 in the high-copy group and at most .30 in the low-copy group, D must take the predicted sign in each group, and the unrelated-line 95% interval must lie within ±.3 nats. OLMo-2-7B’s sign change must fall in 336B–630B and its fitted four-choice accuracy must cross chance afterwards. OLMo-2-1B and Qwen3-0.6B have no directional prediction, leaving nine in the sign test. 

Results. All probability thresholds and all nine signs hold (Table 7). The 336B–630B interval recurs in .999 of joint item resamples, and the 1.79T accuracy crossing follows the sign change in every resample. The unrelated-line interval fails for Pythia-1.4B and Pythia-12B in bfloat16, with means of only +.24 and +.17 , and passes in the float32 re-scores (Appendix D). The six tra-jectory outcomes of Appendix E are unchanged. OLMo-2-13B still shows three sign changes, and the low-accuracy OLMo-2-1B, OLMoE and stan-dard Pythia runs never turn negative.

L Continued pretraining with synthetic documents and attention penalties (360M) 

Five conditions continue pretraining SmolLM2-360M for about 1B tokens, two runs each: original documents, two synthetic-document variants and two attention penalties (Section 6.1; Figure 15). 

Design. All conditions share the starting check-point, compute budget and data order. • Base: SmolLM2-360M at step-1600000 (2.52 trillion tokens), continued on the public proxy corpus smollm-corpus (Ben Allal et al., 2025) for 636 steps, about 1 billion tokens. • Synthetic documents: every 33rd packed se-quence (3 %, about 30 million tokens) is re-placed by documents that define endings. One-unshown documents leave one combination unshown and give its answer. All-shown doc-uments show every requested combination on another stem. Training stems, endings and templates are disjoint from the evaluation in-ventories, and neither condition contains code systems. • Attention penalties: original documents plus a penalty on attention from the answer posi-tion to the closest ending, on either the ten heads with the largest direct contribution to the copied answer in the base model or ten ran-dom heads in the same layers (Appendix N). • Measures: P (copy) , correct-ranks-first, exact generation and D, code and Quechua scores, three external evaluations defined in the sup-plement, FineWeb-Edu and Pile validation losses, and the penalized attention. • Crossings: a condition’s D turns negative at the first checkpoint with mean D < 0 and 

P (copy) ≤ .55 , and it reaches the accuracy criterion at the first with correct-ranks-first at least .50 and exact generation at least .15 .

Comparisons. An intervention counts as earlier only if it crosses before its control, or crosses when the control never does. Transfer to code and Quechua requires a gain of at least .05 on both measures of both tasks in both runs, each with a positive 95% paired item-bootstrap interval, and both validation losses within +.02 nats of original documents. 

Outcome. Both synthetic conditions cross both thresholds at step 32, about 50M tokens, so they tie and neither counts as earlier (Figure 15). At the end the all-shown runs have P (copy) of .27 /. 19 

and exact generation of .65 /. 77 . The one-unshown runs have .27 /. 13 and .71 /. 84 . The one-unshown condition meets the transfer and loss criteria against original documents but not against all-shown doc-uments. The selected-head penalty drives the pe-nalized attention from .310 /. 341 to .008 /. 006 , yet 

P (copy) stays at .56 /. 57 , D at +1 .01 / + 1 .60 ,and exact generation at zero. Original documents and both penalty conditions reach neither threshold within 1B tokens, and the selected-head penalty does not reach the transfer threshold. Practicing an unshown combination is therefore not isolated as the cause, and reducing the selected heads’ atten-tion to the closest ending does not create compos-ing.
