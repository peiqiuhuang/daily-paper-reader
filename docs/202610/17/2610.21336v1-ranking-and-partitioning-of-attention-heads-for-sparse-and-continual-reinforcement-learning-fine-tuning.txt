ABSTRACT 

We study sparse and continual reinforcement learning fine-tuning of language models through attention-head ranking and partitioning. For sparse learning, we rank attention heads by gradient norm to identify a small subset of heads that can be trained effectively. For continual learning, we partition heads across tasks to reduce cross-task interference and preserve previously learned capabilities. Theo-retically, we relate gradient norm to training effectiveness, and further characterize why sparse, separated head updates can remain stable. Empirically, across math, instruction-following, and coding tasks, tuning higher-ranked heads consistently outperforms tuning lower-ranked ones while often matching denser baselines, and head partitioning improves retention while preserving adaptation to new tasks. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement-learning (RL) fine-tuning has become an important approach for improving the ca-pabilities of language models (LMs). However, RL fine-tuning is expensive, especially when a large fraction of model parameters is updated, which increases gradient memory and synchronization costs in distributed training (Miahi & Belilovsky, 2026; Fireworks.ai, 2026). Recent works suggest that effective adaptation can often be achieved by updating only restricted parts of the LM, such as attention modules or individual transformer layers (Jin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). This motivates sparse RL fine-tuning under a limited trainable-parameter budget. In this work, we focus on attention heads as the basic units for sparse RL fine-tuning. Classical pruning studies show that many attention heads can be removed with little performance degradation, while a smaller subset plays more important roles (Michel et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019). More recent studies further suggest that attention heads can specialize toward different latent signals dur-ing training (Sagitova et al., 2026), and that high-level LM capabilities can be localized to sparse, task-specific subsets of heads (Bair et al., 2026). This motivates us to design a criterion to identify those heads that are most effective to update during training. Sparse RL fine-tuning is also appealing for continual learning, where sequential updates can inter-fere with previously learned capabilities and lead to catastrophic forgetting. A common approach is to constrain different tasks to parameter or gradient subspaces (Chaudhry et al., 2020; Doan et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023; Liang & Li, 2024; Qiu et al., 2026). Sparse updates can therefore serve to separate task-specific adaptations. Such parameter isolation avoids direct overwriting, yet indi-rect functional interference may remain (Lanzillotta et al., 2023), requiring careful selection of the trainable parameters such as Iurada et al. (2025); Lin et al. (2025; 2026). 

Contributions. We study attention-head ranking and partitioning for sparse and continual RL fine-tuning. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose gradient norm as a simple criterion for ranking attention heads. Across several tasks, tuning higher-ranked heads consistently outperforms tuning lower-ranked heads, while of-ten matching or exceeding denser baselines. • We propose partitioning attention heads across sequential tasks and study several head-allocation strategies. Our experiments show that head partitioning with these strategies effectively prevents forgetting while preserving adaptation to new tasks. 1arXiv:2610.21336v1 [cs.LG] 15 Oct 2026 • We provide a theoretical analysis to guide both designs. We characterize the role of gradient norm in the optimization dynamics of sparse and continual learning, and further analyze the second-order structure of multi-head self-attention, revealing favorable properties of attention-head blocks for parameter and functional isolation. 

2 RELATED WORKS 

Sparse optimization in deep learning and LMs. Sparse optimization has been studied through subset selection (Dan et al., 2019), sparse reward-model structure (Yao et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), and sparse task-specific parameter updates (Guo et al., 2021). For LMs, recent works show that updating only attention modules (Jin et al., 2025) or a single layer (Zhang et al., 2026) can obtain competitive performance, while sparse updates can improve communication efficiency in distributed RL (Miahi & Belilovsky, 2026; Fireworks.ai, 2026). 

Subspace continual learning. A common approach to continual learning is to reduce interference by constraining new-task updates to subspaces that avoid directions important for previous tasks. Early methods construct such subspaces from past gradients or representations (Farajtabar et al., 2020; Saha et al., 2021; Chaudhry et al., 2020; Doan et al., 2020). Recent works extend this idea to LoRA adaptation (Wang et al., 2023; Liang & Li, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2026), full fine-tuning (Nayak et al., 2026), and model merging (Qiao & Mahdavi, 2026; Tang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). Iurada et al. (2025) rank parameters by sensitivity measured using Fisher matrix, and fine-tune only the least-sensitive ones. Lin et al. (2025) update only parameters that are activated more strongly by new data than by pretraining data, reducing interference with prior knowledge. Lin et al. (2026) dynamically update parameter-level masks based on accumulated gradient magnitudes, whereas we use static head-level gradient norms. 

Theoretical understanding of continual learning. Neural tangent kernel (NTK)-based analyses relate catastrophic forgetting to overlap between task-induced tangent subspaces (Doan et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2026), while kernel-based views have also been developed for LM fine-tuning (Malladi et al., 2023). Parameter isolation has been studied through Hessian-based arguments (Lanzillotta et al., 2023). In RL, Luo et al. (2026) characterize loss of plasticity through degradation of spectral plasticity, while Shenfeld et al. (2026) show that online RL tends to forget less by staying closer to the base policy, and Chen et al. (2026) attribute this advantage to the use of on-policy data. We connect the stability–plasticity trade-off in continual learning directly to gradient norms. 

Mechanism of attention heads. Prior work has shown that attention heads are highly hetero-geneous, with many heads being redundant while a smaller subset exhibits specialized behav-ior (Michel et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019). More directly related to this work, Bair et al. (2026) show that small subsets of attention heads can be critical to performance on particular LM capabili-ties. Sagitova et al. (2026) theoretically characterize how attention heads specialize toward distinct latent signals during training. Ormaniec et al. (2025) further analyzes the second-order structure of self-attention. Together, these results motivate us to focus on attention heads as structured units.

Experimental setup. We evaluate head-sparse RL fine-tuning on Math, Instruction-following (IF), and Coding using QWEN 3-1.7B , QWEN 3-4B , and MISTRAL -7B-I NSTRUCT -V0.3 . For each task, we rank attention heads by gradient norms at the initial checkpoint and compare training the top 50% and bottom 50% heads, together with full-attention tuning and full tuning baselines. We also include random 50% attention-head tuning, where 50% head blocks are randomly selected per layer, as a baseline. All training jobs share the same training configuration, except that, given the substantially larger number of trainable parameters, we use smaller learning rates for full tuning of the QWEN 3 series and for experiments on MISTRAL -7B-I NSTRUCT -V0.3 . Implementation details are provided in Appendix C. 

Main results. Tables 1 and 2 show a consistent advantage of gradient-based head selection. Top-50% tuning outperforms bottom-50% tuning across all model–task pairs and evaluation splits, and also generally outperforms the random-50% baseline. While random selection occasionally per-forms better on some tasks for QWEN 3-1.7B , the advantage of top-ranked heads is more robust across various settings. Despite updating only half of the attention heads, top-50% tuning often matches or exceeds full-attention and full tuning, with the same trend extending to MISTRAL -7B-INSTRUCT -V0.3 . As shown in Table 10 in Appendix B.2, head-sparse tuning also reduces the gra-dients to be stored and synchronized at each step, offering a significant communication-efficiency advantage in distributed training (Fireworks.ai, 2026; Miahi & Belilovsky, 2026). These results support gradient norm as an effective criterion for identifying heads useful for sparse RL tuning, consistent with the theoretical claims in Section 4.1. See supplemental results in Appendix B.1. 4Comments on learning rates. Readers may wonder whether the weaker bottom 50% results simply arise because their smaller gradient scales require larger learning rates. We note that, under the fixed training configuration and the same trainable-parameter budget, this already reflects a nontrivial training-efficiency gap; moreover, if the difference were only a uniform gradient rescaling, Adam’s normalization would cancel this effect. We further sweep the learning rate for bottom 50% attention-head tuning in Appendix B.4. It reveals that larger learning rates help in some cases, but the gap to top 50% attention-head tuning generally remains. 

Head-ranking consistency. Table 11 in Appendix B.2 reports Spearman distances between inde-pendently computed head rankings. Within-task distances are consistently small, indicating stable rankings across subsets of the same task. Cross-task distances are larger, reflecting task-specific dif-ferences in head importance, but remain small overall, suggesting a shared intrinsic hierarchy across tasks. In practice, this consistency also suggests that, when the training set is large, head rankings can be estimated from a small subset of the data to reduce the computation cost. 

Layer-ranking consistency. We further compare gradient-norm ranking with the candidate-layer rankings obtained by direct performance evaluation. As shown in Table 3, the two rankings are highly consistent across the QWEN 3-B ASE series, and exactly match for QWEN 3-4B-B ASE . This provides additional evidence that gradient norm can serve as a useful proxy for identifying trainable attention components without exhaustively evaluating each candidate. The layer distributions of top heads are visualized in Appendix B.3.

All configura-tions use the same training setup, differing only in the number of trainable attention heads. Experimental setup. We evaluate whether partitioning attention heads can reduce interference in continual RL. We sequentially fine-tune QWEN 3-1.7B and QWEN 3-4B on two- and three-task sequences over Math, IF, and Coding. We compare head-partitioned tuning with continual full-attention and full-tuning baselines. Implementation details are provided in Appendix C. Motivated by Section 5.1, we consider three head-allocation strategies: • Top–Bottom : the previous task uses top-ranked heads, while the next task uses the remaining. • Round-Robin : tasks alternately select their highest-ranked unassigned heads. • Shared : in addition to disjoint task-specific heads, a remaining subset is shared across tasks. For two-task experiments, we compare the first two strategies. For three-task experiments, we use the third, assigning 25% of the heads to each task and sharing the remaining 25%. It is worth emphasizing that we do not claim these strategies to be optimal and exhaustive. Instead, they serve to validate our theory and demonstrate possible ways to apply it. 

Main results. Figures 2 to 4 show that head partitioning generally reduces forgetting while pre-serving adaptation to the new task, especially when full-attention tuning causes large drops on earlier tasks. Top–Bottom typically retains the first task better but adapts slightly worse to the second, while Round-Robin shows the opposite trade-off, consistent with their allocation strategies. Figures 5 and 6 further show that the head-partition approach extends to three sequential tasks with minimal degradation on previous tasks, supporting the theoretical claims in Section 5.1. 7 CONCLUSION 

We study sparse and continual RL fine-tuning through gradient-based ranking and partitioning of attention heads. We show theoretically that gradient norm characterizes the training benefit of a head subset. Empirically, high-ranked heads support efficient sparse RL tuning, while partitioning heads across tasks effectively reduces forgetting in continual RL. It is also important to acknowledge our limitations. (i) Our experiments are restricted to relatively small language models, limited tasks, and settings without long reasoning traces. (ii) Our analysis focuses on attention parameters and may not directly extend to MLP layers, which would be impor-tant for sparse full-model fine-tuning. (iii) The theoretical second-order analysis is restricted to a single multi-head self-attention layer. 9AI USE STATEMENT

Codebase. Our codebase is mainly based on miles (Miles Team, 2026) ( https://github. com/radixark/miles ). It is worth noting that our current sparse head tuning implementation is not yet fully optimized, though realizing its full efficiency benefits should be straightforward with modern training infrastructure. Our code is released at https://github.com/srzer/ head-sparse-rl . The code was implemented with the assistance of Codex and Claude Code. 

Datasets and benchmarks. For math, we use a 1k subset of DAPO-Math-17k (https: //huggingface.co/datasets/BytedTsinghua-SIA/DAPO-Math-17k ) for training, a disjoint 1k subset for in-distribution test, and MATH-500 (https://huggingface. co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/MATH-500 ) for out-of-distribution test. For instruction-following, we similarly use a 1k subset of RLVR-IFEval (https://huggingface.co/ datasets/allenai/RLVR-IFeval ) for training, a disjoint 1k subset for in-distribution test, and IFBench (https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/IFBench_test )for out-of-distribution test. For basic coding, we use MBPP (https://huggingface. co/datasets/google-research-datasets/mbpp ) for training, MBPP+ (https: //huggingface.co/datasets/evalplus/mbppplus ) for in-distribution test, and 

APPS-intro-test (https://huggingface.co/datasets/codeparrot/apps ) for out-of-distribution test. Notably, MBPP+ uses the same problem set as MBPP but with strengthened test cases, thus measuring performance under more rigorous evaluation. 

Models. Limited by computation resources, our base models are QWEN 3-1.7B (Qwen Team, 2025) (https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B ), QWEN 3-4B (Qwen Team, 2025) (https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B ), and MISTRAL -7B-I NSTRUCT -

> V

0.3 (https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 ). 

Training hyper-parameters. Each rollout contains 16 prompts and 4 responses per prompt, with a global batch size of 64. We use temperature 1.0 and a maximum generation length of 3,072 tokens (2,048 for coding). Adam is used with learning rate 3 ˆ 10 ´6 (1 ˆ 10 ´6 for full training, 2 ˆ 10 ´6

for MISTRAL -7B ), weight decay 0.1, and pβ1, β 2q “ p 0.9, 0.98 q. The policy-ratio clipping range is 0.2–0.28, with zero KL and entropy coefficients. Training runs for 400–600 rollout steps. 

Evaluation hyper-parameters. We use greedy-decoding for evaluation. For each evaluation ex-ample, we generate a single response with a maximum length of 4,096 tokens, temperature 1.0, and top-k equal to 1. We report pass@1 accuracy using the task-specific verifier for each task. 

Computation resources. Experiments are run on NVIDIA A100 and RTX A6000 GPUs, using four GPUs per job (eight for MISTRAL -7B ), with a typical runtime of approximately one day. 26
