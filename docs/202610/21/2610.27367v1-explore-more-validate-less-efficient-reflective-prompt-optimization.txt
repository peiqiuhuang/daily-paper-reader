## Abstract 

Reflective prompt optimization improves instructions using task feedback, but coupling candidate generation with full-validation-based selection can make search costly and sequential. We propose a two-stage framework that decouples reflective candidate-pool construction from validation allocation. During construction, we sample multiple parents and reuse each parent’s training responses across parallel reflection branches. Each revision is screened against its parent on a small training minibatch, allowing locally improved candidates to support further revisions without full validation. Af-ter construction, an exploration policy selects candidates from the frozen pool under a fixed full-validation budget, and the prompt with the highest measured validation score is returned. This sepa-ration allows candidate-pool size and validation budget to be set independently. We evaluate random selection and learned score prediction with similarity-based exploration on five benchmarks using Qwen3.5-9B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. With 100 retained candidates and 20 full validations, our framework improves over the initial solver by up to 11.22 percentage points. Learned selection improves over the initial solver in all ten comparisons, although neither selection policy consistently outperforms the other. These results support separating reflective search from budgeted valida-tion while leaving room for stronger candidate-selection policies.

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit broad capabilities in lan-guage and reasoning [2, 5, 19], but their task performance depends on how these capabilities are elicited. In-context learning (ICL) specifies tasks through demonstrations without updating model weights [3]; these demonstrations convey input distributions, label spaces, and output formats [18]. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompt-ing improves multi-step reasoning through worked examples of intermediate steps [28], while zero-shot CoT can elicit such steps with a short instruction [11]. Prompt design is also consequen-tial within a task: example selection [14], example order [15], and prompt format [36] can substantially affect performance. These findings make prompts a practical optimization target. Manually inspecting outputs and revising instructions is costly across tasks, motivating automatic prompt optimization that improves prompts while keeping model parameters fixed [1, 20, 37]. Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE) generates and selects instruc-tions [37], while the Multi-prompt Instruction Proposal Optimizer (MIPRO) jointly optimizes instructions and demonstrations [20]. Repeatedly expanding only the best prompt can concentrate search on one strategy; Genetic-Pareto (GEPA) instead retains comple-mentary parents through instance-wise Pareto selection [1]. In its original single-proposal loop, a parent answers a sampled training minibatch, errors guide revision, and a child passing local screening receives full validation before the frontier update. Figure 1 high-lights two bottlenecks: successive reflections wait for preceding evaluations and decisions; all-correct minibatches incur parent-answering and scoring costs but skip reflection, producing no can-didate. Rejected children also consume work without expanding the pool. Parallel proposals relax serial generation [34], but accepted proposals still require full validation before frontier updates. These dependencies limit opportunities to explore alternatives and refine descendants under finite budgets. To address the above challenges, our approach separates reflec-tive candidate-pool construction from validation-based selection, exploring multiple lineages before fully validating a subset of candi-dates. We sample multiple parents and generate revisions in parallel, retaining children that improve upon their parents on small training minibatches. Once the pool is frozen, an exploration policy, such as random sampling or learned score prediction with similarity-based exploration, selects candidates for full validation under a fixed bud-get. The candidate with the highest measured validation score is returned. Removing full validation from the generation loop allows the pool to grow beyond the validation budget, while different selection policies can reuse the same pool without regenerating prompts. Our contributions are threefold: 

• We introduce a two-stage framework that decouples reflective candidate-pool construction from full validation, allowing pool size and validation budget to be set independently. This targets validation efficiency by reserving expensive full evaluations for a selected subset rather than every accepted revision. 

• We develop a multi-parent construction procedure that shares training feedback across parallel reflection branches and locally screens revisions without full-validation scores. Reusing parent responses reduces repeated evaluation, while concurrent expan-sion across parents and minibatches supports broader search over alternative revision paths.

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Automatic Prompt Optimization 

Automatic prompt optimization searches for effective prompts while keeping model weights fixed. AutoPrompt uses gradient-guided search to find discrete trigger tokens for masked language models [25]. For natural-language instructions, APE generates candidates from demonstrations [37]; Optimization by Prompting (OPRO) conditions proposals on past instructions and scores [31]; EvoPrompt evolves prompt populations [6]. MIPRO jointly opti-mizes instructions and demonstrations for multi-stage programs [20]. Beyond individual prompts, AFlow searches code-represented agentic workflows using Monte Carlo tree search and execution feedback [35]. Consensus-Evolve (C-Evolve) evolves prompt groups using voting-based fitness rather than individual scores [12]. meta-TextGrad optimizes the prompts and structure of language-model optimizers themselves [30]. Evaluation-efficient selection is also established. Prompt Opti-mization with Textual Gradients (ProTeGi) combines textual feed-back, beam search, and bandit-based selection [21]. InstructZero uses Bayesian optimization over low-dimensional soft prompts that an open model converts into instructions for a black-box model [4]. Instruction Optimization using Neural Bandits Coupled with Transformers (INSTINCT) uses a multilayer perceptron (MLP) sur-rogate coupled to transformer representations to guide soft-prompt search and instruction generation [13]. Multi-Agent System Prompt Optimization via Bandits (MASPOB) combines graph neural repre-sentations, uncertainty-aware selection, and coordinate ascent to optimize prompts within fixed multi-agent workflows [7]. TRIPLE formulates selection from a pre-generated prompt pool as fixed-budget best-arm identification [24]. Thus, separating gen-eration from budgeted selection is not itself new. Our focus is the construction of a reflective candidate tree without full-validation scores, using shared parent feedback and local child screening. The subsequent policy allocates complete validation-set evaluations over that frozen pool. Unlike soft-prompt search, it selects among already constructed natural-language prompts; the learned selec-tor is an exploration option rather than a new neural prediction paradigm. 

## 2.2 Feedback-driven Improvement 

Self-Refine critiques and revises individual outputs [17], while Re-flexion stores feedback-derived reflections for subsequent agent trials [26]. TextGrad propagates language-model feedback through a computation graph to optimize its components [33]. This ad-dresses how feedback reaches optimization variables, whereas our framework controls when candidate prompts receive full validation. GEPA combines trajectory reflection with instance-wise Pareto se-lection to retain complementary prompts [1]. Its parallel-proposal extension generates revisions from multiple parents, but still fully validates accepted proposals before updating the frontier [34]. Our distinction is therefore not reflection or parallel generation alone: training feedback and local screening drive pool construction with-out full-validation scores, and validation is allocated only after the

## 3.2 Overall Framework 

Our framework separates candidate-pool construction from vali-dation-based selection. In the first stage, multiple parents undergo reflective revision using training feedback, and locally improved children enter the pool. Parent–child relationships are recorded as generation provenance. At the stage boundary, prompt texts and generation metadata are frozen. The second stage applies an exploration policy to this pool, acquiring validation scores without generating additional candidates. Different exploration policies can therefore share the same construction stage. The final output is the prompt with the highest measured validation score. Random selection. In the baseline-inclusive configuration, the initial prompt is selected first, 𝑞 1 = 𝑝 0. The remaining 𝐵 − 1 candi-dates are sampled uniformly without replacement from P \ { 𝑝 0 },independently of their validation scores. 

Prediction-guided exploration. We also evaluate an MLP trained on prompt embeddings and observed full-validation scores. After random initialization, the policy repeatedly refits the predictor and ranks unvalidated candidates by a weighted combination of pre-dicted score and novelty. Novelty is one minus the maximum cosine similarity to any already validated prompt. Predictions and novelty are separately min–max normalized over the remaining candidates before combination. This policy uses only acquired validation labels and the frozen pool; unlike the baseline-inclusive random policy, it does not force the initial prompt to be selected. ## 4 Experimental Setup 4.1 Research Questions 

We organize the experiments around three research questions (RQs): 

RQ1. How do the two-stage framework and its exploration poli-cies compare with the initial solver, prompt optimization, and preference fine-tuning across two models? 

RQ2. What do the reported construction and full-validation times indicate about the relative costs of our workflow and GEPA? 

RQ3. How do the retained candidate trees compare with GEPA in mean depth and mean branching factor? 

## 4.2 Datasets 

We evaluate on five benchmarks. HotpotQA [32] tests multi-hop question answering, scored by normalized exact match using three retrieved passages per question. SuperGPQA [16] contains graduate-level questions with ten answer options, scored by accuracy. LiveBench-Math [29] covers mathematical reasoning and uses task-specific answer checkers. IFBench [22] measures instruction following using the mean fraction of satisfied constraints per example. BIG-Bench Extra Hard (BBEH) [9] evaluates challenging reasoning tasks with binary answer matching. Training, validation, and test subsets contain 150, 300, and 300 examples, respectively, except for LiveBench-Math, which uses 122 examples per subset. For IFBench, training and validation use multi-constraint instruction data, while testing uses the official test set. All subsets are fixed across methods; validation guides prompt selection, and test data are reserved for final evaluation.

## 4.3 Models 

We evaluate Qwen3.5-9B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [5]. Within each prompt-optimization experiment, the same model performs task solving, reflection, and prompt revision, with its parameters fixed; Qwen’s thinking mode is disabled. ## 4.4 Compared Methods 

We compare against four reference methods: the initial solver, MIPRO version 2 (MIPROv2), GEPA, and Direct Preference Op-timization (DPO) [23]. They cover an unoptimized prompt, prompt optimization, and preference-based parameter adaptation. Within each model block, methods start from the same base checkpoint and use the same test examples and scoring metrics. 

Initial solver. The initial prompt provides a reference without reflective search. We retain the initial prompt for HotpotQA, Su-perGPQA, IFBench, and BBEH. For LiveBench-Math, the reference uses the minimal task instruction from the existing evaluation setup with an empty evolvable component, rather than an optimized pool candidate. 

MIPROv2. MIPROv2 extends MIPRO [20] in the Declarative Self-improving Python (DSPy) framework [10] and jointly searches over instructions and few-shot demonstrations. 1 It provides a prompt-optimization comparison that keeps solver parameters fixed. 

GEPA. We compare against a local implementation based on GEPA [1], which interleaves reflective revision with validation-based Pareto selection. The task-score configuration uses the shared-parent proposal variant, with five proposal slots per selected parent, and a budget of 30 full-validation evaluations. This is an imple-mentation variant, not an unmodified single-proposal reproduction. The budget counts full-validation evaluations rather than reflection rounds. The separate runtime comparison reports GEPA-20, the time consumed by 20 full-validation rounds of GEPA, including evaluation of the initial prompt. 

DPO. We use DPO [23] to train a separate adapter for each model– dataset pair using preference pairs constructed only from training examples. The base model generates candidate answers, which are scored by the dataset’s native evaluator. The highest-scoring answer is chosen and the lowest-scoring answer is rejected; pairs are retained only when their scores differ strictly. Training uses Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [8], with the base parameters frozen and the adapter-disabled base model serving as the reference policy. The DPO loss uses sequence log probabilities over answer tokens only. This baseline adapts model parameters rather than searching for a task prompt.

## 4.5 Implementation Details 

All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 hardware with 80 GB of accelerator memory. We report candidate construction and full validation separately in minutes, with 20 full-validation evaluations per method in the runtime comparison. These are refer-ence times for relative comparison within our experimental setting, not hardware-independent absolute runtimes. The Qwen IFBench construction entry is estimated, and the entries have not all been recalculated using the same procedure. 

Prompt optimization. We set the target pool size to 𝑁 = 100 prompts, including the initial prompt, using the training-only pro-cedure in Section 3.3. Each generation batch samples up to 𝑃 = 5 par-ents. Each parent first answers 𝑀 = 15 training examples. From its scored responses, we sample 𝐾 = 5 minibatches of 𝑏 = 3 examples, with replacement across minibatches but not within a minibatch. In the updated single-reflector runs, LiveBench-Math uses strati-fied sampling targeting two failures and one success, with counts adjusted when a stratum is unavailable. The other benchmarks sam-ple three distinct examples uniformly and reject all-successful or repeated ordered minibatches. All branches use the same ordinary error-analysis instruction. After freezing the pool, the random policy validates the initial prompt and 19 additional candidates sampled uniformly without replacement. The MLP-based policy uses five randomly selected candidates for initialization, then allocates the remaining 15 vali-dations sequentially. It embeds prompt text with a frozen MPNet-based sentence encoder [27] and refits an MLP with one 16-unit hidden layer, tanh activation, an L2 regularization coefficient of 1, and a maximum of 500 quasi-Newton optimization iterations. Acquisition weights are 0 .4 for normalized predicted score and 0 .6for normalized novelty. Both policies select the prompt with the highest measured validation score, breaking ties by fixed pool or-der. For task-score comparisons, our method uses 20 full-validation evaluations, and GEPA is configured with a budget of 30. We conduct training on multiple NVIDIA A100 graphics process-ing units (GPUs). 

DPO training. For each training question, we sample four an-swers at temperature 0 .7 with a maximum output length of 4,096 tokens. LoRA uses rank 16, scaling parameter 𝛼 = 32, and dropout 0.05. We train for two epochs with learning rate 5 × 10 −6, four gradient-accumulation steps, and DPO preference-loss coefficient 

𝛽 = 0.1. We use the second-epoch checkpoint throughout. Test-ing uses the fixed test examples and native scorers, with scores averaged over three inference seeds.

## 5.3 Search-Tree Structure (RQ3) 

The third experiment characterizes the retained search trees using mean depth and mean branching factor. Depth counts revision steps from the initial prompt; its zero depth is included in the mean over all retained nodes. Mean branching factor is the number of retained parent–child edges divided by the number of non-leaf nodes, mea-suring the average number of direct children per expanded node. Table 3 reports both statistics. Our retained trees have greater mean branching in eight of ten comparisons, with four of five tasks for each model; the exceptions are Llama LiveBench-Math and Qwen BBEH. Mean depth is greater in nine of ten comparisons, with Llama IFBench the exception at 3.37 versus 5.73. Thus, expanded nodes generally retain more children, and revision paths are usually longer. However, the methods differ in retained-node counts and retention rules. These observations characterize search behavior; they do not by themselves establish better task performance, greater efficiency, or semantic diversity.

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented a two-stage framework that separates training-driven candidate construction from full-validation-based selection. Shared parent feedback supports parallel reflection, while later descen-dants can be selected without fully validating intermediate prompts. Across five benchmarks and two models, our variants lead on all five Qwen tasks and two Llama tasks, but neither exploration pol-icy dominates. Reference timing totals are lower in eight of ten comparisons against GEPA-20 under the reported estimation proce-dure. Retained trees show greater mean depth in nine comparisons and greater branching in eight, under differing node counts and retention rules. Better candidate-selection policies and matched-total-budget comparisons remain important directions. 

## Limitations 

Our learned exploration policy uses an MLP over individual prompt embeddings, without modeling their reflection paths. Future work could replace the MLP with a recurrent neural network (RNN) that encodes prompts, reflections, and revisions along each parent– child path. The resulting trajectory representations could exploit evolution history to predict candidate performance and guide full-validation selection. Our experiments cover two open-weight models, Qwen3.5-9B and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, but no closed-source models such as GPT [19] or Claude [2]. Whether the results extend to these model families remains to be tested.
