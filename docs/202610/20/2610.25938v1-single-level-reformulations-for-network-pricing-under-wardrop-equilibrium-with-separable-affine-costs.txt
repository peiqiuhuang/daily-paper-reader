Abstract 

We consider the problem of setting tolls on a subset of the arcs of a congested network so as to maximize the revenue of a network authority, anticipating that users route according to a Wardrop equilibrium. This problem is naturally cast as a bilevel program in which the leader sets tolls while the followers respond by choosing an equilibrium routing. We focus on the important special case of separable affine arc cost functions. Under this assumption, Wardrop equilibria admit Beckmann’s convex characterization, from which we derive three new single-level re-formulations of the bilevel problem, exploiting both Beckmann’s formu-lation and its dual: an arc-based formulation, which we solve exactly, and two path-based formulations, solved by column-and-constraint gen-eration. Beyond the reformulations, we further derive from Beckmann’s potential an upper bound on the optimal revenue that can be used to certify optimality gaps across all formulations. An extensive computational study on instances derived from the Sioux Falls and Eastern Massachusetts networks compares our formulations with the recent mixed-integer nonlinear formulation of Beck, Labb´ e and Schmidt [3]. The arc-based formulation solves substantially more instances to proven optimality (within a time limit of 1800 s) than the formulation of [3] and, unlike it, returns a feasible solution on every instance, remaining effective on the largest 74-node network, on which [3] solves none. The magnitude of the upper bounds on the tolls proves to be a major driver of tractability: the genuinely hard core of the benchmark emerges once the regulatory cap 

1arXiv:2610.25938v1 [math.OC] 19 Oct 2026 on tolls is removed. The two path-based formulations, used as heuristics, return a solution within 1% of the optimum on every instance whose op-timal value is known, often in seconds where exact formulations reach the time limit. 

## 1 Introduction 

Pricing mechanisms play a central role in transportation and telecommunication networks, both as a source of revenue and as a means of influencing users’ routing decisions in order to improve the overall performance of the system [1, 18]. In many practical situations, however, the travel cost experienced by users also depends on congestion effects generated by the aggregate traffic, a phenomenon well documented in the road traffic literature [4, 19]. As a consequence, pricing decisions and traffic equilibria become strongly coupled, leading naturally to bilevel optimization models in which a central authority sets tolls anticipating the equilibrium response of the network users [6, 11]. In this work, we consider a toll-setting problem in a congested network. The network is represented by a directed graph G = ( V, A ), where V denotes the set of nodes and A the set of arcs. For each arc a ∈ A, the unit transportation cost is given by a flow-dependent function ca(·), and no capacity constraints are imposed on the arcs. All arc cost functions are assumed to be nonnegative, continuous, nondecreasing, and separable, meaning that ca depends only on the total flow routed through arc a.A set K ⊆ V × V of origin-destination (OD) pairs is given. For each com-modity k ∈ K , the pair ( ok, s k) is associated with a demand dk > 0 that must be fully routed from ok to sk. We denote by Pk the set of elementary paths from ok to sk in G, and assume throughout that Pk̸ = ∅ for every k ∈ K .A leader seeks to determine nonnegative tolls τ = ( τa)a∈A belonging to a prescribed compact and convex set T ⊆ RA

This assumption is satisfied in particular when T is defined by independent upper bound constraints on each toll, which is a common setting in the literature. The followers are nonatomic users who route themselves selfishly according to Wardrop’s equilibrium princi-ple [19, 20]: for each commodity, flow is distributed only along minimum-cost paths with respect to the effective costs bca.The resulting optimization problem can be formulated as the bilevel program max τ ∈T , f F (f, τ )s.t. f ∈ W (τ ), (BW)

Depending on the application, F may model revenue maximization, network efficiency, or other system-level performance criteria [8, 10]. Problem ( BW ) is inherently difficult to solve, combining a bilevel structure, equilibrium constraints, and a dependence of arc costs on the equilibrium flows. In this work, we focus on the practically and theoretically important special case of separable affine arc cost functions [12, 15]. Under this assumption, Wardrop equilibria admit a characterization through Beckmann’s convex potential for-mulation [4], which provides a powerful and tractable framework for deriving exact single-level reformulations. The main contribution of this paper is the derivation of new exact single-level reformulations of problem ( BW ) in the affine separable case, exploiting both Beckmann’s formulation and its dual. We introduce three such reformulations — an arc-based formulation, which we solve exactly, and two path-based for-mulations, which we solve heuristically by column-and-constraint generation — and compare them with the recent formulation proposed by Beck et al. [3]. Beyond the reformulations, we show that the common assumption that every commodity admits a toll-free path induces finite implicit upper bounds on the tolls (Proposition 2), allowing the unbounded case to be treated without arbi-trary bounds, and we derive from Beckmann’s potential an upper bound on the optimal revenue that certifies optimality gaps uniformly across all formulations. An extensive computational study on the Sioux Falls and Eastern Massachusetts networks then shows that the arc-based formulation solves substantially more instances to proven optimality and remains effective on the largest, 74-node network, on which the formulation of Beck et al. solves none, while the two path-based heuristics recover the optimum — or a solution within less than 1% of it — wherever it is known. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the re-lated literature. Section 3 recalls Beckmann’s potential formulation for Wardrop equilibria and derives the dual formulation used throughout the paper. Single-level reformulations are then introduced and analyzed in Section 4. Bounds and a reduction of the model size are discussed in Section 5, and computa-tional experiments are reported in Section 6. Concluding remarks are given in Section 7. 

## 2 Related works 

Network pricing problems have been extensively studied in the literature, es-pecially in the context of transportation and telecommunication networks; see [1, 18] for surveys covering a broad range of models and applications. A large part of the literature considers the case where arc costs are fixed, that is, in-dependent of congestion effects. In this setting, the followers’ problem reduces to a collection of independent shortest path computations, one for each origin-destination pair. When the leader seeks to maximize toll revenues, the resulting 3bilevel problem corresponds to the classical network pricing problem , investi-gated for instance in [6, 7, 11]. Complexity aspects of this problem have received considerable attention: [7] establishes asymmetry results in the complexity of the multi-commodity case, and polynomial special cases have been identified in [17]. The framework considered in the present paper differs fundamentally from these models because arc costs depend on the equilibrium flow. Consequently, the lower-level problem is no longer a collection of independent shortest path problems, but rather the computation of a Wardrop equilibrium, which couples all commodities through shared congestion effects. Another classical line of research concerns congestion pricing and marginal-cost pricing. Under suitable assumptions on the arc cost functions, it is well known that marginal-cost tolls can induce a Wardrop equilibrium that minimizes the total travel cost, X

> a∈A

thereby achieving a socially optimal outcome. Such results are classical in transportation science and algorithmic game theory [14, 4]. However, these approaches focus on achieving socially optimal equilibria through analytically derived tolls, whereas problem ( BW ) considers a general bilevel optimization framework in which the leader may pursue arbitrary objectives, including rev-enue maximization or system efficiency, subject to a feasible toll set T . The structure of T itself has been the subject of dedicated studies: Harks et al. [10] investigate the impact of toll caps in privatized road networks, showing that re-stricting the toll space can significantly affect equilibrium outcomes and network performance. Several works have investigated pricing models involving equilibrium con-straints under assumptions that differ from those considered here. In [5], the authors consider fair resource allocation problems in capacitated networks, fol-lowed by a revenue optimization phase based on Lagrange multipliers associated with capacity constraints. Ba¸ sar and Srikant [2] study a Stackelberg game under Nash equilibrium constraints, where followers correspond to finitely many users choosing among predetermined routes, and where congestion and pricing jointly influence user utilities. Dempe and Zemkoho [8] investigate theoretical proper-ties and optimality conditions for bilevel road pricing problems with separable arc costs, providing a rigorous analytical foundation for this class of models. To the best of our knowledge, the work of Beck et al. [3] is currently the only contribution proposing both single-level reformulations and computational experiments for a bilevel toll-setting problem with Wardrop equilibrium con-straints in a setting directly comparable to ours. Notably, Beck et al. also con-sider a robust variant of the problem under budgeted uncertainty on the travel costs, whereas the present paper focuses on the deterministic case. We build upon the affine separable structure of the arc cost functions to derive alternative exact single-level reformulations based on Beckmann’s potential formulation [4] and duality arguments, introduce bounds, a model size reduction and provide a 4computational study of the resulting formulations.

The formulations introduced in Section 4 can be tightened with bounds that exploit structural properties of the toll-setting problem. When the data permit, they can also be reduced in size. This section establishes three distinct families of bounds, and one model size reduction. The first family of bounds (Section 5.1) is concerned with bounding the toll set T . In Beck et al. [3], explicit upper bounds on toll values are assumed, which may be interpreted as regulatory restrictions but are otherwise arbitrary. We show that, under the common assumption that every commodity admits a toll-free alternative path (e.g., [6, 11, 7]) — interpretable as a route offered by a competitor of the leader — finite upper bounds on toll values can be derived endogenously, without any arbitrary choice. Moreover, as our computational experiments will demonstrate, the magnitude of these bounds can have a signif-icant impact on computational performance. The second (Section 5.2) concerns default bounds on the variables appearing in the formulations of Section 4. These bounds are either valid inequalities, satisfied by every feasible solution, or constraints that can be added without loss of optimality, in the sense that at least one optimal solution satisfies them. In both cases they may improve the quality of continuous relaxations and thereby accelerate the solution process. They are added to the new formulations (i.e. the ones of Sections 4.2 and 4.3) and kept active throughout our computational experiments. The third family (Section 5.3) is of a different nature: an upper bound on the optimal value z⋆ of ( BW ), derived from Beckmann’s formulation. It can be used to certify optimality gaps but unlike the previous two it is not added to any formulation in our computational experiments. Lastly, we show (in Section 5.4) how to reduce the model size for formula-tions involving per-commodity arc-flow variables ( fa,k ). When the arc costs are independent of the commodity, commodities sharing a common destination can be merged without affecting the set of optimal solutions. This grouping reduces the number of per-commodity arc-flow variables and of the associated coupling constraints.

6.1 Experimental setup 

All experiments were carried out on a machine equipped with a 13th Gen Intel Core ™ i7-1355U processor (1.70 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM, running a 64-bit operating system. The formulations were solved with Gurobi 13.0.2 [9], ex-ploiting its ability to handle convex quadratic constraints as well as nonconvex bilinear terms; the latter is enabled by setting the parameter NonConvex to 2. Gurobi solves the resulting nonconvex quadratically constrained problems us-ing a spatial branch-and-bound framework combined with outer approximation techniques. All other parameters are left at their default values. Because the i7-1355U has a hybrid architecture (two performance and eight efficiency cores), we fix the number of threads to 4 ( Threads = 4) and pin the solver to the four logical processors of the two performance cores, so that every run executes on identical cores. Wardrop equilibria are computed by a deterministic single-threaded barrier method. They arise in the computation of the bounds and, for the constraint-generation formulations, at each iteration, where the equilibrium under the current toll vector is recomputed to turn the relaxation flow into a bilevel-feasible incumbent. All these computations are included in the reported solution times; each is solved in at most a few seconds, even on the largest (Eastern Massachusetts) instances. To further improve the reproducibility of the reported solve times, Turbo Boost was disabled, fixing the performance cores at their nominal base frequency of 1.70 GHz. The relative optimality gap tolerance is left at its default value of 0 .01%, so that a run is considered solved to optimality once this tolerance is reached. As a consequence, objective val-ues reported for different formulations on the same instance may differ slightly. Unless stated otherwise, a time limit of 1800 seconds is imposed on each run. 

Instances. Our benchmark is built from three road networks of increasing size, all drawn from the TransportationNetworks data repository [16]. Through-out Section 6 we essentially work with a subnetwork of the Sioux Falls net-work [12], composed of 12 nodes and 36 arcs and already considered in [3]; we turn to the two larger networks — the complete Sioux Falls network (24 nodes, 76 arcs) and the Eastern Massachusetts network (74 nodes, 258 arcs) — only to assess how the formulations scale with the topology. For each network we vary the number of toll arcs in |Atoll | ∈ { 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 } and the number of commodities along a nested demand ladder, where the level |K| = k uses the first k demands in a fixed per-network ordering, so that smaller demand levels are contained in larger ones. Further details are given in Appendices A, E and F. 

Grouping commodities by destination. Following Section 5.4, commodi-ties are grouped by destination in all the considered formulations; this is possible here because the arc costs of our instances do not depend on the commodity. Since origins and destinations play symmetric roles once arcs and OD pairs are 20 reversed, we assume without loss of generality that the number of distinct des-tinations does not exceed the number of distinct origins, reversing arcs and OD pairs whenever this is not the case. The reduction is applied uniformly to all formulations, ensuring a fair comparison; in particular, the formulation (7) of Beck et al. is used in its grouped-by-destination form. This form is equivalent to the original one in terms of optimal solutions, while offering a computational advantage over the ungrouped version that grows with instance size, so that our comparison is not biased against [3]. 

Formulations under comparison. We compare four formulations, which fall into two groups according to the guarantees they provide: 

• Two exact formulations, which solve problem ( BW ) to global optimality: the formulation of Beck et al. (7) and the arc-based strong-duality formu-lation (14). 

• Two path-based formulations, namely the path-flow formulation (15) and the complementarity-based formulation (16). Both involve a number of path variables and associated constraints that grows exponentially with the network size, and are therefore solved by column-and-constraint gen-eration. Starting from a restricted set of represented paths, one iteratively solves the current restriction and searches for violated path constraints; whenever such a constraint is found, both the constraint and the path vari-able associated with the corresponding path are added to the formulation. In our implementation, the set of paths represented in the constraints co-incides with the set of paths represented by the variables. The procedure terminates when no violated path inequality remains. Since the restricted models are nonconvex, this termination certifies feasibility but not opti-mality: it does not rule out the existence of a path whose addition, as a variable, would improve the objective. The returned solution is therefore not guaranteed to be optimal for ( BW ), and these two formulations are used as heuristics (see Section 6.5). The bounds from Sections 5.1 and 5.2 are always included in the formulations whenever applicable (i.e. if the bounded variables appear in the formulation) for our experiments.

This advantage is not uniform: on the instance |K| = 8 with 6 toll arcs, (SD-A) is markedly slower, taking 1268 s against 874 s for (BLS), and this single instance accounts for most of its cumulative time (1817 s, against 3790 s for (BLS)). The two gap columns reveal a second, more qualitative difference. On every instance solved to optimality, each formulation certifies its own solution: gap own 

is then at most 0 .01%, i.e. the solver tolerance. On the time-limited runs, however, the two formulations behave in opposite ways. For (BLS), gap own 

ranges from 0 .051% to 111 .4%, with a median of 21 .3%, whereas the pooled gap never exceeds 0 .881% and is at most 0 .01% on 12 of the 14 such runs: (BLS) frequently reaches an optimal, or near-optimal, solution, but its own dual bound remains too loose to certify it. Its difficulty on these instances is therefore one of certification rather than of primal search. For (SD-A), on the contrary, gap own 

coincides with the pooled gap on two of its three time-limited runs — |K| = 8 and |K| = 30, both with 10 toll arcs, at 0 .985% and 0 .281% — and pooling improves it only on the third, |K| = 7 with 10 toll arcs, from 0 .023% to 0 .010%. The strong-duality formulation is thus essentially self-certifying: pooling the dual bounds improves its gaps only marginally, so that the gaps reported for (SD-A) are not an artefact of borrowing bounds from (BLS). The constraint-generation procedure terminates in a handful of iterations. The shortest-path initialization provides a single path per commodity for each of the two reference toll vectors τ = 0 and τ = τ up ; being deliberately sparse, it leaves at least one violated inequality on every instance of the benchmark, so that separation is triggered throughout. The exact separation nonetheless terminates the procedure quickly: at most 11 path inequalities are generated on any instance, in at most 5 iterations. As each separation round amounts to one reverse-Dijkstra computation per distinct destination, the cost of (SD-A) is dominated by the solution of the successive restrictions — each a nonconvex quadratically constrained program — rather than by the separation itself. We emphasize that (SD-A) does not dominate (BLS) uniformly. On |K| = 7 with 10 toll arcs, (BLS) is solved within the time limit (in 1739 .1 s) while (SD-A) reaches it; on |K| = 8 with 6 toll arcs, both formulations solve the instance to optimality, but (BLS) is the faster of the two (874 .3 s against 1268 .4 s); and on |K| = 8 with 10 toll arcs, where both reach the time limit, (BLS) returns the better feasible solution (38 825 .91 against 38 785 .82, an advantage of about 0.1%). Conversely, (SD-A) is the stronger method on the |K| = 30 instances: with 8 toll arcs it solves the instance to optimality (in 439 s) whereas (BLS) finds no feasible solution, and with 10 toll arcs, where both time out, it returns the better feasible solution (107 221 .60 against 107 075 .08). Moreover, the three instances on which (SD-A) reaches the time limit (highlighted in bold in Table 1) are not the largest ones: (SD-A) solves |K| = 132 with 10 toll arcs in 27 .7 s, exploring only 225 branch-and-bound nodes, whereas |K| = 8 with 10 toll arcs 24 requires more than 3 .7 million nodes without closing the gap. The relative performance of the two formulations, and the difficulty of an instance for (SD-A), therefore depend on the structure of the instance rather than on its size alone.

6.5 Path-based formulations as heuristics 

We now evaluate the two path-based formulations, (SD-P) (15) and (CC) (16), solved by column-and-constraint generation as described in Section 6.1. As explained there, the termination of this procedure does not certify global op-28 timality for ( BW ); these formulations are therefore used as heuristics, and we assess them in terms of the quality of the solutions they produce and of the associated computational effort. 

Evaluation by bounding. For each instance, the heuristic returns a valid lower bound z on the optimal value z⋆ of ( BW ), obtained as follows. If the procedure terminates within the time limit (no violated constraint is found), the flow of the current restriction is a Wardrop equilibrium for the corresponding tolls, so the restriction’s optimal value is feasible for ( BW ) and is reported as z.If the time limit is reached, we take the tolls of a feasible solution of the current restriction, compute a Wardrop equilibrium for these tolls (by solving (3)), and report the resulting leader’s revenue, which is feasible for ( BW ). The certified relative gap z − zz

is then an upper bound on the true gap between the heuristic solution and the optimum. 

Results. The results are reported in Table 10, for both (SD-P) and (CC). 

Solution quality. Both heuristics prove remarkably accurate. On the 53 in-stances for which one of the exact formulations of Section 6.2 proved optimality, (SD-P) and (CC) both return a solution whose value coincides with z⋆, up to the solver tolerance, on all 53 of them. On the two remaining instances — 

|K| = 8 with 10 toll arcs and |K| = 30 with 10 toll arcs — for which no exact formulation closed the gap within the time limit, both heuristics match the best feasible solution found by any method. The certified gaps of Table 10 are cor-respondingly small: at most 0 .01% on 52 of the 55 instances for (SD-P) and on 53 for (CC), with a median of 0 .005%. The larger values, up to 0 .881%, occur on the two instances that no exact formulation closed — where the heuristic solution is the best one known and the residual gap may reflect the looseness of 

z — and, for (SD-P), on |K| = 6 with 10 toll arcs (0 .011%), where the returned value lies within the solver tolerance of the optimum. 

Comparison of the two heuristics. The two formulations return essentially the same objective values — the largest relative difference between their values over the whole benchmark is 2 .2·10 −5 — but they differ sharply in computational effort. (CC) is faster on 54 of the 55 instances, by a median factor of 13 .6and by up to nearly three orders of magnitude; over the whole benchmark it requires 20 .5 seconds and 25 069 branch-and-bound nodes in total, against 1026 seconds and 2 .3 million nodes for (SD-P). Both terminate within the time limit on every instance. The contrast is sharpest on the instance with |K| = 8 and 10 toll arcs, where (SD-P) needs 475 seconds and more than 1 .5 million nodes while (CC) terminates in 0 .8 seconds after 1 176 nodes, both returning the same objective value. Since the two formulations share the same column-and-constraint generation scheme, the same initialization and, as observed above, the same objective values, this gap in performance is attributable to the way the 29 equilibrium conditions are expressed: the complementarity conditions handled through SOS1 constraints in (CC) are processed far more efficiently than the strong-duality inequality of (SD-P), whose quadratic term couples all arc flows. 

Comparison with the exact formulations. The heuristics are most useful precisely where the exact formulations struggle. On the three instances on which (SD-A) reaches the time limit, (CC) returns in at most 2 .9 seconds a solution at least as good as the incumbent (SD-A) reaches after 1800 seconds; on |K| = 30 with 8 toll arcs, where (BLS) finds no feasible solution at all, it returns the best solution known in under two seconds. The constraint-generation procedure behaves as it does for (SD-A): the sparse shortest-path initialization leaves at least one violated inequality on every instance, so that separation is triggered throughout, but it converges in at most five iterations, generating at most 13 path inequalities on any instance. 

Sensitivity to the initial path set. Initializing with the zero-toll equilib-rium alone preserves the accuracy of both formulations: each still returns an optimal solution on every instance whose optimal value is known. The burden merely shifts to the separation, which generates about 60% more path inequal-ities over the benchmark (402 against 250). Initializing with the equilibrium at 

τ = τ up alone, by contrast, degrades the results markedly, and does so slightly more for (CC) than for (SD-P): (SD-P) returns an optimal solution on 46 of the 53 instances, with a worst-case shortfall of 10 .2%, and (CC) on 42, with a worst case of 11 .2%. The explanation is structural. At τ = τ up the tolled arcs are expensive, so the corresponding equilibrium largely avoids them, and the resulting path set offers little capacity to route flow through the toll arcs — precisely the flow on which revenue is raised. Although the separation is still triggered on every instance, it is driven by the relaxation flow and does not, on its own, recover the missing revenue-carrying routes: the column-and-constraint generation then converges to an impoverished restricted model, leaving the returned solution suboptimal on 7 of the 53 instances for (SD-P) and on 11 for (CC). Consistently with this, the two formulations, which return essentially identical solutions under the reference initialization, no longer do so under this one. These experiments show that the quality of the solutions returned by the path-based formulations is not an automatic consequence of the column-and-constraint generation scheme: it relies on the initial path set containing the routes that users take when tolls do not deter them. In this paper, we studied the toll-setting problem ( BW ) under Wardrop equilib-rium constraints, in the case of separable affine arc cost functions and with the objective of maximizing the leader’s revenue. Starting from Beckmann’s convex formulation of Wardrop equilibria and its dual, we derived three new single-level reformulations of the problem: an arc-based formulation (SD-A), which we solve exactly, and two path-based formulations, (SD-P) and (CC), solved by column-and-constraint generation. We showed that the common assumption that every commodity admits a toll-free path induces finite implicit bounds on the tolls (Proposition 2), which makes it possible to treat the case of unbounded tolls without resorting to arbitrary bounds, and we derived from Beckmann’s potential an upper bound on the optimal revenue that applies uniformly to all the formulations considered and is used to certify optimality gaps. On the computational side, we conducted an extensive study on instances derived from the Sioux Falls and Eastern Massachusetts networks, comparing our formulations with the recent formulation of Beck et al. [3]. The arc-based formulation (SD-A) proved markedly more robust: it solved to proven opti-mality substantially more instances than (BLS) and, unlike (BLS), returned a feasible solution on every instance of every network. The difference widened with network size — on the largest network, (BLS) solved none of the instances and returned no feasible solution on most of them, whereas (SD-A) still solved a sizeable fraction — and it is one of formulation rather than of instance size, since on all three networks some small-demand instances resisted while some larger ones were solved. Pooling the dual bounds of the formulations, rather than relying on each solver’s own bound, also allowed us to localize one diffi-culty: on many instances left unsolved by (BLS) with an incumbent, the latter turned out to be optimal, or nearly so, the obstacle being the certification of optimality rather than the search for a good solution. Used as heuristics, the two path-based formulations proved remarkably accu-rate: they returned an optimal solution on every instance whose optimal value is known on the subnetwork and on the Eastern Massachusetts network, and on all but a few on the full Sioux Falls, always within a fraction of a percent of the optimum; and they returned a feasible solution on every instance on which the exact formulations found none, often in a few seconds where those formulations reached the time limit. The complementarity-based formulation (CC) proved consistently and substantially cheaper than (SD-P), and, on the larger networks, the more robust of the two; the two return essentially identical solutions on the subnetwork, but no longer coincide as the network grows. This accuracy rests on the initial set of paths: an ablation of the initialization shows that the shortest paths at the zero-toll equilibrium are important to it. Finally, and beyond the specific figures, our experiments highlight that the magnitude of the toll bounds is a significant factor affecting computational per-formance. Several directions for future research emerge from this work. First, the path-based formulations (SD-P) and (CC) are used here as heuristics: termination of 33 the column-and-constraint generation guarantees feasibility, but not optimality. Given their accuracy in our experiments, devising a way to turn them into exact methods is a natural next step. Second, the paths and feasible solutions generated by the path-based heuristics could be used to initialize or warm-start the exact formulation (SD-A); our observation that the bottleneck often lies in certification rather than in primal search suggests, however, that the benefit of a better incumbent alone may be limited, and that such an initialization would have to be combined with techniques improving the dual bound. Third, a more systematic study of the influence of the toll-arc cost structure, only briefly touched upon here, would be of interest. Finally, another promising direction for future research is to develop tailored solution approaches that exploit the specific structure of the reformulations, such as the fact that the nonconvexity of (SD-A) is concentrated in a single bilinear constraint.

The following procedure is used for the three networks considered. Let t de-note the desired number of toll arcs; in our computations we consider t ∈{2, 4, 6, 8, 10 }. First, we compute a Wardrop equilibrium assuming zero tolls on all arcs, and we let f WE  

> a

denote the equilibrium flow on arc a ∈ A. Then, for each commodity k ∈ K , we compute a shortest path with respect to the arc costs ca(f WE  

> a

), and we order the arcs by decreasing number of shortest paths in which they appear, ties being broken arbitrarily. We then process the arcs in this order. For each candidate arc, we set it as a toll arc provided that a toll-free path still exists for every commodity afterwards; otherwise, the arc is left toll-free and the next candidate is processed similarly. The procedure stops once t toll arcs have been set, or once all arcs have been processed. Being greedy, it offers no guarantee of reaching t toll arcs while preserving a toll-free path for every commodity, even when such a set of toll arcs exists; on all the instances considered in our experiments, however, the desired number t was reached. Finally, since the candidate arcs are processed in a fixed order that does not depend on t, and since the decision taken for a candidate arc depends only on the arcs already selected, the procedure yields a nested family of toll-arc sets: for t1 < t 2, the set of toll arcs of the instance with t1 toll arcs is contained in that of the instance with t2 toll arcs.

Scalability. The larger network sharpens the contrast already visible on the subnetwork. Within the time limit, (BLS) solves only 7 of the 55 instances — all with |K| ≤ 6 — and, more tellingly, returns no feasible solution at all on 31 of them: on every instance with |K| ≥ 10, and on one further instance with 

|K| = 7. Its shifted geometric mean solution time, 1306 seconds, is close to the time limit itself. In contrast, (SD-A) solves 35 of the 55 instances, returns a feasible solution on every instance, and is more than five times faster in shifted geometric mean (233 seconds). On the six instances solved by both formulations, (SD-A) is faster on five, with a median speed-up of 21 ×. A single instance — 45 |K| = 4 with 6 toll arcs — is solved by (BLS) but not by (SD-A), confirming that neither formulation dominates the other; but the overall gap in robustness is wide, (SD-A) solving five times as many instances. 

Difficulty and network size. As on the subnetwork, the difficulty of an instance is not governed by the number of commodities alone. (SD-A) solves all five full-demand instances ( |K| = 528), in between 149 and 1355 seconds, from an initial path set of about 540 paths; yet it reaches the time limit on three of the five instances for each of |K| ∈ { 20 , 25 , 30 }, and even on two of the five four-commodity instances ( |K| = 4 with 6 and 10 toll arcs). 

Gaps at the time limit. The certified gaps show that, here too, the fail-ures of both formulations are primarily of certification rather than of primal search. On the ten instances that (SD-A) solves and on which (BLS) returns a feasible solution without proving its optimality, that solution is within 0 .79% of the optimum, and 0 .093% on average. On the nineteen instances that neither formulation solves, the pooled certified gap of (SD-A) remains moderate — a median of 2 .2% and a maximum of 13 .2% — so that even where optimality is out of reach, the strong-duality formulation delivers solutions of controlled quality. Overall, the complete network confirms the conclusion drawn on the subnetwork: (SD-A) is the more robust and more reliable of the two exact formulations, and the margin in its favour widens as the network grows.
