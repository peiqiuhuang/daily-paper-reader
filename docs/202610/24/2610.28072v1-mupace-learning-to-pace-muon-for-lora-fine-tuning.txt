## ABSTRACT 

Learning rate schedules are typically fixed before training begins, yet when fine-tuning large language models with low-rank adapters (LoRA), the appropriate scale varies substantially with the model, task, and adapter configuration. Practi-tioners re-tune a schedule for every new problem, yet a tuned schedule still cannot react to the training dynamics it encounters. We instead amortize learning rate selection across LoRA fine-tuning problems. We leave the base optimizer, Muon, untouched, so that its normalized update fixes the direction, and meta-learn only the global step size. Our method MuPACE is a small recurrent policy, meta-trained across a distribution of fine-tuning runs, that conditions on the observed trajec-tory and emits bounded adjustments to an interpretable analytic schedule. On 64 fine-tuning problems, MuPACE outperforms standard fixed schedules at a strong shared default on 64–78% of problems and is on par with the same schedules with per-task tuned peaks. Meta-trained only on models with up to 16 layers, MuPACE transfers one-shot to unseen tasks on models as deep as 64 layers, such as Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.8-27B, where a single run remains competitive with swept schedules. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Modern deep learning optimizers increasingly decouple the update direction from its overall scale. Adaptive methods such as Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) rescale gradients coordinate-wise, while more recent matrix-aware optimizers such as Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018), SOAP (Vyas et al., 2025), and Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) exploit the structure of neural network parameters. Muon, in particular, constructs updates for matrix-valued parameters by orthogonalizing the momentum update and has shown promising performance in language-model pre-training (Liu et al., 2025) and LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) fine-tuning (Qu et al., 2026). The perspective of separating the update direction from the step size is also reflected in the norm-based view of steepest descent (Bernstein & Newhouse, 2024). At iteration t, the optimizer forms an optimization signal st from the gradient gt = ∇θ ℓ(θt), such as the gradient itself or the momentum estimate. We denote by O the optimizer-specific map from the optimization signal st ∈ Rm×n of an m × n matrix to an update direction dt ∈ Rm×n of the same shape, with the update scaled by the learning rate ηt ∈ R+, i.e.:

Normal-ization can improve progress in challenging landscapes such as river valleys (Shen et al., 2026), but 1arXiv:2610.28072v1 [cs.LG] 20 Oct 2026 Preprint it also reduces the influence of gradient magnitude on the update size. A small residual gradient can still be mapped to a direction of approximately fixed norm, leaving ηt responsible for determining whether the optimizer makes useful progress or overshoots the local solution. Step-size selection is therefore an important and distinct control problem. In practice, this control problem is often solved by committing to a learning rate schedule before training begins. Common choices such as cosine decay (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) and warmup-stable-decay (WSD) (Hu et al., 2024) set ηt primarily as a function of training progress, while their overall scale is selected through hyperparameter optimization. This is expensive because finding a good learning rate schedule can require several training runs, and the resulting setting does not transfer across fine-tuning problems, as the appropriate schedule varies with the downstream task, adapter parameterization, batch size, and optimization budget (Biderman et al., 2024; Lee & Lee, 2026). This lack of transfer makes fixed schedules especially costly, since tuning the peak learning rate for every new problem adds an extra search procedure, while the resulting schedule still cannot react to the optimization trajectory. We therefore ask the following question: Can learning rate schedule selection instead be amortized across LoRA fine-tuning problems? We introduce MuPACE (Mu on Policy for Adaptive Control across LoRA Fine-tuning Episodes), a learned, trajectory-conditioned learning rate scheduler for LoRA. LoRA fine-tuning is especially relevant because only a few large organizations train large language models from scratch, while most users adapt existing pretrained models (Bommasani et al., 2021). Unlike previous learned optimizers that meta-learn the update rule itself (Metz et al., 2022b; Moudgil et al., 2025), we leave Muon unchanged and meta-learn only its global step-size schedule. A small recurrent policy observes the optimization trajectory and predicts bounded modifications to an analytic schedule family, explicitly separating changes in overall scale from changes in schedule shape. The scheduler is meta-trained across a distribution of fine-tuning episodes and then applied one-shot to new problems, producing an adaptive schedule within a single training run without hyperparameter search or downstream-specific tuning. To assess the effectiveness of our approach, we evaluate whether a single meta-trained scheduler can replace per-task learning rate search across a broad range of fine-tuning settings, spanning pre-trained models, downstream tasks, adapter configurations, batch sizes, and training horizons. On 64 problems, MuPACE outperforms strong fixed defaults on 64–78% of tasks and performs on par with schedules tuned separately for each problem, while requiring only a single trial. Moreover, despite being meta-trained only on models with up to 16 layers, MuPACE generalizes in a one-shot manner to large-scale models such as Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.8-27B (both with 64 layers) on unseen down-stream tasks. It achieves competitive performance close to that of swept schedules while requiring only a single run. Our key contributions are: • We introduce MuPACE , a meta-learned, trajectory-conditioned learning rate scheduler for LoRA fine-tuning that adapts a bounded analytic schedule while leaving the base optimizer, Muon, un-changed, enabling one-shot application without target-task learning rate search. • We show that a single meta-trained scheduler generalizes across fine-tuning problems and model scales. On 64 problems, MuPACE outperforms strong fixed defaults and performs comparably to per-task tuned schedules, while transferring one-shot to Qwen3-32B and Qwen3.8-27B on unseen tasks, where it remains competitive with tuned learning rate schedules in a single run. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Learning rate scaling and schedules. The maximal update parameterization ( μP) jointly defines width-dependent initialization, parameter multipliers, and per-layer learning rates, enabling near-optimal hyperparameters to transfer across width (Yang et al., 2021). Depth-μP extends this prin-ciple to residual networks with single-layer blocks (Yang et al., 2024). Transfer has been related to approximately width- and depth-independent Hessian sharpness under feature learning (Noci et al., 2024), though for Adam-trained Transformer language models, standard parameterization with suit-able per-layer learning rates also transfers and can outperform μP when per-layer constants are tuned (Everett et al., 2024). A parallel line of work concerns the shape of the schedule rather than its scale. Cosine annealing (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) remains common in LLM pretraining, along-2Preprint side warmup-stable-decay (WSD) schedules that maintain a constant learning rate before a terminal cooldown (Hu et al., 2024). Additional approaches include linear decay to zero (Bergsma et al., 2025) and schedule-free optimization (Defazio et al., 2024). However, frontier labs remain divided on the choice of learning rate schedule. Kimi K2 (Team et al., 2025b) uses WSD, DeepSeek V4 (Xu et al., 2026) uses a warmup, long constant plateau, and terminal cosine cooldown, whereas GLM-4.5 (Team et al., 2025a) and Kimi K3 (Team et al., 2026) report favoring cosine over WSD. Fixed-horizon theory similarly predicts power decay in easier regimes and a WSD-like schedule in harder ones (Li et al., 2026). Much of this work, however, concerns pretraining from random initial-ization over a known token budget, and does not establish that either the learning rate scale or the schedule shape transfers across LoRA fine-tuning problems. 

Online learning rate adaptation. Learning rates can also be adapted during training using heuris-tic or learned rules, rather than prescribed by a fixed schedule in advance. For example, the heuristic ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler (Krizhevsky et al., 2012) reduces the learning rate when a monitored metric stops improving. More flexible schedules can be learned by differentiating through the train-ing trajectory (Maclaurin et al., 2015), while Hypergradient Descent adapts learning rates online using hypergradients (Baydin et al., 2018). Related approaches include Self-Tuning Networks for bilevel online hyperparameter optimization (Mackay et al., 2019), D-Adaptation for automatically estimating an appropriate learning rate scale (Defazio & Mishchenko, 2023), and MetaOptimize for adapting step sizes based on their longer-term effects on future losses (Sharifnassab et al., 2025). In contrast, we amortize learning rate adaptation across a distribution of fine-tuning problems. Af-ter meta-training, a frozen trajectory-conditioned policy adapts one-shot to new problems within a single fine-tuning run, without downstream specific hyperparameter search. 

Learned optimizers and schedulers. Learning-to-learn replaces hand-designed update rules with meta-learned ones (Andrychowicz et al., 2016; Metz et al., 2022a). VeLO (Metz et al., 2022b) scales this methodology to a general-purpose optimizer using a hierarchical architecture in which a per-tensor LSTM hypernetwork generates the weights of a small per-parameter MLP update rule. It requires no per-task optimizer hyperparameter tuning but incurs roughly 4,000 TPU months of meta-training. Celo (Moudgil et al., 2025) obtains strong out-of-distribution meta-generalization with only 24 GPU hours by combining task augmentation with a learned per-parameter MLP update rule and a learned global scheduler, meta-trained in two stages. Celo2 (Moudgil et al., 2026) further separates update-rule learning from step-size selection. It meta-learns only the update rule, leaves the step size tunable, and defers learning a generally applicable scheduler to future work. Closest to our setting, L3RS specializes learned optimization to fine-tuning pretrained models and learns, for each layer, both an update magnitude and a mixture of directions supplied by base optimizers using temporal and optimization-statistic features (Kristiansen et al., 2024). Our approach instead focuses on a smaller part of the optimization process. We keep the update direction from a fixed base optimizer, Muon, and meta-learn only the global learning-rate schedule for low-rank fine-tuning.

The family is controlled by a 5-dimensional vector λ = ( λ1, . . . , λ 5), which is what the policy emits. This analytic form combines gradual warmup with flexible cosine decay, keeping the policy’s output space low-dimensional and interpretable while allowing bounded adjustments to a known-good default.

## 4 EXPERIMENTAL PROTOCOL 

We meta-train MuPACE on a distribution over LoRA fine-tuning tasks built from a deck of 37 

pretrained nanochat models (Karpathy, 2025) spanning depths {8, 12 , 16 } and widths 128 to 2048 ,trained on 100 Lots-of-LoRAs sub-tasks (Wang et al., 2022), ranks r ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 } and batch sizes 

b ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 }. Conditioned on such a cell, the adapter scaling is drawn as a ∼ LogUniform[8 , 32] ,the budget as T ∼ LogUniform[100 , 300] , and the seed ξ. We place adapters on the query and value (Q and V ) projections of every attention block (Hu et al., 2022). We initialize A with a random Gaussian distribution and B = 0 , so training starts from the pretrained model, and scale the update 

BA by a/r (Hu et al., 2022). MuPACE therefore must handle combinations of model scale, adapter capacity and scaling, downstream task, and training length. Full parameter ranges, the deck listing, and the sub-task ids are given in Appendix A. We open-source our implementation to foster future research 1.4.1 ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING 

MuPACE is an LSTM with a hidden width of 48 with one linear head per schedule parameter, ∼20 Kmeta-parameters in total. We estimate the meta-gradient with PES at σ = 0 .01 over a truncation window of L = 50 inner steps, keeping N = 8 task instances in flight and averaging the estimate over them. We train on the objective in Equation 9 with f = 0 .2, scoring the smoothed log validation loss over the last 80% of each episode to emphasize consistent performance beyond the initial loss drop. An EMA with decay 0.9 reduces batch noise. Each task runs for its budget T and then is resampled. The scale augmentation of Section 3.4 draws κ ∼ LogUniform[0 .05 , 20] . For the depth factor of Equation 6 we set Lref = √8 · 16 ≈ 11 .31 , the geometric centre of the meta-training depths. Meta-parameters are updated with AdamW at an outer learning rate of 10 −3 on a cosine decay with gradient clipping. We train for 9000 outer iterations, which takes roughly 72 hours on four NVIDIA H100 GPUs. Remaining hyperparameters are reported in Appendix B. 4.2 BASELINES 

We compare MuPACE against three fixed learning rate schedules, cosine (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017), constant (warmup to a flat peak) and WSD (Hu et al., 2024), which we run at a default peak of 10 −3, matching MuPACE’s default schedule scale for λ01 (H1 in Section 5) and with the peak tuned per task by validation loss (H2 in Section 5). In the out-of-distribution setting (H3 in Section 5) we add Hypergradient Descent (Baydin et al., 2018), which adapts the learning rate online from the dot product of consecutive gradients and, like MuPACE, needs no per-task sweep. We run it as published and with the depth-μP post-scaling of Equation 6 applied to its learning rate, so that it receives the same multiplier as our learned scheduler. Every method runs the same Muon update from the same adapter initialization, so only the learning rate over time differs. 4.3 BENCHMARKS

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

H1: Meta-learned schedulers can outperform standard default learning rate schedules on in-distribution fine-tuning problems. 

By default, MuPACE learns a bounded correction to a cosine learning-rate schedule, where the base schedule is scaled to have a peak learning rate of 10 −3. H1 therefore tests the value of this learned correction against cosine, WSD, and constant schedules at that same fixed peak. Win rates are tested against 50% using two-sided exact paired sign tests (Dixon & Mood, 1946) with Holm correction (Holm, 1979) across the three baselines ( α = 0 .05 ). The learned scheduler achieves lower test loss on 78%, 66%, and 64% of draws against cosine, WSD, and constant, respectively, all significant after correction (Figure 2). Median improvements are 2–4% across the three comparisons. A further check against cosine at its best global scale (2.154 × 10 −3)2 gives a smaller, non-significant win-rate advantage, while the learned scheduler retains a median reduction of about 4%. Overall, the learned correction provides gains beyond a strong fixed default by adapting the schedule to the task and to the observed training trajectory (Figure 4).

Effect of meta-training. We compare against the same scheduler architecture with no meta-training, fixing all output heads at zero so it follows the default cosine schedule from Figure 1. Meta-training lowers median in-distribution test loss and improves performance on Qwen3-32B (Appendix D). 

Analytic schedule parameterization. We ablate the analytic schedule family by replacing the five heads defining Equation 4 with a single head that predicts the learning rate directly. Direct prediction has similar typical in-distribution performance but a heavier tail of poor runs and no wins against the baselines in the OOD setting, suggesting that the analytic parameterization primarily improves robustness (Appendix E). 

Shape normalization. We remove the shape normalization of Equation 5, so the shape parameters can also change the schedule’s overall magnitude. This has no in-distribution effect, and on Qwen3-32B normalization is better on five of eight datasets, a possible but not significant transfer benefit (Appendix F). 

Depth-scaled baseline (fixed) schedules. We apply the same depth-dependent scaling Equation 6 to each fixed-schedule baseline at (i) its default peak and (ii) its best peak from an in-distribution sweep. On both models, MuPACE achieves lower test loss in nearly all comparisons. These results suggest that MuPACE improves beyond transferring depth-scaled schedules, with in-distribution tuned peaks (Appendix G). 

Depth-dependent post-scaling. We meta-train a variant without depth-dependent post-scaling. In-distribution performance is similar, but scaling improves test loss on all eight Qwen3-32B datasets, suggesting a benefit for one-shot transfer to larger models (Appendix G.2). 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduce MuPACE, a recurrent learning rate scheduler that adapts an interpretable analytic schedule to the observed optimization trajectory while leaving Muon’s update rule unchanged. Across 64 fine-tuning problems, it outperforms strong default schedules and recovers 78–83% of the benefit of per-task tuning in a single training run. Although meta-trained only on models with up to 16 layers, it transfers one-shot to 64-layer models and unseen tasks, where a single run remains competitive with swept schedules. ## E ABLATION : D IRECT LEARNING -R ATE HEAD 

In the following ablation, we replace the five schedule heads of Equation 4 with a single head that directly predicts a learning rate before depth-μP post-scaling. We extend its range downward (approximately [1 .1 × 10 −8, 9.0 × 10 −2]) to accommodate the small learning rates produced by the normalized analytic schedule (especially λ4). Everything else remains unchanged. At initialization, this head outputs a constant 10 −3 before post-scaling, so it must learn how the rate changes over time, including warm-up. The policy is meta-trained for 9000 outer iterations. Figure 10 shows example schedules after meta-training, demonstrating that warm-up emerges through learning rather than being set by the initialization. The direct head clearly outperforms default cosine and WSD, but not default constant, and performs worse than tuned constant (Figure 11). Its median performance is close to that of the analytic scheduler, with a difference of only 0.4% that is not significant ( p = 0 .38 ), although it performs worse on a few tasks. On Qwen3-32B, it trails all tuned baselines across the eight datasets but consistently outperforms Hypergradient Descent (Figure 12). ## F ABLATION : S HAPE NORMALIZATION 

In the following ablation, we remove the shape normalization of Equation 5, so the shape parameters can also change the overall magnitude of the schedule. All other components remain unchanged, and both policies are meta-trained for 9000 outer iterations. On the 64 in-distribution draws, the normalized scheduler wins on 33 and reduces median test loss by 0.15% (two-sided exact sign test p = 0 .90 ), so we find no in-distribution effect. On Qwen3-32B, it wins on 5/8 datasets, with a median reduction of 1.37% (sign test p = 0 .73 ). The differences are small on most datasets, but large where normalization helps, most clearly on mbpp ( 15 .4% )and wmt14 en de ( 8.3% ). With eight datasets, this suggests a possible transfer benefit rather than a significant one. G.1 DEPTH -SCALED BASELINE FIXED SCHEDULES 

We ask whether MuPACE still outperforms the fixed schedules when the latter are given the same depth-dependent scaling and their learning-rate peaks are selected without any target-task search. For each schedule family, we transfer (i) the default peak of 10 −3 and (ii) the best fixed peak from a sweep on the in-distribution evaluation, denoted p∗. For WSD and constant, the source-selected and default peaks coincide. Thus, the comparison tests whether MuPACE provides gains beyond transferring either default or source-tuned depth-scaled fixed schedules. All arms report test loss at their own best-validation checkpoint. MuPACE outperforms every depth-scaled fixed control on every dataset of both models except default-peak cosine on GSM8K for Qwen3-32B (Figure 14).
