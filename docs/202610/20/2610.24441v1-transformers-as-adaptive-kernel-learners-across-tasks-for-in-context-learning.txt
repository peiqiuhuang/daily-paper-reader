# Transformers as Adaptive Kernel Learners across Tasks for In-Context Learning 

Zhaiming Shen ∗ Alexander Cloninger † Rongjie Lai ‡ Wenjing Liao §

October 20, 2026 

Abstract 

Transformers have emerged as a dominant architecture in modern machine learning, with in-context learning (ICL) enabling models to adapt to new tasks by conditioning on prompts, without any parameter updates. To understand the attention mechanism in transformer blocks, a growing line of theoretical work interprets ICL through the lens of classical statistical estima-tors. Recent works have established a connection between the attention mechanism and kernel methods, while the kernel function is usually assumed to be fixed. In practice, the query and key matrices in the attention mechanism are trained, which allows the attention mechanism to serve as an adaptive kernel method. In this work, we demonstrate that transformers can act as adaptive anisotropic kernel learners. Specifically, we study ICL for low-rank linear and multi-index nonlinear regressions, and conduct numerical simulations to show that the learned attention scores closely resemble the oracle kernels for these function classes. We then develop a general framework to analyze the generalization error of ICL by connecting transformers to the oracle kernel estimator for each function class. Our theory gives an explicit and constructive implementation of the oracle kernel estimators for these function classes using a wide and shal-low transformer architecture, illustrating how the prediction is carried out by the interaction between the query token and the context tokens. Our work complements existing studies by pro-viding numerical and theoretical evidence of how the attention mechanism adapts to anisotropic kernels in both linear and nonlinear settings. 

# 1 Introduction 

Introduced by Vaswani et al. [2017], the transformer architecture has become a cornerstone of modern machine learning, powering significant progress in natural language processing, computer vision, and related fields. In contrast to traditional feedforward and convolutional neural networks, transformers are built upon an attention mechanism that enables each token to attend to all others, dynamically weighting their contributions based on learned relevance. This approach yields representations that are both highly adaptive and sensitive to context. Consequently, transformers underpin many state-of-the-art generative models, including GPT [Achiam et al., 2023], BERT [Devlin, 2018], SORA [Brooks et al., 2024], as well as their subsequent developments. Beyond their empirical success, transformers exhibit a striking form of in-context learning (ICL), also often refer to as few-shot learning, in which the model effectively adapts to new tasks by

This emergent capability has prompted a growing line of work aimed at uncovering its underlying principles. Recent theoretical studies [Bai et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024; Von Oswald et al., 2023; Aky¨ urek et al., 2023; Cole et al., 2024; Lu and Yu, 2025] demonstrate that transformers can implicitly implement classical estimation procedures, including least squares, ridge regression, Lasso, generalized linear models, and linear inverse problems. Notably, problems resembling ICL were studied even before the advent of transformers, often referred to as meta-learning or multi-task learning in the literature [Baxter, 2000; Pontil and Maurer, 2013; Maurer et al., 2016; Tripuraneni et al., 2021; Du et al., 2021]. Beyond linear regimes, a growing body of work has investigated the expressive power, approxi-mation capabilities, and statistical properties of transformers for nonlinear functions and sequence-to-sequence mappings [Yun et al., 2019; Takakura and Suzuki, 2023; Gurevych et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2026; He et al., 2026]. For instance, Yun et al. [2019] established the universal approxima-tion property of transformers for continuous sequence-to-sequence functions with compact support, albeit with network complexity that can grow exponentially with the input dimension. Extending this line of research, Takakura and Suzuki [2023] derived approximation and estimation guarantees for sequence-to-sequence mappings on infinite-dimensional domains under anisotropic smoothness assumptions, while Gurevych et al. [2022] studied the statistical properties of transformer classi-fiers under hierarchical composition and H¨ older regularity assumptions. More recently, Jiao et al. [2026] investigated the ability of transformers to overcome the curse of dimensionality in nonlin-ear function approximation, while He et al. [2026] established quantitative approximation bounds for smooth function classes, further characterizing the approximation efficiency and limitations of transformer architectures. Complementary studies [Havrilla and Liao, 2024; Shen et al., 2025a; Shi and Liao, 2026] have demonstrated that transformers can exploit low-dimensional structure, yielding approximation or generalization guarantees governed by intrinsic rather than ambient di-mensionality. These works have focused on approximating or learning a fixed target function or mapping, rather than characterizing the ability of a single transformer to learn previously unseen nonlinear functions from in-context examples. In contrast, in-context learning (ICL) places transformers in a fundamentally different regime, where a single model must implicitly encode and execute a family of learning procedures, adapting to previously unseen tasks through contextual examples without updating its parameters. This paradigm introduces a distinct form of task-level generalization, requiring the model to transfer its learned inference mechanism across different target functions rather than merely generalize to unseen samples from a fixed task. While substantial theoretical progress has been made in understanding linear ICL, extending these results to nonlinear function classes remains an active area of research. Recent studies have demonstrated that transformers can implement learning algorithms, including functional gradient descent, kernel regression, and polynomial regression [Li et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2025b; Hsu et al., 2026]. Complementary works have investigated nonlinear feature learning and the training dynamics of transformers [Kim and Suzuki, 2024; Li et al., 2024], while Kim et al. [2024]; Ching et al. [2026]; Shen et al. [2025b] established statistical guarantees for nonparametric ICL over smooth function classes. Beyond finite-dimensional regression and classification, ICL has also been investigated in the context of operator learning [Chiu et al., 2025], further broadening its scope to function-space learning problems. Another important yet less explored direction is understanding how shared structure across tasks can be exploited to reduce learning complexity and improve generalization in the task space [Lu and Yu, 2025; Cole et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026]. In inverse linear regression, Lu and Yu [2025] 2showed that a linear transformer can learn a cross-task prior and an effective regularization strategy. Cole et al. [2026] studied in-context operator learning for optimal transport on probability measure spaces and established generalization bounds that quantify the effect of the intrinsic task dimension. For nonlinear task families, Shi et al. [2026] characterized cross-task complexity through covering numbers and constructed transformers that exploit this structure. A generalization error bound is derived in Shi et al. [2026] to demonstrate the context-sample efficiency with low-dimensional cross-task structures. Despite of these theoretical progresses, how transformers learn and adapt to shared cross-task structure remains incompletely understood, both theoretically and computationally. In this work, we investigate how the attention mechanism adapts to shared cross-task structure in linear and nonlinear regression through the lens of adaptive anisotropic kernels. We demonstrate this kernel perspective through numerical experiments and theoretical analysis in two representative settings: low-rank linear regression and anisotropic kernel regression with noisy observations. Our main contributions are summarized as follows. 

• We investigate the in-context learning behavior of transformers in low-rank linear and multi-index nonlinear regression models. Numerical experiments reveal a strong correlation between the attention scores in the final layer of trained transformers and the underlying ground-truth algorithmic kernels in both settings, providing empirical evidence that transformers can implicitly learn task-specific prediction algorithms. 

• Motivated by these empirical observations, we establish the generalization error bounds for transformer-based ICL in both settings, demonstrating that transformers can implicitly im-plement low-rank linear regression and anisotropic kernel regression respectively. 

• We provide explicit, step-by-step constructions of wide and shallow transformer architectures that realize the corresponding in-context learning algorithms in both settings. These con-structions elucidate how transformers leverage contextual examples to perform task-specific inference without updating their parameters. The rest of paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the notations and preliminary definitions. In Section 3, we introduce the problem setup for In-Context Learning (ICL) and transformer for ICL. In Section 4 and 5, we illustrate how transformer adapt to the cases such as low-rank linear regression and anisotropic kernel regression, as well as discuss their statistical estimation error for functions in these two classes. We also refer the two regression tasks as low-rank linear model and multi-index model respectively. In Section 6 and Section 7, we rigorously show the generalization error bound for low-rank linear model and multi-index model respectively. Finally, Section 8 concludes the paper by discussing its limitations and potential directions for future research.

In this work, we demonstrated that transformers can act as adaptive and anisotropic algorithmic learners in context, extending the kernel perspective on ICL beyond the isotropic setting considered in prior work. Our numerical experiments revealed a strong alignment between the attention pat-terns of trained transformers and the underlying anisotropic kernels, suggesting that attention can adapt to the low-dimensional and direction-dependent structure shared across tasks. Motivated by these empirical observations, we developed a theoretical framework for both low-rank linear regres-sion and anisotropic kernel regression. We established generalization error bounds demonstrating that transformers can implicitly implement these learning algorithms even in the presence of noisy responses. We further provided explicit constructions of wide and shallow transformer architectures that perform inference solely through prompt conditioning without parameter updates. Together, our empirical and theoretical results suggest that attention goes beyond measuring similarities in the raw input space: it can capture task-dependent geometric structure and exploit intrinsic low-dimensional information that isotropic kernels may fail to capture. 

Limitations and future directions. While our analysis establishes representational and gener-alization guarantees for transformer-based ICL, it does not address whether gradient-based training can provably recover the proposed constructions. Future research could investigate the optimiza-tion dynamics of transformers, characterize the trade-off between depth and width, and extend the framework to settings with local task-dependent anisotropic structures. Such extensions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the adaptability and robustness of transformers as in-context algorithmic learners.
