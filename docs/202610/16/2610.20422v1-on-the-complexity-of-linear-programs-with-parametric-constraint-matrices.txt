Abstract 

While sensitivity analysis for linear programs with parametric right-hand side vectors is well established, cases involving parametric con-straint matrices remain less explored. To address this gap, we inves-tigate linear programs where the constraint matrix depends linearly on a scalar parameter. Our goal is to compute the minimum and maximum optimal values of these parametric linear programs within a fixed parameter range. This corresponds to solving two bilevel pro-grams. We show that both decision versions of these optimization problems are NP-complete by proving both NP-hardness and mem-bership in NP. We additionally identify three sufficient conditions on the parametric matrix under which the bilevel programs are no longer NP-hard. We introduce a reformulation for both problems, together with a Farkas-based certificate for the lower-level feasibility condition, and compare the execution time for solving them on standard bench-mark instances. The experiments highlight a practical dichotomy. Al-though both bilevel optimizations are NP-hard, computing the param-eter value corresponding to the minimum of the linear program is fast, whereas computing the value corresponding to the maximum is not. We relate this asymmetry to the parametric equality constraints, whose free dual variables leave the relaxation of the maximum reformulation unbounded.

1.1 Related work 

Three streams of research are related to our work: sensitivity analysis, robust optimization, and bilevel optimization. Parametric and sensitivity analysis in linear programming study how optimal solutions and objective values evolve when problem data are per-turbed. Studies mainly focus on variations in the objective coefficients or in the right-hand side. Perturbations on the constraint matrix have received less attention. Among the works that explicitly consider parameter-dependent constraint matrices, Zuidwijk [22] studies linear programs whose constraint matrix depends on a scalar parameter and derives local expressions for the evolution of the optimal value. Zuidwijk’s analysis relies on the characteriza-tion of regions in which a given basis remains optimal, leading to a piecewise description of the value function. Similarly, Khalilpour and Karimi [14] con-sider uncertainty on the left-hand side and propose a parametric solution approach based on basis-dependent computations. 3More recently, Derval et al. [7] propose bounding procedures to estimate the value function over a parameter interval, yielding computable upper and lower bounds. Derval et al. [8] study the evaluation of optimal solutions and objective values for a finite set of parameter values and develop warm-start strategies to accelerate repeated solves. Both works consider the same parametric model as the present paper, but focus primarily on its compu-tational treatment. Their contributions are complementary to ours, which studies the computational complexity of optimizing the value function over an entire parameter interval and develops exact single-level reformulations for computing its minimum and maximum. A related line of research is robust optimization [2], whose goal is to find a solution that optimizes the objective function under the worst-case realization of uncertain data while remaining feasible across all potential data realizations. This framework is also heavily motivated by parameter uncertainty within the constraint matrix coefficients, e.g., see [19, 1, 3]. In contrast to this focus on identifying a robust decision, our objective is to characterize the best- and worst-case values reached by the optimal value function of the linear program when the scalar parameter varies over a given interval. Our work is also connected to bilevel optimization. Indeed, optimizing the value function with respect to the parameter yields a bilevel formulation, where the upper-level variable is the scalar parameter λ and the lower level corresponds to a linear program. From a complexity perspective, the decision version of bilevel linear optimization problems is NP-complete; Jeroslow [12] established NP-hardness and Buchheim [5] showed inclusion in NP. This NP-hardness persists under other restrictive settings, such as min-max variants of bilevel linear optimization problems [11]. Moreover, bilevel optimization problems remain NP-hard even when the upper level involves a single deci-sion variable [20], or when considering the pessimistic variant with a fixed number of lower-level variables [13]. Despite this, our problem does not fall directly into the class of bilevel linear optimization problems. Indeed, the dependence on λ appears in the constraint matrix of the lower-level problem and breaks the linearity. Thus, existing complexity results for bilevel linear optimization cannot be directly transferred to our setting. Related com-plexity analyses have been conducted for bilevel problems involving bilinear terms, such as bilevel quadratic programs, toll-pricing and combinatorial pricing [13, 18, 6, 9]. In these formulations, the bilinearities arise in the objective function from interactions between upper- and lower-level decision variables, whereas in our setting they involve a single scalar parameter λ

that affects the feasible region. 41.2 Our contributions 

In this paper, we study parametric linear programs in which the constraint matrix depends linearly on a scalar parameter. As formalized in Section 2, we investigate the problem of computing the parameter values that minimize and maximize the optimal value of the resulting linear program over a given interval. In Section 3, we prove that both decision versions of the optimiza-tion problems are NP-complete by establishing polynomial-time verifiable certificates together with polynomial reductions from known NP-complete problems. We then identify three sufficient conditions on the parametric matrix under which both bilevel problems become polynomially solvable. In Section 4, we derive single-level reformulations for both problems. To this end, dualization of the lower-level is standard, but here it is valid only over parameter values where the lower-level is feasible and bounded. We there-fore pair the strong duality reformulation of the maximum problem with a Farkas-based certificate. Finally, in Section 5, we confront the theoreti-cal complexity picture with practice on benchmark instances proposed by Derval et al. [7]. Although both problems are NP-hard, the minimum refor-mulation can be solved efficiently, whereas computing the maximum remains significantly more challenging. Section 6 concludes the paper.

3.3 Membership in NP 

We now show that the decision versions of Problems (1) and (2) introduced at the beginning of this section belong to NP. 

Theorem 4. The decision problems D-min and D-max belong to NP. 

Our proof builds on the certificate approach used by Buchheim [5] to establish membership in NP for bilevel linear programming. In that setting, an optimal basis of the lower-level problem serves as a certificate, and once the basis is fixed, verification reduces to checking the feasibility of a system of linear constraints. Two additional difficulties arise in our setting. First, because the lower-level constraint matrix depends on λ, fixing a basis yields rational conditions in λ, which become univariate polynomial constraints. Second, as observed in Remark 1, the lower-level problem may be unbounded or infeasible for some values of λ, in which case an optimal basis certificate need not exist. # 4 Single-level Reformulations 

The bilevel formulations (1) and (2) are not directly usable by standard optimization solvers. We therefore rely on the reformulations introduced in Section 2.1 to derive equivalent single-level problems that can be handled by nonlinear optimization solvers. The min–min problem (1) requires no further work. As shown in Sec-tion 2.1, it is already equivalent to the single-level problem Pmin .We then consider the max–min problem (2). Unlike the min–min case, the formulation remains bilevel and cannot be directly reduced to a single-level optimization problem. Our reformulation relies on the lower-level prob-lem being feasible for all values of λ in the interval and having a finite optimal value. 5.3 Why the max–min reformulation is hard 

Both single-level models contain bilinear terms λx i or λy i, which Gurobi relaxes by McCormick envelopes [16, 10]. In ( Pmin ), xi ≥ 0 is bounded. In ( Pmax ), the dual variable yi is free when the primal has equality con-straints, so the relaxation can be unbounded and the solver might find no finite dual bound. This is the case in the 67 runs that end with an infinite gap. All except one of the 77 time limits of ( Pmax ) occur on an instance with at least one parametric equality (Table 4). The magnitude of the dual variables is a candidate explanation for the practical difference between the two models. We explore it by re-solving the 77 time-limit instances after adding a box −M ≤ yi ≤ M on every dual variable. We take M = 10 4 and a reduced time limit of 120 seconds. On the 35 instances on which ( F) proved the lower-level problem feasible for all 

λ, the box is decisive. Every one of them reaches a proven optimum, in a median of 0.73 seconds, where 1 200 seconds had left most of them without even a finite dual bound.

# 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we consider parametric linear programs with constraint ma-trices depending linearly on a scalar parameter. We study the problem of computing the minimum and maximum of the associated value function over a bounded interval. We prove that both corresponding decision versions of the bilevel problems are NP-complete, and we identify restricted assumptions under which the bilevel programs are no longer NP-hard. The min–min problem reduces directly to a single-level bilinear program, whereas the max–min problem requires a strong-duality reformulation that is valid only when the lower level is feasible over the whole parameter interval. Rather than assuming this condition, as is common in the bilevel literature, we certify it through a Farkas-based feasibility problem. The computational study on 180 instances derived from Netlib reveals a clear practical asymmetry between the two problems. The min–min formu-lation is solved on all but one instance, with a median running time below one second. In contrast, the max–min reformulation reaches a proven opti-mum on less than half of the instances, and the feasibility check itself reaches 25 the time limit on 54 of them. The difficult instances are almost exclusively those carrying a parametric equality constraint. Their dual variables are free, the McCormick relaxation of the corresponding bilinear terms is then unbounded, and the solver terminates without any finite dual bound. Impos-ing bounds on these variables restores a finite relaxation and lets the solver terminate, but the bounded model is no longer exact. Several questions remain open. Bounds on the dual variables that are valid rather than imposed would keep the reformulation exact and make its relaxation informative. A second direction is algorithmic. The problem depends on a single scalar parameter, and a dedicated method could branch on this parameter alone. 26 Statements and Declarations
