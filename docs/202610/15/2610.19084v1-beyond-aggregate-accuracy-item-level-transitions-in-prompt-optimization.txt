# Beyond Aggregate Accuracy: Item-Level Transitions in Prompt Optimization

Abstract 

Automatic prompt optimization (APO) uses feedback from model responses to improve prompts automatically. However, its effec-tiveness is usually measured through overall scores, which do not reveal which questions become correct, which previously correct an-swers become wrong, or whether improve-ments extend to unseen questions. We intro-duce Reflective Feedback-Driven Prompt Opti-mization (RFDPO), a tool that tracks recover-ies (incorrect to correct), regressions (correct to incorrect), and churn (all changed outcomes). RFDPO uses this feedback to guide subsequent revisions and records prompt changes through-out optimization. We evaluate RFDPO across five benchmarks covering law, knowledge, in-struction following, mathematics, and privacy, using GPT-5 as the optimizer and GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1 Mini as solvers. Our results show that overall accuracy can hide substantial changes: in five of nine configurations, churn was at least five times the absolute net change, and a single-item net change concealed up to 17 changed outcomes. Average validation gains were 7.9 percentage points, compared with 5.2 points on held-out tests. An exploratory abla-tion showed that item-level feedback reduced churn but did not improve test accuracy. These findings motivate reporting recovery, regres-sion, churn, and performance on unseen data alongside overall accuracy. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for document classification, information extraction, question answering, and instruction-following tasks. Their behavior is commonly con-trolled through natural-language prompts that spec-ify task instructions, relevant context, constraints, and expected output formats. However, even minor changes in prompt wording or structure can sub-stantially affect model performance (Sclar et al., 2024). Developing effective prompts therefore of-ten requires repeated revisions and evaluations. Au-tomatic prompt optimization (APO) seeks to auto-mate this process by iteratively refining prompts using feedback from model outputs (Zhou et al., 2023b; Yang et al., 2024; Pryzant et al., 2023). In LLM-based APO, an optimizer proposes prompt revisions based on evaluation feedback, while a solver executes the task using the revised instruc-tions. Despite advances in APO, evaluating optimiza-tion effectiveness remains challenging. Existing studies primarily report aggregate performance be-fore and after optimization, such as accuracy im-provements on benchmark datasets. Although these metrics indicate whether a revised prompt performs better overall, they do not reveal which previously incorrect examples were fixed or which correct examples became incorrect. This distinction is important for practical LLM applications, where prompt revisions may introduce unexpected fail-ures even when overall performance improves. For example, Ma et al. (2024) found that 10.9% of in-dividual predictions regressed across hosted LLM API updates for toxicity detection, and previously correct predictions broke in 87.9% of cases where aggregate accuracy improved. Developers have also reported difficulties debugging inconsistent LLM behavior across prompt variations (Errica et al., 2025). These observations highlight the im-portance of examining behavioral changes beyond aggregate scores. Consider two prompt revisions that each improve accuracy by two examples. The first may correct two previously incorrect examples without intro-ducing new failures, while the second may correct ten examples but break eight previously correct ones. Although both revisions achieve the same net improvement, their effects on individual ex-amples differ substantially. Another concern is whether improvements ob-served during optimization generalize to unseen examples. APO methods commonly select prompts using validation performance, and some also ex-pose validation examples as optimization feedback. Consequently, improvements on these tuning ex-amples may not translate into comparable improve-ments on held-out data (Recht et al., 2019). Under-standing prompt optimization therefore requires ex-amining both item-level behavioral changes and the relationship between tuning and test performance. To address these concerns, we introduce Re-flective Feedback-Driven Prompt Optimization 

(RFDPO), an auditable prompt optimization tool that explicitly tracks the effects of individual prompt revisions (Figure 1). RFDPO identifies 

recoveries (incorrect-to-correct transitions), regres-sions (correct-to-incorrect transitions), and churn 

(the total number of changed outcomes). Unlike approaches that primarily use aggregate scores, cur-rent errors, or execution traces to guide revisions, RFDPO returns the observed consequences of the preceding edit to the optimizer. Beginning with the second round, this feedback identifies recovered and regressed examples, their associated outputs, and the prompt sections modified by the previous revision. RFDPO also records prompt versions, evaluation outcomes, and resource consumption to support analysis of optimization trajectories. We evaluate RFDPO on five benchmarks— LegalBench-Hearsay, MMLU, IFBench, AIME, and PUPA—using GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4.1 Mini as solver models. Our results show that aggre-gate accuracy can conceal substantial behavioral changes: in five of nine configurations, churn was at least five times the absolute net change, and a single-item net change concealed up to 17 changed outcomes. Across edited-prompt returns, average held-out test improvements were approximately 61% of average validation improvements. We fur-ther examine how prompt revisions evolve across optimization rounds. An exploratory ablation on LegalBench-Hearsay shows that providing item-level feedback is associated with approximately 40% lower mining-set churn at similar net gains, although it does not improve test accuracy. Our main contributions are: • An auditable prompt optimization tool. We develop RFDPO, which incorporates item-level recovery and regression feedback into iterative prompt revision and records prompt versions, evaluation outcomes, and resource consumption (§3). 2• An empirical characterization of prompt revisions. We analyze recovery, regression, and churn across five benchmarks, demonstrat-ing how aggregate accuracy conceals changes in individual outcomes and how validation improvements differ from held-out test perfor-mance (§5). • An analysis of feedback and editing be-havior. We examine prompt modifications across optimization rounds and conduct an exploratory ablation to investigate how item-level feedback affects outcome churn. Based on these findings, we discuss implications for evaluating and reporting APO behavior (§5.3, §6). Overall, our findings motivate incorporating re-covery, regression, churn, and validation-to-test transfer into the evaluation of automatic prompt optimization, alongside conventional aggregate per-formance metrics. 

2 Related Work 

Automatic prompt optimization. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) has been explored through discrete token search (Shin et al., 2020), instruction generation and selection (Zhou et al., 2023b), LLM-based optimization (Yang et al., 2024), textual gradients (Pryzant et al., 2023; Yuksekgonul et al., 2025), and evolutionary ap-proaches (Guo et al., 2024; Fernando et al., 2024). Other methods optimize multi-stage pipelines or structured prompts (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024; Schn-abel and Neville, 2024; Khattab et al., 2024). Among these, MPO (Sharma and Henley, 2026) uses the same five-section prompt structure as RFDPO and applies section-local textual gradi-ents. More closely related to our feedback mech-anism, GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) uses reflec-tive prompt mutation and Pareto-based selection, while Trace2Policy (Zha et al., 2026) clusters er-rors and applies a regression gate when refining decision rules. Self-feedback methods also guide revisions through verbal critiques (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023). While existing methods use scored histories, execution traces, or regression checks to guide optimization, RFDPO explicitly returns the observed item-level consequences of the preceding edit to the optimizer, identifying re-covered and regressed examples and the prompt sections modified. Unlike regression-gated ap-proaches, RFDPO retains changed candidates even when validation performance decreases, allowing subsequent revisions to build on earlier edits while recording their effects. 

Behavioral evaluation and regression tracking. 

Aggregate accuracy can conceal important changes in model behavior. CheckList (Ribeiro et al., 2020) demonstrates the limitations of aggregate evalua-tion, while negative flips have motivated regression-free model updating (Yan et al., 2021). Paired outcome comparisons also have a long history in statistical evaluation (McNemar, 1947). For LLM applications, Ma et al. (2024) highlight how pre-viously correct behavior can break following API updates. Related studies show that small prompt changes can alter model predictions (Salinas and Morstatter, 2024), motivating sensitivity and con-sistency measures (Errica et al., 2025). However, some apparent prompt sensitivity may result from evaluation artifacts (Hua et al., 2025). Unlike eval-uations of prompt perturbations or model updates, our work tracks labeled recoveries and regressions across successive prompt optimization revisions using benchmark-specific correctness criteria. We additionally measure churn to distinguish the ex-tent of behavioral change from the net accuracy difference. This distinction is particularly relevant when several recovered examples are offset by re-gressions, leaving aggregate performance nearly unchanged. 

Evaluation protocols and generalization. 

Prompt optimization results depend on evaluation settings, including data splits, solver models, baseline prompts, and feedback availability. Consequently, published APO scores are not directly comparable across different protocols (Appendix A.3, Table 6). Moreover, improvements on tuning data may not generalize to unseen examples (Recht et al., 2019), and benchmark contamination presents additional evaluation concerns (Oren et al., 2023). Test-set exposure is particularly relevant: Trace2Policy reports a refinement round informed by held-out errors (Zha et al., 2026). In our diagnostic experiment, providing test examples as feedback yielded an accuracy of 95.3%, compared with 73.4% when they were withheld (Appendix D.2). This diagnostic illustrates the importance of distin-guishing optimization feedback from held-out evaluation. RFDPO excludes test examples from feedback and prompt selection within each optimization run. Because validation examples 3also contribute feedback, we treat validation performance as a tuning measure and separately report performance changes on test examples to examine generalization. 

3 RFDPO 

We introduce Reflective Feedback-Driven Prompt Optimization (RFDPO) that uses item-level evalua-tion outcomes to guide prompt revisions. RFDPO extends conventional feedback-driven optimization by explicitly identifying examples that become cor-rect or incorrect after each revision and returning these changes to the optimizer. Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow. 

3.1 Prompt Representation and Evaluation 

RFDPO uses a solver LLM to execute tasks and an optimizer LLM to revise prompts, without modifying the solver’s parameters. Following MPO (Sharma and Henley, 2026), each prompt contains five sections: System Role, Context, Task Details, Constraints, and Output Format. These sections remain fixed throughout optimization, al-lowing RFDPO to track which parts of a prompt are modified. The tool operates on three disjoint sets: mining (M ), validation ( V ), and test ( T ). Mining exam-ples provide feedback for prompt revisions, while validation examples are used for candidate selec-tion and, from the second round, also contribute feedback. The test set is excluded from optimiza-tion and evaluated only with the initial and returned prompts. 

3.2 Item-Level Transition Tracking

3.3 Feedback-Driven Prompt Revision 

Algorithm 1 presents the RFDPO optimization pro-cedure. The solver first evaluates the initial prompt, and the optimizer generates a revision using the observed outcomes. From the second round, the optimizer additionally receives the recoveries and regressions caused by the preceding revision on M

and V , together with the affected questions, solver responses, gold answers, previous text of modified sections, and validation accuracy changes. Each valid, changed candidate is evaluated and committed as the starting point for the next round, even if its validation accuracy decreases. This al-lows subsequent revisions to build on earlier edits without a regression-based acceptance gate. Under best-validation selection, RFDPO returns the committed candidate with the highest validation accuracy, breaking ties by mining accuracy and candidate order. Once a candidate is committed, the seed is not reconsidered; if no candidate is committed, the seed is retained. 4 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate 5 benchmarks using RFDPO and 2 solver models, covering different task types, an-swer formats, and scoring criteria. Table 1 summa-rizes the benchmark configurations. 

4.1 Benchmarks 

We select five benchmarks covering diverse tasks, including legal reasoning, general knowledge, in-struction following, mathematical reasoning, and privacy preservation. 

LegalBench-Hearsay (Guha et al., 2023) evalu-ates binary classification of statements under U.S. hearsay law. 

MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) consists of four-way multiple-choice questions. We select six subjects: college mathematics, computer security, econometrics, high school biology, philosophy, and professional law, optimizing each independently and reporting macro-averaged accuracy. 

IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2025) evaluates compli-ance with verifiable output constraints. An example is correct only when all applicable constraints are satisfied, using a programmatic verifier. (Zhou et al., 2023a). 

AIME (Mathematical Association of America, 2025) evaluates mathematical reasoning with inte-ger answers between 0 and 999. We use problems from 2022–2024 for optimization and the 30 prob-lems from 2025 for testing. 

PUPA (Li et al., 2025) evaluates privacy-conscious delegation to an external LLM. We op-timize only the query-redaction prompt. Correct-ness requires a composite score (quality + 1 −

leakage) /2 of at least 0.7.

4.2 Models and Experimental Protocol 

We use GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI, 2024) and GPT-4.1 Mini (OpenAI, 2025b) as solvers, both with temperature 0, and GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025a) as the optimizer with temperature 0.7. PUPA additionally uses a fixed GPT-4.1 external model and GPT-5 as its quality judge. All experiments use seed 0 and at most three optimization rounds. Optimization is skipped when initial mining accuracy reaches 95% , except for PUPA. The evaluation includes 19 baseline/final records: seven non-MMLU configurations and twelve inde-pendently optimized MMLU subjects. Both end-points of each matched pair are evaluated within the same run. Two earlier AIME runs, which used a 2,048-token output limit, are reported separately in Appendix C.5 and excluded from the analyses. We report test accuracy, validation accuracy changes, recovery, regression, and churn. We also examine revision-level transitions and recorded token consumption. Detailed run configurations, scoring procedures, and optimizer instructions are provided in Appendices A, A.2, and B.

5.3 Prompt Revision Behavior 

We analyze how the optimizer modifies prompts across successive rounds. In round 1, where no effect report of a previous edit is available, the optimizer modified an average of 4.3 of the five prompt sections, compared with 1.3 in rounds 2–3, where it receives recovery and regression feedback on its previous edit. Revisions thus became more localized after the initial round. 

Effect-report ablation (exploratory). To see what the effect report contributes beyond the op-timization round, we ran an exploratory ablation on LegalBench-Hearsay using GPT-4o-mini. Both conditions replayed the same round-1 prompt and continued through rounds 2 and 3. They differed in whether the optimizer received the item-level ef-fect report and its accompanying instructions (Ap-pendix B.1). We performed five runs per condition (Appendix C.4). Without the report, round-2 revisions already modified only 1.6 prompt sections on average, com-pared with 2.0 when the report was included. Thus, narrower edits also occur without the report. The report did change the number of changed outcomes. With it, an average of 3.1 mining exam-ples changed correctness per round, compared with 5.2 without it ( p = 0 .024 , exact permutation test over runs, uncorrected). This average pools rounds 2 and 3, and round 3 starts from different parent prompts in the two conditions. Both conditions achieved similar net gains of 0.3 and 0.2 mining examples per round, respectively. Test accuracy fell in both conditions, by 5.3 and 5.7 percentage points with and without the report, respectively, so neither condition improved test ac-curacy. We therefore treat the churn difference as exploratory evidence about what item-level feed-back changes in this setting, not as evidence that it improves accuracy.

6 Discussion 

Beyond aggregate accuracy. Our findings high-light the limitations of evaluating prompt opti-mization solely through aggregate accuracy. In five of the nine configurations, churn was at least five times the absolute net change, with a single-item improvement or decline concealing up to 17 changed outcomes. Such changes are important in practice because an optimized prompt may intro-duce failures on examples that previously worked. Recovery and regression tracking makes these changes visible, allowing developers to distinguish newly corrected examples from newly introduced failures. This aligns with regression testing for LLM applications, where previously correct behav-ior must be monitored as systems evolve (Ma et al., 2024). 

Role of item-level feedback. The optimizer made more localized revisions in later rounds, but our ablation shows that this behavior cannot be attributed solely to the effect report. Neverthe-less, providing item-level feedback reduced aver-age mining-set churn from 5.2 to 3.1 examples per round while maintaining similar net gains. This suggests that explicit recovery and regression feed-back may help limit unintended changes without necessarily improving overall accuracy. However, the evidence remains exploratory, as the ablation covers one benchmark and solver with five runs per condition, pools two rounds, and includes addi-tional optimizer instructions with the report. Test accuracy declined in both conditions, so we do not interpret the reduced churn as improved generaliza-tion. 

Generalization and reporting implications. Im-provements during optimization do not necessarily transfer to unseen examples. Across edited-prompt returns, average test gains were approximately 61% of average validation gains, and four runs expe-rienced test declines despite validation improve-ments. Because validation examples also guide optimization, their performance should be inter-preted as tuning results rather than independent ev-idence of generalization. We therefore recommend reporting recovery, regression, and churn along-side validation and test accuracy, prompt versions, evaluation exclusions, and resource consumption. These measurements can be obtained from exist-ing item-level evaluations without additional solver calls. RFDPO automatically records this infor-mation, supporting more transparent analysis of prompt optimization (Appendix D.4). 

7 Conclusion 

Aggregate accuracy captures the overall effect of prompt optimization but obscures which examples improve or regress. We presented RFDPO, an auditable optimizer that tracks item-level recov-eries, regressions, and churn and incorporates this feedback into subsequent revisions. Across five benchmarks and two solver models, a single-item net change concealed up to 17 changed outcomes, while churn was at least five times the absolute net change in five of nine configurations. Aver-age held-out test gains were approximately 61% of validation gains. An exploratory Hearsay ablation showed that item-level feedback reduced churn at similar net gains but did not improve test accuracy. These findings highlight that prompt optimization can introduce failures despite aggregate improve-ments, motivating the reporting of recovery, regres-sion, churn, and validation-to-test transfer along-side accuracy. 8Limitations 

Evaluation scope. Our evaluation covers five benchmarks, two closed-weight solvers, and one optimizer, with a single seed and relatively small test sets. We did not evaluate open-weight models or longer optimization trajectories. Differences in selection rules and evaluation configurations also limit cross-model comparisons. 

Evaluation variability. Observed recovery and regression may partly reflect model output vari-ability rather than prompt changes, as shown by unchanged-prompt re-evaluations. PUPA addition-ally relies on an LLM judge, while the IFBench verifier supports only a subset of constraint types. 

Feedback ablation. Our exploratory ablation covers one benchmark and solver, with five runs per condition. It combines two optimization rounds, and the effect report includes additional optimizer instructions. Although feedback reduced mining-set churn, it did not improve test accuracy, and its effect may not generalize. 

Generalization and data exposure. Validation examples are used for both feedback and selection, potentially inflating tuning gains. Test examples remain excluded from feedback within each run, but small test sets limit generalization claims. Our verbatim-overlap check also cannot detect para-phrased copying or semantic memorization. 

Ethical Considerations 

Data come from public benchmarks, resplit as de-scribed in Table 1. PUPA contains personal in-formation from public user–LLM conversations. Hosted solver and optimizer APIs receive the original queries during evaluation and feedback; redaction protects only the designated external model. String-matched leakage does not capture paraphrased or inferred information. Released traces would contain item IDs and outcomes only, without raw inputs, predictions, or gold text (Ap-pendix D.4).

B.1 Optimizer System Prompt 

{task_description} and {max_rounds} are filled per benchmark and run. The template’s wording is preserved, with reflowed lines and ASCII arrows typeset as →.

Optimizer system prompt (best-validation version) 

You are working with an expert prompt engineer to help a smaller LLM (the “solver”) solve {task_description} more reliably. 

HOW THIS PROCESS WORKS (read carefully – this is a measured, multi-round experiment, not a one-shot rewrite): 1. You get up to {max_rounds} rewrite rounds. After each round, your rewritten prompt is run on a WORKING SET of examples and on a VALIDATION set – a second, disjoint batch meant to approximate unseen data. 2. From round 2 on, you are shown the measured EFFECT of your previous rewrite, IN FULL, on BOTH sets: every working-set item it RECOVERED (wrong → right), every item it REGRESSED (right → wrong) together with the solver’s new wrong answer, the previous text of every section you changed, and – just as fully – every VALIDATION item it recovered and every validation item it regressed, with the same detail. Nothing about validation is held back from you. 3. In those later rounds your job is causal: first diagnose WHICH of your previous edits caused WHICH regressions and recoveries on EITHER set, then rewrite – keep the edits that recovered items, revert or repair the edits that regressed items, 

14 and address the remaining failures. Do not churn wording that was not implicated by the evidence. 4. There is no “last round always ships” rule. Every round commits unconditionally to a version history, and at the end, whichever COMMITTED round scores BEST on validation accuracy is the one that actually ships – not necessarily the last round, and not necessarily this one. Validation accuracy is therefore not just a diagnostic number: it is literally the criterion your rewrite is competing on. Treat every round as a real, standalone candidate for shipping, not a throwaway step toward some final round. 5. Because you now see every validation item that flips, a rewrite that merely patches the specific items shown – without addressing the underlying rule that produced them – will look good this round and can still fail on the next genuinely new case. Prefer fixing the general rule that explains the failures over specific patches to the exact items shown. You will see: the solver’s current markdown-formatted prompt; (from round 2) the EFFECT REPORT of your previous rewrite described above; EVERY question the solver currently gets wrong on the working set, with the solver’s answer and the correct answer alongside (not a sample – all of them); and EVERY question the current prompt already gets right on the working set (not a sample – all of them), so you know exactly what must not break. 

CRITICAL RULE ABOUT EXAMPLES – READ BEFORE WRITING ANYTHING: you are shown every failure, every success, and every validation item so you can DIAGNOSE what is going wrong, NOT so you can turn them into illustrative examples. When you write an invented example in the Context or Task Details section: (a) Do NOT reuse the underlying scenario, fact pattern, or entities of ANY failure, gold, or validation item shown to you in this conversation – not verbatim, and not paraphrased with different names. Changing “Linda and her editors” to “Sarah and her colleagues” is still copying; the solver pattern-matches on the STORY, not the names. (b) Before writing an invented example, actively check: does this resemble the setup of anything I was just shown (a lab report, a bystander pointing, an email admitting knowledge, a customer asking for a refund, someone hearing about a planned crime, a doctor’s diagnosis, and so on)? If yes, discard it and invent a genuinely different scenario. (c) Prefer stating the principle abstractly with NO example at all over reusing a shown scenario’s shape. An abstract rule generalizes; a disguised replay of a shown item does not – it is memorization with a different name attached, and it is exactly what causes a prompt to score well on the items you were shown and fail on the next genuinely new one. 

CRITICAL FACT ABOUT THE SOLVER: it has NO hidden scratchpad. Its ONLY reasoning space is the text it actually writes out. So “think step by step” works only if the solver WRITES the steps in its output, before the final answer line. Instructions like “reason internally”, “use a scratchpad but do not show it”, or “output only the answer” DESTROY the reasoning entirely – for this model they are identical to “do not reason”, regardless of task type. Where a task does not need reasoning, the correct instruction is to keep the answer direct – never phrasings like these. 

FIRST, identify the task type from the failures you are shown: (i) Reasoning tasks – anything needing calculation, derivation, formal logic, OR applying a rule/definition to a specific scenario (e.g. mathematics, econometrics, and hearsay / most legal analysis): the solver RELIABLY does better when it WRITES OUT its working step by step before the final answer line. A bare answer starves it. (ii) Pure factual-recall / lookup tasks – where the answer is simply known or not, with no intermediate steps (e.g. a memorised definition or fact): forced step-by-step reasoning can make the solver over-think and second-guess a fact it already recalled, LOWERING accuracy. Sharpen the definitions and framing instead, and keep the answer direct. When unsure which it is, ALLOW visible reasoning – it helps more often than it hurts, and the final answer line is extracted either way. Match your rewrite to the task type before doing anything else. 

A good rewrite: surfaces the general reasoning principle that separates correct from incorrect answers on this task, and states it in terms the solver can apply to any new case it has not seen before; gives the solver the mental scaffolding it needs – a clearer way to think about the problem, a checklist, a decision procedure, a definition it can lean on; illustrates principles with GENUINELY INVENTED examples when helpful – different scenario, different entities, different setup from anything you were shown – or no example at all; keeps the fixed markdown schema ( ## System Role , Context, Task Details, Constraints, Output Format), where you may edit any section but must not add or remove these headers; keeps the FINAL answer line’s format exactly (e.g. a line Answer: <LETTER> or Answer: Yes/No ), which is ALL the scorer reads and is searched for anywhere in the output, so you MAY, and for reasoning tasks SHOULD, let the solver write visible step-by-step working BEFORE it (NEVER add “output only the answer”, “do not show your working”, or “reason internally”); and MATCHES the reasoning style to the task type, with the objective being accuracy, never verbosity for its own sake. 

RESPONSE FORMAT: you MAY begin your reply with a short ## Analysis section (2-6 sentences diagnosing which previous edit caused which regression or recovery – state the cause before the fix; in round 1 you may use it to state your hypothesis instead). The ## Analysis section is discarded automatically by the parser. After it, output the full rewritten markdown with the five standard section headers. No other prose, no code fences, nothing after the last section.

AIME. a competition-level (AIME) math problem, substantially harder than grade-school word problems – multi-step algebra, combinatorics, number theory, geometry, or probability. The correct answer is always a specific integer between 0 and 999. The output must end with a line containing the final numeric answer. 

IFBench. an instruction-following task with the same mechanically verifiable constraint structure and the same FINAL RESPONSE: marker convention as IFEval (see that description for the full explanation), but drawn from a broader set of constraint types (word/consonant/vowel counts, keyword-frequency and forbidden-word rules, formatting requirements, and more). Correctness means the text after the marker satisfies every listed constraint; there is no single gold answer to match. 

PUPA. a privacy-conscious query-rewriting task: the input is a PRIVATE user request (may contain names, contact info, employers, or other personal details). You are ONLY editing the REWRITE instruction – the prompt that tells a model how to anonymize/generalize the request before it is sent to a separate, untrusted external model. You are NOT editing how the final answer to the user is composed; that step is fixed elsewhere. A failure means the rewritten request still leaked one or more specific private details (shown to you as “leaked N/M PII units”) – the fix is to make the rewrite instruction more systematic about generalizing/abstracting every private detail (names → roles, specific employers → generic descriptions, exact locations → broader regions, etc.) while still preserving enough of the request’s substance that the external model’s answer stays useful. The “gold”/quality signal shown to you reflects the FINAL answer’s usefulness (not directly under your control), so do not sacrifice the request’s meaning purely to hide details – an unusable rewrite scores just as badly as a leaky one.

B.4 Per-Round Feedback 

The user message contains the iteration number and budget, the current prompt, every mining failure (question, wrong output, gold answer), and every mining success (question and gold). From round 2, its effect report adds the previous text of changed sections, recovered and regressed items on both M

and V , and before/after validation accuracy. Recovered items include question and gold; regressed items also include the new wrong output, truncated to its last 400 characters. The message ends with a request to diagnose the observations and return the five-section markdown. The parser discards an optional ## Analysis preamble; a no-change response does not commit a new version. Hearsay: returned Task Details (round 2 excerpt) 

Decide whether the given statement is hearsay by applying this decision procedure and explain your steps briefly before the final answer: 

Step 1. Is there a “statement”? Identify whether the evidence is an assertion (oral/written) or assertive conduct. Non-assertive words (questions, commands, offers) and non-assertive conduct are not statements. 

Step 2. Was it made out of court? In-court testimony in this proceeding is not hearsay for this purpose. 

Step 3. What specific proposition is the evidence offered to prove (ultimate or intermediate)? Then apply the independent relevance test: would the evidence still be probative of that proposition even if the content of the statement were false? If yes, it is offered for a non-truth purpose; if no, it is offered for its truth. 

Step 4. If the evidence is offered solely for a non-truth purpose, answer No. Common non-truth purposes: effect on the listener, notice, knowledge or motive where only the hearing of the words matters; proving that a conversation or words occurred; and circumstantial use of words to show a condition or state of the speaker unrelated to their truth. 

Step 5 (guardrail on embedded assertions). If the proponent relies on the statement to establish an intermediate fact that itself depends on the truth of an asserted proposition within it (e.g., “I heard/know/saw X”), the statement is used for its 

18 truth; treat as hearsay. Do not invoke the guardrail when the use is only to show that words were spoken, the speaker’s state, or an effect on the listener where content truth is irrelevant under Step 3.  

> Step 6. If out-of-court and offered for the truth of what it asserts, answer Yes; otherwise answer No.

D Evaluation Checks and Artifacts 

D.1 Re-evaluation of an Unchanged Prompt 

A GPT-4o-mini IFBench run that returned no edited prompt re-evaluated an unchanged prompt on the same 42 test items: one outcome changed (no recoveries, one regression). The edited prompt of the main GPT-4o-mini IFBench run, which started from a different seed prompt, changed ten outcomes (six recoveries, four regressions). A GPT-4o-mini AIME run that returned its seed prompt shows the same effect: two evaluations of the identical prompt solved 4 and 3 of 30 problems (Appendix C.5). 

D.2 Test-Exposure Diagnostic 

A diagnostic on LegalBench-Hearsay used an earlier variant of the optimizer with GPT-5, seed 42, two rounds, 30 training items, and 64 test items. With the test items held out, accuracy changed from 44/64 to 47/64 (68.8% to 73.4%). Supplying the same test items as optimizer feedback raised accuracy to 61/64 (95.3%), a gain more than five times the held-out gain. This run is excluded from all other analyses.
