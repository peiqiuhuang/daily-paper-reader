Large language model writing interfaces often make users steer outputs by repeatedly articulating desired changes in natural language. Yet writers may recognize useful stylistic directions only after seeing alternatives, making revision recall-heavy. We present DimSteer, an authoring interface that samples prompt-local completions, discovers high-variance activation-space axes of variation, labels them, and exposes them as sliders with pole previews, diff comparison, and reset controls. Users can manipulate discovered dimensions, reducing the need to reformulate prompts for each stylistic adjustment. In a within-subjects study with 16 participants against a matched prompt-only baseline, DimSteer reduced mental demand, effort, and frustration while preserving comparable perceived success. Participants valued the surfaced dimensions, yet 15 of 16 disagreed that they would have thought to request the same changes in a prompt. Results suggest prompt-local controls can shift LLM authoring from recall-based prompting toward recognition-based exploration and direct manipulation, while preserving prompting for open-ended edits. The deeper issue is not simply whether a model can produce a desired style, but whether a writer can identify and articulate the right preference precisely enough to steer it [22]. A growing body of work responds by combining language with direct manipulation [10, 20]. DirectGPT brings localized, direct editing to LLM outputs [14]; PromptCanvas turns task-relevant facets into dynamic widgets [2]; and Malleable Prompting reifies preference expressions as sliders, toggles, dropdowns, and other controls [25]. Related systems expose semantically defined steering axes in chat [3] and image generation [11]. These approaches make a specified preference easier to manipulate. But the control space is still defined semantically—by what a user says, what a designer chooses, or what a language model proposes—rather than discovered from the model’s own prompt-local variation. This distinction matters because preferences are often constructed in context rather than retrieved fully formed [21], and interactive elicitation can surface considerations users did not anticipate on their own [12]. In classic HCI terms, this suggests an opportunity to shift from recall toward recognition [16]: instead of requiring writers to name every useful dimension before they can manipulate it, an interface could surface candidate dimensions for them to recognize and evaluate. Rather than asking writers to enumerate the control space, we derive candidate controls from variation already present in the model’s responses. Prior interpretability work shows that some high-level model properties admit approximately linear representations and can be manipulated through activation-space interventions [18, 19, 23, 26], while other features are intrinsically multidimensional [7]. Most recently, researchers sample the model repeatedly and use the resulting activation variance to recover prompt-specific stylistic axes without manually specifying the target attributes, demonstrating that these axes often correspond to stylistic dimensions people later request [13]. This establishes a technical route to discovering controls. It leaves the central HCI question open: can writers recognize, interpret, and productively use machine-discovered dimensions they did not articulate themselves? We present DimSteer, an LLM authoring interface that closes this loop from discovery to control. For each prompt, DimSteer samples a cloud of completions, extracts the one to three highest-variance stylistic axes from the model’s activations, labels them, and renders them as continuous sliders beside the draft, each tagged with the share of variation it explains. Writers steer by dragging: adjustments are applied through activation-level interventions rather than re-prompting, and a persistent diff view shows what changed between versions. When discovery misses a dimension a writer wants, they can define their own control, which is compiled on the fly into a contrastive steering vector [19]. The pipeline is training-free and prompt-conditional: the controls presented to a writer are specific to what the model itself varies for that instruction. We evaluate DimSteer in a within-subjects study (N = 16) against a matched prompt-only baseline using the same target model and diff view. Prior work has already shown that direct-manipulation controls can improve preference adjustment when the relevant dimensions are specified in advance [25]; our central question is what happens when the dimensions themselves are discovered. Participants reported lower mental demand, effort, and frustration with DimSteer while maintaining comparable perceived success. They also strongly endorsed the surfaced dimensions as useful, even though most reported they would not have thought to request the same changes in a prompt. We examine qualitative cases in which participants recognized useful dimensions after they were surfaced, ranging from relabeling vague prior intentions to discovering conceptual handles they explicitly said they could not name beforehand. 2 Related Work 2.1 Prompting and LLM Writing Workflows 

LLM writing interfaces commonly rely on natural-language prompting as the primary mechanism for control. In these interfaces, the prompt acts not only as an instruction to the model, but also as the user’s main mechanism for specifying preferences, constraints, and revisions. Prior work has shown that this interaction model can impose substantial cognitive demands. Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. found that non-AI experts often struggle to formulate effective prompts, relying on brittle strategies and folk theories about model behavior [24]. Subramonyam et al. describe a related “gulf of envisioning,” in which users have difficulty anticipating what a model can produce and translating underspecified goals into effective prompt instructions [22]. In writing contexts, this difficulty is amplified because desired changes are often stylistic, rhetorical, or affective rather than clearly specified factual requirements. Studies of LLM-assisted writing further show that writing with models is iterative rather than one-shot. Mysore et al. identify recurring patterns of human-AI collaboration in which users revise intents, compare alternatives, and modify prior generations over time [15]. Gero et al. similarly emphasize that working with multiple LLM outputs creates sensemaking burdens: users must compare versions, identify differences, and infer how a prompt or model behavior led to a particular output [9]. These findings motivate interfaces that reduce the burden of articulating and tracking stylistic changes. DimSteer builds on this motivation by shifting part of the interaction from prompt formulation to recognition and manipulation of surfaced dimensions of variation. 

2.2 Direct Manipulation for LLM Interfaces 

The design of DimSteer is grounded in the HCI principle of direct manipulation: interfaces can reduce cognitive effort by making objects and actions visible, incremental, and reversible [10, 20]. Recent LLM interfaces apply this principle to generative text and media. DirectGPT enables users to manipulate generated text through localized editing operations rather than repeatedly formulating full natural-language prompts [14]. PromptCanvas provides a composable workspace in which prompt components and task facets can be represented as dynamic widgets [2]. Malleable Prompting turns user-authored preference expressions into sliders, toggles, dropdowns, and other controls, allowing users to adjust preferences after they have been expressed in language [25]. Dynamic Prompt Middleware similarly generates contextual prompt-refinement controls to help users refine AI responses [5]. Malleable Prompting is the closest prior work to ours in its goal of turning prompt-level preferences into manipulable interface controls: it takes preferences that users have already expressed in natural language and reifies them as widgets that can be adjusted after the fact [25]. DimSteer differs in the source, timing, and mechanism of control. Its sliders are not derived from user-stated preferences, but from variation the model already exhibits across sampled completions for 4 Ajit Mallavarapu and Ziwei Gu the current prompt; they are surfaced before the user has named the relevant dimension; and moving a slider steers internal activations rather than rewriting or parameterizing the prompt. More broadly, prior widget-based systems show that direct manipulation can make specified preferences easier to adjust. In systems such as PromptCanvas and Dynamic Prompt Middleware, controls are generally derived from task context, prompt components, designer choices, or LLM-generated prompt refinements [2, 5]. DimSteer instead studies whether writers can recognize and use automatically discovered, prompt-local controls during revision. Semantic sliders have also been explored in other generative domains. AdaptiveSliders, for example, supports user-aligned slider-based editing for text-to-image generation [11]. Like these systems, DimSteer uses continuous controls to support exploration. Unlike them, it focuses on LLM authoring and studies how writers interpret and use automatically discovered prompt-local controls during text revision. 

2.3 Preference Elicitation and Recognition over Recall 

DimSteer also builds on work showing that user preferences are often constructed through interaction rather than retrieved fully formed. Slovic argues that preferences are frequently assembled in context, shaped by the options and tradeoffs people encounter [21]. Related work on preference elicitation shows that interactive systems can surface considerations users did not initially articulate [12]. This perspective is especially relevant for LLM authoring: a writer may not know in advance that they want a message to be more direct, warmer, less deferential, or more vivid, but may recognize that preference once the dimension is made visible. This connects to the classic HCI principle of recognition over recall [16]. Prompt-only interfaces require users to recall and formulate the relevant revision instruction before they can act on it. DimSteer instead presents candidate dimensions of variation as inspectable controls. The central question is therefore not only whether users can manipulate known preferences more easily, but whether automatically surfaced dimensions help them recognize preferences they had not explicitly named. 

2.4 Activation Steering and Representation-Level Control 

DimSteer uses activation-space interventions as the mechanism for applying discovered controls. Prior work has shown that model behavior can be influenced by modifying internal representations without updating model weights. Activation Addition demonstrates that adding activation differences associated with natural-language prompts can steer model behavior [23]. Representation Engineering frames internal representations as manipulable objects for transparency and control [26]. Contrastive Activation Addition constructs steering directions from paired examples that contrast a desired behavior with an undesired one [19]. Recent steerable chat systems further show that activation steering can support interactive user-facing control [3]. Most activation-steering methods begin with a target concept specified by a researcher, developer, or user, such as sentiment, truthfulness, refusal, or another named behavior [19, 23, 26]. DimSteer differs by using the model’s own prompt-local variation to propose candidate controls. For a given prompt, the system samples multiple completions, extracts internal activations, performs PCA over the resulting variation cloud, and surfaces the top axes as interface controls. This makes the system less dependent on a predefined vocabulary of controllable attributes. At the same time, discovered axes should not be treated as ground-truth semantic variables. Prior work on the linear representation hypothesis suggests that some model properties can be represented and manipulated along approximately linear directions [18], while other work shows that language-model features are not always one-dimensionally linear or disentangled [7]. Farquhar et al. caution that unsupervised representation-discovery methods can recover salient DimSteer: Steering LLM Authoring with Automatically Discovered Stylistic Controls 5features without uniquely identifying the intended semantic property [8]. Sparse autoencoder methods offer one route toward more localized and interpretable features [4], but typically require training a feature dictionary and selecting which features matter for a task. DimSteer therefore treats each discovered direction as an interface hypothesis : a candidate control that is prompt-local, approximate, and subject to user interpretation. The interface pairs labels with pole previews, diff views, variance tags, reset controls, and custom dimensions so that users can inspect, test, ignore, or repair discovered controls during authoring. 

3 DimSteer 

DimSteer is an LLM authoring interface that surfaces automatically discovered dimensions of generation variation as interactive slider controls. Designed for revision-oriented writing tasks, DimSteer enables users to fine-tune stylistic, rhetorical, or tonal properties of an existing draft through direct manipulation rather than repeated prompt engineering. The central design premise of DimSteer is that discovered controls represent interface hypotheses —prompt-local handles on observed patterns of variation among candidate generations. Because these handles may occasionally be mislabeled, redundant, or subtle, the interface provides continuous visual feedback, inspection tools, and repair pathways.

3.1 Design Goals 

The design of DimSteer responds to a recurring tension in LLM authoring: natural language is expressive, but users may not know how to name the stylistic change they want until they see alternatives. We therefore drew on HCI work on recognition over recall, direct manipulation, sensemaking, and recoverable human-AI interaction to design controls that are visible and easy to test, but still provisional and correctable. 

G1: Support recognition over recall. Prompt-based revision asks writers to recall and formulate the right instruction before they can act. However, HCI heuristics emphasize that interfaces can reduce cognitive burden by making relevant actions visible rather than requiring users to retrieve them from memory [16]. This is especially important when preferences are constructed through interaction rather than fully known in advance [12, 21]. DimSteer therefore surfaces candidate dimensions of variation directly, so writers can recognize potentially useful directions without first articulating them as prompt instructions. 

G2: Enable continuous stylistic adjustment. Many writing preferences are matters of degree: a draft can be slightly more formal, much more concise, or somewhat warmer. Direct manipulation interfaces support this kind of incremental control by making operations visible, continuous, and reversible [10, 20]. DimSteer represents discovered dimensions as sliders so users can explore graded stylistic changes without committing to a full prompt rewrite at each step. 

G3: Preserve comparison across iterations. LLM authoring often involves evaluating several plausible versions rather than choosing between clearly right and wrong outputs. Prior work shows that comparing generated alternatives creates sensemaking burdens, as users must track what changed and infer which action caused the change [9]. DimSteer DimSteer: Steering LLM Authoring with Automatically Discovered Stylistic Controls 7therefore includes diff-based comparison so users can inspect the textual effect of each adjustment and calibrate whether a discovered control behaved as expected. 

G4: Support recovery from imperfect controls. Because DimSteer’s controls are automatically discovered, they may be mislabeled, entangled, redundant, or irrelevant to a user’s goal. Human-AI interaction guidelines emphasize that users should be able to understand, correct, and recover from imperfect system behavior [1]. DimSteer therefore treats discovered controls as provisional: users can ignore them, reset them, reinterpret them through pole previews and diffs, or supplement them with manually specified custom dimensions. 

3.2 Authoring Workflow 

A typical session begins with an initial draft and a panel of discovered controls, each initialized to a neutral baseline. Users inspect the draft, review slider labels and pole previews, adjust one or more controls, and compare the revised draft against the baseline using the diff view. They can then continue adjusting, reset controls, use prompt-based instructions, define a custom dimension, or accept the draft. This workflow complements natural-language prompting: instruction-based rewrites handle structural or factual updates, while sliders facilitate rapid exploration of stylistic and rhetorical nuance. 

3.3 Discovered Slider Cards & Pole Previews 

Rather than requiring users to manually name controls, DimSteer automatically extracts dimensions based on how the model’s own outputs vary for the active prompt. Each discovered slider card combines a short dimension label, a centered bidirectional slider, a qualitative variance tag, and pole previews showing examples from each end of the axis. The variance tag indicates how prominent the component was in the sampled variation cloud; it is not a confidence score. Pole previews help users interpret ambiguous labels by showing the concrete textual effect of the control for the current prompt. 

3.4 Draft-Level Steering & Diff-Based Comparison 

When sliders are adjusted, DimSteer applies global activation interventions to the draft generation. Adjusting multiple sliders simultaneously blends the respective steering directions, enabling writers to navigate stylistic trade-offs (e.g., increasing Warmth while decreasing Length ). To reduce cognitive load during evaluation, DimSteer features a diff view highlighting added, deleted, and modified text. This view aids general editing while enabling users to verify whether a slider intervention produced the intended outcome without introducing unintended side effects. 

3.5 Recovery Pathways & Matched Baseline 

To handle scenarios where automated discovery yields insufficient or noisy dimensions, DimSteer supports three recovery pathways: (1) Bypassing/Resetting: Irrelevant controls can be ignored or reset to zero with one click. (2) Direct Prompting: Users can issue natural-language edit instructions alongside active slider states. (3) Custom Controls: Writers can manually enter a target attribute (Figure 3C) to synthesize an on-demand steering vector. 8 Ajit Mallavarapu and Ziwei Gu

4.1 System Architecture & Model Hosting 

The prototype used a React/Vite frontend connected to a live Python/FastAPI backend running in Google Colab on an NVIDIA T4 GPU. The Colab backend hosted Qwen3.5-4B for generation and steering, exposed REST-style endpoints for prompt generation, axis discovery, and steering, and was made reachable to the local frontend through a LocalTunnel URL. Both the prompt-only baseline and DimSteer used this same backend and target model, so differences between conditions reflect the interaction mechanism rather than model capability. The frontend sent user prompts to the backend, displayed returned drafts and discovered controls, and issued steering requests when users adjusted sliders. 4.2 Activation Harvesting & PCA Engine 

For each user prompt, DimSteer samples 𝑁 = 30 stochastic completions from the target model to form a prompt-local variation cloud. For each completion, we extract hidden states from transformer layer 21 over the generated-token window and mean-pool them into a single completion-level activation vector. This produces an activation matrix 

𝐴 ∈ R𝑁 ×𝑑 , where each row summarizes one sampled completion. We mean-center this matrix and apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) across completions, retaining the top three components as candidate prompt-local steering axes before labeling and deduplication. The variance explained by each component is surfaced in the interface as a descriptive prominence cue, not as a confidence score or guarantee of semantic usefulness. These axes should therefore be interpreted as approximate interface controls rather than ground-truth semantic factors. This discovery procedure is motivated by prior validation of the same sampling-based PCA approach on Qwen3.5-4B [13]. In that evaluation, eight annotators provided 245 unconstrained stylistic-control requests across 20 prompts; the top two discovered axes achieved 72.8% precision and 43.6% macro-recall against these human-requested dimensions at a semantic matching threshold of 𝜏 = 0.65. A separate polar-validity evaluation found that 75.6% of human ratings judged the polar generations accurate to their labels, with 90.9% adjacent agreement. We use these results as evidence that prompt-local activation variance can surface human-interpretable candidate controls; in this paper, we study how such controls function in a live authoring interface. Sampling parameters and layer-selection details are provided in Appendices A.1 and A.3. 

4.3 Auto-Labeling & Polarity Alignment Pipeline 

Because the sign of a PCA direction is mathematically arbitrary, the system must ground each axis in representative text to make the directions semantically meaningful to the user. For each principal component, we project the 𝑁 sampled generations onto the axis and sort them by projection score. We select exemplar generations from the positive and negative ends of this projection distribution and pass these pole examples to Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct in a single structured judging prompt. The judge compares the two poles and returns a structured JSON-style object with a concise label, a polarity judgment, a coherence score, and a short rationale: 

{"label": "<one- or two-word label>", "positive_pole_has_more_label": <true or false>, "coherence": <numeric score>, "rationale": "<one short sentence>" }

The label field is used as the slider name. The positive_pole_has_more_label field resolves the arbitrary sign of the PCA direction: if it is false, we flip the axis vector and swap the pole examples so that positive slider movement corresponds to increasing the labeled property. 4.4 Axis Deduplication 

To reduce redundant controls, DimSteer filters near-duplicate axis labels after labeling and polarity alignment. Labels are normalized by lowercasing and removing whitespace and punctuation; if two labels share a contiguous character sequence of at least four characters, the system keeps the higher-variance axis and removes the lower-variance duplicate. This lightweight heuristic prevents the interface from showing multiple similarly named sliders that offer little additional user value. 

4.5 Custom Dimension Creation 

Custom dimensions used Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) rather than PCA. Given a user-entered attribute, Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct helped operationalize the request by generating positive and negative contrastive descriptions for the current prompt. The backend ran Qwen3.5-4B on these contrastive conditions, extracted transformer layer-21 activations over the generated-token window, and used the normalized mean positive-minus-negative activation difference as the steering vector. The resulting CAA direction was exposed through the same slider interface and coefficient range 𝛼 ∈ [− 1.5, +1.5], providing a repair path when PCA-discovered axes did not cover a desired control.

4.7 Frontend Engineering & Interface Details 

The frontend is built using React and Vite, focusing on a seamless transition between prompt drafting and slider manipulation. Because each unseen steering configuration requires a backend inference request, rapid slider movements could overwhelm the backend. Slider movements were debounced before being sent to the FastAPI backend, reducing redundant steering requests during rapid interaction. The frontend also cached completed steering results within a session: if a participant returned to a previously viewed slider configuration, the interface restored the cached draft rather than issuing another backend request. Unseen slider configurations still triggered live steering requests. 5 User Study 

To evaluate DimSteer, we conducted a within-subjects laboratory study comparing auto-discovered continuous steering controls against a standard natural-language prompting baseline. The evaluation focused on three questions: whether DimSteer reduces perceived effort while preserving participants’ ability to shape generated text (RQ1), whether auto-discovered controls surface desired dimensions that users may not have articulated themselves (RQ2), and how slider-based steering affects participants’ sense of control, exploration, and preference relative to open-ended prompting (RQ3). The user study was conducted under an approved IRB. 

5.1 Participants and Apparatus 

We recruited 16 participants: 8 female and 8 male. Ages ranged from 18 to 54: 11 were 18–24, 4 were 25–34, and 1 was 45–54. All participants reported prior experience with writing prompts for AI tools: 6 were somewhat experienced, 6 were experienced, and 4 were very experienced. Participants also reported frequent use of AI writing tools: 13 used AI tools for writing or editing daily, and 3 used them a few times per week. All 16 participants completed all study tasks. Participants were compensated $20 for completing the study. The study compared two interfaces using the same live backend and the same underlying model, Qwen3.5-4B. Interface A was a standard chat-based prompting interface in which participants revised drafts through natural-language instructions. Interface B was DimSteer, which presented auto-discovered continuous controls for modifying the generated draft along prompt-local dimensions of variation. Because both interfaces used the same model and backend, differences between conditions reflect the interaction mechanism rather than differences in model capability. The apparatus consisted of a local React/Vite frontend connected to a Python/FastAPI backend running in Google Colab. The backend was exposed to the frontend through a LocalTunnel URL. The backend supported prompt-local sampling, activation extraction, PCA, labeling, polarity alignment, duplicate-axis filtering, and activation steering. During the study, the four shared prompts used the same backend-discovered axes across participants to hold the control set constant, while participant-specific prompts used axes generated for that participant’s prompt. Slider adjustments then triggered live steering requests to the same backend. Each participant completed the same set of tasks in both interfaces: four shared prompts covering descriptive writing and workplace communication, plus one participant-specific open-ended prompt. The participant-specific prompt was held constant across both interfaces for that participant. This design allowed us to compare interaction mechanisms within participants while keeping the writing goal fixed across systems.

5.3 Measures and Analysis 

After each interface block, participants completed a questionnaire using 7-point Likert scales. Workload items asked about mental demand, physical demand, time pressure, perceived success, effort, and frustration. Additional interface DimSteer: Steering LLM Authoring with Automatically Discovered Stylistic Controls 13 items asked whether the interface made it easy to explore options, supported creativity, provided control over style, produced expected changes when adjusted, and helped participants match the text to what they had in mind. For DimSteer, participants also rated whether slider labels accurately described the changes they made, whether sliders changed only what their labels described, whether the surfaced dimensions were things they wanted to adjust, and whether they would have thought to ask for the same style changes in a written prompt. To complement subjective workload ratings, we summarized interaction logs from each task. Logs captured task duration, revision actions, diff-view use, and custom-dimension use. In 𝐶 𝑃𝑅𝑂𝑀𝑃𝑇 , revision actions corresponded to submitted natural-language reprompts; in 𝐶 𝑆𝑇 𝐸𝐸𝑅 , they corresponded to committed slider adjustments. We use these process measures descriptively to contextualize perceived effort and to examine whether lower workload ratings were accompanied by differences in revision behavior. After completing both conditions, participants answered forced-choice comparison questions and open-ended questions about what they liked, what felt limiting, and what features were missing. Because responses were collected on ordinal scales in a within-subjects design, we analyzed paired condition differences using exact Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and report medians, ranges, and exact 𝑝 -values. For DimSteer-specific items, we used exact one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against the neutral midpoint of 4. For forced-choice comparisons, we used exact binomial tests against chance. We treat the interaction logs as descriptive behavioral evidence rather than inferential outcomes.

5.4.4 Supplementary Control-Source Evaluation. To probe whether DimSteer’s PCA-discovered controls differed from controls a general-purpose LLM could propose, we ran a small blinded label-only comparison with eight additional participants. For each of the four shared prompts, participants saw two unlabeled three-control sets: the PCA-discovered controls used in DimSteer and a GPT5.6-luna-generated set of stylistic, tonal, or rhetorical controls for the same prompt [17]. GPT5.6-luna [17] is a publicly available commercial chat model that was accessible on a free tier at the time of the study. Descriptively, across 32 set-level judgments, PCA-discovered sets were selected 21 times (65.6%) and GPT5.6-luna-generated sets 11 times (34.4%). Individual dimensions from both sources were often judged useful (PCA: 78/96, 81.3%; GPT5.6-luna: 81/96, 84.4%), but PCA dimensions were more often useful-but-not-anticipated (28/96, 29.2%) than GPT5.6-luna dimensions (16/96, 16.7%). Excluding labels shared by both sources, source-unique PCA dimensions were useful-but-not-anticipated in 28/64 judgments (43.8%), compared with 16/64 (25.0%) for source-unique GPT5.6-luna dimensions. Because this was a label-only comparison rather than an interactive slider baseline, we treat it as exploratory. 

6 Discussion 

Our study suggests that prompt-local steering controls are most valuable not because they replace language, but because they change what users must do cognitively during revision. In the prompt-only baseline, participants had to inspect a draft, decide what was wrong, translate that judgment into a prompt, and then evaluate whether the resulting revision matched their intent. In DimSteer, the system instead externalized candidate dimensions of variation as manipulable controls. This shift reduced perceived mental demand, effort, frustration, and task time while preserving comparable perceived success. At the same time, participants’ divided responses on control and own-writing preference complicate a simple “sliders are better than prompting” interpretation. Below, we discuss four implications for LLM authoring interfaces. 

6.1 From Prompt Engineering to Recognition-Based Control 

Our results reinforce prior work showing that effective LLM use depends not only on model capability, but also on whether users can express what they want [22, 24]. In writing, that expression problem is often stylistic: users may sense that a draft feels wrong without knowing how to name the desired change. DimSteer addresses this problem by surfacing prompt-local dimensions of generation variation as candidate controls. Participants’ responses support this recognition-over-recall framing. All 16 participants rated the surfaced dimensions as things they wanted to adjust, while 15 of 16 disagreed that they would have thought to ask for the same changes in a DimSteer: Steering LLM Authoring with Automatically Discovered Stylistic Controls 17 written prompt. This suggests that the relevant preference was often recognized after being made visible rather than recalled in advance. The result aligns with work on constructed preferences and interactive elicitation, which argues that preferences are often assembled through interaction with available options [12, 21]. This finding also clarifies the value of prompt-locality. Unlike fixed tone controls or widgets derived from already-stated preferences, DimSteer surfaces dimensions from the model’s own variation for the current prompt. A workplace announcement may expose Formality or Enthusiasm , while a descriptive task may expose Vividness , Sensory detail, or Atmosphere . This makes the controls adaptive to the current writing situation. However, prompt-locality is also a constraint: a useful dimension for one prompt may not appear for another, and the same label may behave differently across tasks. Interfaces should therefore present prompt-local controls as contextual hypotheses rather than universal model attributes. 

6.2 Effort Reduction and Perceived Control Are Different Outcomes 

The study separates two outcomes that are often conflated: reducing effort and increasing perceived control. DimSteer clearly reduced effort. Participants reported lower mental demand, effort, and frustration; interaction logs showed shorter task durations; and 14 of 16 participants selected Interface B as requiring less effort. Qualitative comments echoed this result: participants described the sliders as reducing the need to think through and phrase each desired change. However, this effort reduction did not translate into uniform preference for DimSteer as the more controllable system. Perceived control was divided, with 9 of 16 participants selecting Interface B and 7 selecting Interface A. Participants were also evenly split on which interface they would rather use for their own writing. These results suggest that participants distinguished between low-effort adjustment and open-ended expressive freedom. This distinction is important for LLM interface design. Sliders make available adjustments fast, visible, and reversible, consistent with classic direct-manipulation principles [10, 20]. But language remains better suited for structural, factual, idiosyncratic, or open-ended changes. Several participants explicitly asked for a mix of both interfaces. Because the full DimSteer prototype supports prompting, but the study constrained 𝐶 𝑆𝑇 𝐸𝐸𝑅 to focus on sliders, these comments should be interpreted as support for the intended hybrid workflow rather than as evidence that prompting should be removed. Future authoring systems should let users reprompt while preserving useful slider states, compose prompt edits with active steering settings, and compare versions across both modes. 

6.3 Discovered Controls Must Be Inspectable and Repairable 

A central design choice in DimSteer is to treat discovered axes as interface hypotheses rather than precise semantic knobs. Participants generally found slider labels descriptive and adjustments predictable, but they were more moderate about whether sliders changed only the labeled attribute. This reflects a known limitation of activation-space steering: useful linear directions can still be entangled with length, specificity, emotional tone, formatting, or other properties [7, 18]. Our findings show how this technical limitation appears at the interface level. Users can often make sense of a discovered control, but they still need ways to inspect what it actually changed. Thirteen of 16 participants used the diff view during 𝐶 𝑆𝑇 𝐸𝐸𝑅 tasks, and 9 kept it open throughout their slider-based tasks. This suggests that participants did not treat labels as self-explanatory; they actively checked the textual consequences of steering. Because DimSteer performs draft-level rather than span-level steering, this comparison support is especially important: a single slider movement can subtly alter multiple parts of the output. 18 Ajit Mallavarapu and Ziwei Gu Repair mechanisms were also important. Five participants used the custom-dimension feature, indicating that discovered controls sometimes failed to cover the dimension a participant wanted. Pole previews, variance tags, diff views, reset controls, and custom CAA-based dimensions are therefore not secondary interface features. They are what make imperfect machine-discovered controls usable. More broadly, controllable generation systems should combine steering mechanisms with accountability mechanisms that let users see, test, reverse, and repair the effects of control. 

6.4 Implications for Design and Evaluation 

Our findings suggest several implications for controllable LLM authoring systems. First, interfaces should support recognition before articulation. Users may recognize useful stylistic directions only after they are surfaced, so the prompt should not be treated as the only site where intent can be expressed. Second, discovered controls should be presented as provisional and inspectable. Labels approximate patterns of model variation; they should be paired with examples, diffs, reset controls, and other feedback that lets users judge whether the control is useful for the current task. Third, prompting should remain available as an expressive escape hatch. Sliders reduce effort for available dimensions, but users still need language for precise, structural, factual, or idiosyncratic revisions. Fourth, interfaces should communicate prompt-locality: a control discovered for one prompt should not be implied to be a stable global attribute of the model. These findings also have implications for evaluation. A single preference or output-quality measure would miss the tradeoff we observed. DimSteer reduced effort and supported recognition, yet did not dominate prompt-only interaction on perceived control or own-writing preference. Future evaluations of controllable LLM interfaces should therefore measure effort, recognition, interpretability, perceived control, recovery from mismatch, and final output quality separately. Our supplementary label-only comparison with GPT5.6-luna-generated controls provides an initial check against one publicly available LLM-suggested control source, suggesting that PCA-discovered dimensions may contribute more useful-but-not-anticipated controls. However, future evaluations should still include full interactive component baselines when possible, such as fixed designer-authored sliders, user-specified sliders, LLM-suggested sliders, or Malleable Prompting-style widgets. Thus, the supplementary comparison narrows but does not eliminate the need for component-level interactive evaluations. Overall, DimSteer illustrates how prompt-local steering controls can reduce the burden of articulating every stylistic revision in advance. The goal is not to replace prompting, but to combine language with direct manipulation so that writers can discover, inspect, and steer useful dimensions of variation as they work. 

7 Limitations and Future Work 

This study has several limitations. First, it involved 16 participants and short-form writing tasks, so the findings should be interpreted as evidence about interaction patterns rather than broad claims about all LLM authoring contexts. Future work should evaluate longer documents, professional writing settings, collaborative writing, and a wider range of genres. Second, the 𝐶 𝑆𝑇 𝐸𝐸𝑅 condition constrained participants to focus on sliders even though the full DimSteer prototype supports prompt-based editing. This design isolated slider-based steering for the study, but it made the condition less naturalistic and likely reduced perceived open-ended control relative to the intended hybrid prompt-and-slider workflow. Third, the prototype relied on a live Colab-hosted backend exposed through LocalTunnel. This made the study more representative of actual end-to-end interaction than a precomputed demo, but it also introduced deployment DimSteer: Steering LLM Authoring with Automatically Discovered Stylistic Controls 19 constraints: GPU availability, Colab session stability, tunnel reliability, and backend queueing could affect latency and responsiveness. Future deployments should use more stable serving infrastructure and report end-to-end latency for generation, discovery, labeling, and steering separately. Fourth, the main study compared DimSteer’s discovered sliders against a matched prompt-only baseline. We chose this baseline because prompt-only chat remains the dominant interaction pattern for LLM writing, and our goal was to test whether adding prompt-local direct-manipulation controls changes that experience. Our supplementary control-source evaluation provides an initial blinded, label-only comparison with GPT5.6-luna-generated controls, but it did not test a full interactive interface with the same slider UI and different control sources. A stronger component-level evaluation would compare DimSteer against interactive baselines using fixed designer-authored dimensions, user-specified dimensions, LLM-suggested dimensions, or Malleable Prompting-style widgets. Our results therefore support claims about DimSteer as an integrated discovered-control authoring interface relative to current prompt-only practice, while leaving open how much of the benefit comes specifically from the discovery mechanism versus slider-based direct manipulation more broadly. Finally, the current prototype uses PCA-discovered directions from one target model and applies draft-level steering at a fixed layer. PCA identifies prominent directions of activation variance, not guaranteed disentangled semantic factors, and participants’ ratings suggest that sliders sometimes affected properties beyond their labels. Future work should evaluate additional models, compare PCA with other steering-direction discovery methods, audit label and polarity accuracy with independent raters, and assess output quality, content preservation, and long-range coherence with blinded evaluations. 

8 Conclusion 

We presented DimSteer, an LLM authoring interface that turns prompt-local variation in model generations into interactive steering controls. Rather than requiring writers to fully specify stylistic preferences through repeated prompting, DimSteer surfaces candidate dimensions that users can inspect, manipulate, reset, and supplement. In a within-subjects study with 16 participants, DimSteer reduced perceived mental demand, effort, and frustration relative to a matched prompt-only baseline while preserving comparable perceived success. Participants also identified the surfaced dimensions as changes they wanted, even though most reported they would not have thought to ask for those changes in a prompt. Interaction logs further suggested that slider-based revision reduced the time cost of each iteration. At the same time, participants continued to value natural-language prompting for open-ended control. The broader design implication is therefore not that sliders should replace prompting, but that LLM authoring interfaces should combine the flexibility of language with the immediacy of direct manipulation. DimSteer illustrates one path toward hybrid systems that help writers discover, inspect, and steer useful dimensions of variation that might otherwise remain unarticulated.

Ethics and Privacy Statement 

This work studies interfaces for controllable LLM-based writing. Participants completed writing, revision, and control-preference tasks and provided survey responses and open-ended feedback about their experience. We report results in aggregate and use anonymized quotes; no participant names or identifying information are included in the paper. The system is intended to increase user agency by making stylistic variation inspectable and adjustable. However, more controllable style steering could also be misused to produce manipulative, deceptive, or impersonated text. We therefore frame DimSteer as an authoring and revision aid, emphasize transparency through labels, pole previews, diff views, and reset controls, and discuss the need for future deployments to include safeguards appropriate to their writing context. The study evaluated a bounded research prototype with fixed study tasks and constrained steering coefficients. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence about interaction design and user experience, not as an endorsement of unrestricted style manipulation in high-stakes or deceptive communication settings.

A Technical Details of Dimension Discovery and Steering 

This appendix provides additional implementation details for the prompt-local dimension discovery, labeling, and steering pipeline described in Section 4. 

A.1 Sampling Parameters 

For reproducibility, the discovery pipeline sampled 𝑁 = 30 completions per prompt using nucleus sampling with temperature 𝑇 = 0.9, top-𝑝 = 0.95, and a maximum length of 500 new tokens. 

A.2 Prior validation of axis discovery. 

Prior work evaluated the same sampling-based PCA discovery procedure on Qwen3.5-4B over 20 prompts with eight annotators, who produced 245 free-text “magic slider” requests [13]. Top-two axes achieved 72.8% precision and 43.6% macro-recall at 𝜏 = 0.65; because only two axes were evaluated, the estimated recall ceiling was 51.7%. Pole generations had median validity 4/5, 75.6% top-two-box accuracy, 57.5% exact agreement, and 90.9% adjacent agreement. Across nine seeds, axes had 82.5% top-two rediscovery; a text-only PCA baseline reached 38.4% precision and 21.2% recall. 

A.3 Layer Sweep Calibration 

To choose a fixed intervention layer for the prototype, we ran a pilot sweep over candidate transformer layers 

{12 , 16 , 18 , 21 , 24 , 25 }. The sweep used four calibration prompts spanning descriptive writing and workplace communi-cation tasks. For each layer and prompt, we ran the discovery pipeline, retained the top three PCA axes, and generated paired steered outputs at 𝛼 = −1.5 and 𝛼 = +1.5, yielding 72 layer–prompt–axis configurations. 22 Ajit Mallavarapu and Ziwei Gu We evaluated these configurations with Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct as an automated judge. For each configuration, the judge was shown the original prompt, the discovered axis label, and the two steered outputs. The judge assessed whether the two outputs formed a coherent contrast along the labeled dimension, whether each output preserved the original task, whether the outputs remained fluent, and whether they contained major artifacts such as reasoning leakage, prompt continuation, unnecessary constraint insertion, multiple-option answers when not requested, or task failure. The sweep did not identify a single layer that was uniformly optimal across all prompts and axes. Instead, usable steering was concentrated in a mid-to-late band: layers 18–24 produced the most consistent trade-off between visible steering movement and preservation of fluent, task-adherent outputs. Earlier and later candidates were more prone to either weaker steering or output instability under the same coefficient range. We therefore selected layer 21 as a representative fixed layer within this high-performing band, rather than optimizing the layer separately for each prompt or dimension. We treat this as an implementation calibration for Qwen3.5-4B, not as evidence that layer 21 is universally optimal across models, prompts, or steering methods. 

A.4 Dimension Labeling and Polarity Alignment

For each discovered principal component v𝑘 , we projected the 𝑁 sampled generations onto the axis and selected representative examples from the negative and positive ends of the projection distribution. These examples were passed to Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct [6] as pole examples for labeling and polarity alignment.

A.5 Interactive Steering Mechanism Calibration 

To determine the usable parameter ranges for activation-space interventions, we calibrated the steering coefficient through pilot sweeps over 𝛼 ∈ {− 3, −2.5, −2, −1.5, −1, −0.75 , −0.5, 0, 0.5, 0.75 , 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3}. Based on qualitative inspec-tion of output fluency and task adherence, we established 𝛼 ∈ [− 1.5, 1.5] as the conservative slider boundary used in the live prototype. 

A.6 Supplementary Control-Source Evaluation 

We conducted a small blinded supplementary evaluation comparing PCA-discovered controls with GPT5.6-luna-generated controls for the four shared study prompts. GPT5.6-luna is a publicly available commercial chat model accessible on a free tier at the time of the study. It was used only to generate the supplementary LLM-suggested control baseline and was not part of the DimSteer discovery, labeling, or steering pipeline. Eight additional participants completed all four prompt evaluations. We did not collect demographic information for this supplementary evaluation. For each prompt, participants saw two unlabeled three-control sets, presented as Set A and Set B. One set contained the PCA-discovered controls used in DimSteer; the other contained three stylistic, tonal, or rhetorical controls generated by GPT5.6-luna for the same prompt. Source position varied across tasks: for Tasks 1 and 3, Set A was PCA and Set B was GPT5.6-luna; for Tasks 2 and 4, Set A was GPT5.6-luna and Set B was PCA. The GPT5.6-luna controls were generated with a fixed prompt asking the model to propose exactly three useful controls for revising the writing task: 

> Given the following writing task, propose exactly three useful controls that a writer might want while revising the model’s output. Each control should be a one- or two-word stylistic, tonal, or rhetorical dimension. Avoid content-specific nouns. Avoid redundant controls. Return only the three labels as a comma-separated list. Writing task: [task]

Participants first selected which complete set of controls they would rather have while revising the text. They then selected which individual dimensions they considered useful, and which dimensions they believed they would have independently thought to ask an AI writing assistant to adjust before seeing the list. For prompts where both sources produced the same label, the shared dimension was displayed once in the individual-dimension questions but counted once for each source in source-attributed summaries. Across 32 set-level judgments, PCA-discovered sets were selected 21 times (65.6%) and GPT5.6-luna-generated sets 11 times (34.4%); no participant selected “no preference.” Five of eight participants selected the PCA set more often, one selected the GPT5.6-luna set more often, and two selected the two sources equally often. At the individual-dimension level, PCA-discovered dimensions were selected as useful in 78 of 96 source-attributed judgments (81.3%), while GPT5.6-luna-generated dimensions were selected in 81 of 96 judgments (84.4%). PCA-discovered dimensions were independently anticipated in 50 of 96 judgments (52.1%), compared with 65 of 96 judgments (67.7%) for GPT5.6-luna-generated dimensions. Consequently, PCA-discovered dimensions were more often useful-but-not-anticipated: 28 of 96 judgments (29.2%) versus 16 of 96 judgments (16.7%) for GPT5.6-luna-generated dimensions. Because Tasks 3 and 4 included controls shared by both methods, we also computed a source-unique summary excluding shared labels. Among 64 source-unique judgments per source, PCA-discovered dimensions were useful in 46 24 Ajit Mallavarapu and Ziwei Gu judgments (71.9%), independently anticipated in 18 (28.1%), and useful-but-not-anticipated in 28 (43.8%). GPT5.6-luna-generated dimensions were useful in 49 judgments (76.6%), independently anticipated in 33 (51.6%), and useful-but-not-anticipated in 16 (25.0%). These results suggest a difference in the character of the two control sources. GPT5.6-luna-generated controls were slightly more often judged useful overall and more often independently anticipated, suggesting that they captured familiar revision dimensions. PCA-discovered controls were more often useful-but-not-anticipated, suggesting that prompt-local activation variation can surface less obvious but still valuable controls. Because this was a small label-only evaluation rather than an interactive slider condition, we treat it as exploratory.
