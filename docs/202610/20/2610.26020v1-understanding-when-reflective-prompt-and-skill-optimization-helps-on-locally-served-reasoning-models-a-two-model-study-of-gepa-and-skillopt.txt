Abstract 

Reflective text-space optimizers such as GEPA and SkillOpt have reported large gains on frontier and mid-size models. We test replication across two reasoning-native target-serving configurations: DeepSeek V4 Flash through DwarfStar and Qwen3.8-27B through LM Studio. For each target, we evaluate the same held-out tasks and four conditions - minimal baseline, GEPA prompt, SkillOpt skill, and manually engineered prompt - on HotpotQA ( n = 500 per condition) and MATH-500 ( n = 250 ). In the primary Qwen v2 rerun under the same frozen protocol, GEPA selects the HotpotQA seed and exactly reproduces baseline behavior, whereas SkillOpt’s validation gain reverses into held-out degradation. On MATH-500, GEPA raises exact match from 0.828 to 0.904 while reducing latency but increasing token consumption, so lower response time does not imply lower token cost. DeepSeek likewise shows its strongest GEPA effect on MATH-500 (0.852 to 0.908) . Across both targets, GEPA’s strongest gains occur on MATH-500, while neither optimizer improves the HotpotQA baseline. Optimization is therefore task- and method-dependent, and validation gains may reverse on held-out evaluation. An earlier Qwen run is retained as serving-sensitivity evidence. These results suggest that textual headroom for a model-task pair and the observed effect of a text-space optimizer are more informative when decomposed into content/reasoning, answer-realization/evaluator alignment, and serving-completion/reliability. The cross-benchmark pattern further suggests that optimization may benefit from low-ambiguity, actionable evaluation signals. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models are increasingly adapted to downstream tasks without touching their weights: by evolving prompts [6, 9, 19], by compiling multi-stage programs [11, 13], or by training external textual artifacts such as skill documents [20]. Two recent systems stand out for pushing this text-space adaptation furthest. GEPA [1] evolves prompts through natural-language reflection on execution traces and Pareto-based candidate selection, reporting gains over reinforcement-learning post-training with a fraction of the rollouts. SkillOpt [20] treats a Markdown skill file as the sole trainable parameter of a frozen agent, accepting bounded edits only when they strictly improve a held-out validation score, and reports best-or-tied results across 52 (model, benchmark, harness) cells. 1arXiv:2610.26020v1 [cs.AI] 19 Oct 2026 Both papers evaluate mostly on hosted frontier or mid-size models. Meanwhile, reasoning-native open-weight models can be served through substantially different local stacks. We study DeepSeek V4 Flash [2] through DwarfStar (ds4) [16] and Qwen3.8-27B [14] through LM Studio on Apple Silicon. This setting changes the economics of text-space optimization, but it also exposes an under-studied interaction: an artifact may change not only the correctness of completed answers but also whether a request completes under a particular serving stack. We study this question through controlled within-target comparisons and a protocol-preserving cross-target replication that preserves datasets, splits, framework seed, conditions, metrics, and optimizer implementations. Because the serving stacks necessarily differ, the design supports paired within-model effects and cross-model replication analysis, not a pooled claim that model identity alone causes every observed difference. Our research questions are: • RQ1. Which GEPA and SkillOpt effects replicate across two reasoning-native target models under paired held-out evaluation? • RQ2. How do content and reasoning, answer realization and evaluator alignment, and serving completion and reliability distinguish observed text-space optimization effects? • RQ3. How strongly do optimizer effects vary across target-serving configurations, and how do reliability and condition order affect their interpretation? • RQ4. How reliable are validation- and selection-gated acceptance criteria at the set sizes used here? 

Contributions. (i) A configuration-driven benchmarking framework that runs official GEPA and SkillOpt implementations against any OpenAI-compatible endpoint, with deterministic scoring, incremental persistence, endpoint failover, and a statistical layer (bootstrap CIs, paired permu-tation tests). (ii) Controlled within-target comparisons and a protocol-preserving cross-target replication spanning two models, two benchmarks, and four conditions, with 6,000 held-out task-condition observations in total. (iii) A three-layer framework distinguishing content/reasoning, answer-realization/evaluator alignment, and serving-completion/reliability, supported by response-completion and success-conditional analyses. (iv) Cross-model interaction and multiplicity analyses showing HotpotQA GEPA non-improvement across two configurations, including an exact Qwen no-intervention outcome, task-consistent MATH GEPA gains, and method-dependent SkillOpt transfer. (v) Selection-noise and optimization-trajectory analyses showing why small adaptive validation gains should be treated as diagnostics, not deployment guarantees. 

# 2 Related Work 

Reflective prompt and skill optimization. GEPA [1] samples system-level trajectories, reflects on them in natural language, and maintains a Pareto frontier of prompt candidates; it outperforms the GRPO reinforcement-learning baseline by up to 20% with up to 35 × fewer rollouts, and also beats MIPROv2 [13]. SkillOpt [20] is, to our knowledge, the first systematic text-space optimizer for agent skills: a separate optimizer model turns scored rollouts into bounded add/delete/replace edits on a single skill document, gated by strict improvement on a held-out selection set, with a textual learning-rate budget and a rejected-edit buffer. Both are used here through their official open-source implementations. 2FLARE [17] uses error-focused reflection grounded in few-shot references; its authors report gains over GEPA across their evaluated task–model pairs, including HotpotQA, although its retrieval-aware metric and experimental stack preclude direct numerical comparison. WikiSkill [18] co-evolves executable skills and persistent wiki knowledge while separating immutable experience, accumulated knowledge, and active skills. It provides related methodological evidence rather than a HotpotQA replication. 

Prompt-optimization lineage. OPRO [19] casts the LLM itself as an optimizer over prompt space; PromptBreeder [6] and EvoPrompt [9] apply evolutionary search; TextGrad [22] back-propagates natural-language “gradients” through compound systems; DSPy [11] compiles declarative pipelines and optimizes their prompts jointly (MIPROv2). Several of these methods report particularly large gains when the initial prompt is weak or when substantial task-specific improvement remains available — a pattern consistent with the headroom perspective studied here. 

Reasoning models may reduce the value of explicit prompt engineering. Both targets expose a reasoning mode before the visible answer. Our HotpotQA results (§6.2) show on two models that several behaviors typically induced through extractive-QA prompting — short-answer discipline, span-copying, and multi-hop decomposition — are already competitive under a minimal instruction. This is evidence about the tested model–task pairs, not about reasoning models as a class. 

Validation-set overfitting. Our selection-noise analysis is an instance of the classic adaptive overfitting problem: repeatedly evaluating candidates on the same small set and keeping the best inflates the apparent score [15]. The phenomenon is formalized in the adaptive data analysis literature [4], and the reusable holdout [3] was proposed precisely to preserve validity under repeated adaptive querying of a holdout set. The statistical machinery we use is standard: nonparametric bootstrap [5] and permutation tests in the Fisher tradition [7]. 

Agentic design patterns. The Reflection and Learning-and-Adaptation patterns that GEPA and SkillOpt instantiate are surveyed in Gulli [8]; we cite it as background on the design space, not as a source on either system. 

# 3 Background: GEPA and SkillOpt 

GEPA. GEPA (Genetic-Pareto) [1] maintains a pool of candidate prompts for the components of an AI system. Each iteration: (1) select a parent candidate from the Pareto frontier of per-task validation scores; (2) roll out the system on a minibatch of training tasks; (3) have a reflection model read the full trajectories (inputs, reasoning, outputs, per-task feedback) and propose a revised prompt in natural language; (4) evaluate the proposal on the minibatch, and, if it improves, on the full validation set; (5) add it to the pool. The per-task (rather than aggregate) Pareto criterion preserves candidates that win on different task subsets. The final artifact is the candidate with the best full-validation score. The budget is expressed in metric calls (single-task evaluations). 

SkillOpt. SkillOpt [20] trains a Markdown skill document that is prepended to a frozen target model’s context. Each step: (1) roll out the target with the current skill on a training batch; (2) an 

analyst / optimizer model reflects on failures and successes and emits bounded add/delete/replace edits (at most the textual learning rate per step); (3) the edited skill is evaluated on a held-out 

selection set ; (4) the edit is accepted only if the selection score strictly improves over the best so 3far, otherwise it enters a rejected-edit buffer that discourages re-proposal. Deployment adds zero optimizer calls: only the final best_skill.md travels with the model. Both methods rely, at selection time, on the assumption that improvements measured on their validation or selection data are informative about unseen examples. Our study examines that assumption directly. 

# 4 Benchmark Framework and Methodology 

4.1 Architecture 

The framework (Python, no heavyweight numerical dependencies) is organized in five layers, connected as in Figure 1: 1. Adapter layer. A common interface over targets; here an OpenAI-compatible adapter with connection validation, per-call timeout control, and automatic dual-NIC endpoint failover (if the primary base URL stops answering, the adapter pings the alternates and retries once on the first reachable one). 2. Optimizer runners. Thin integrations that run the official gepa and skillopt packages against any adapter target, with archived run directories so that repeated optimizations never silently resume stale state. 3. Evaluation engine. Deterministic per-task scoring (§4.3), incremental JSONL persistence with resume support, and a circuit breaker that skips a target after repeated consecutive connection errors instead of accumulating spurious failures. 4. Results store and reporting. Per-task rows ( runs.jsonl ), aggregated CSVs with bootstrap CIs, paired-delta CSVs with permutation p-values, and an HTML report. 5. Configuration layer. A single YAML file defines targets, conditions, datasets, optimizer budgets, and scoring; adding a model or a condition is configuration, not code. Optimized artifacts are versioned with manifests recording seed, budgets, reflection model, validation scores, and convergence traces; every number in this paper is traceable to one of those files. 

4.2 The four-condition design 

Both benchmarks compare four conditions on identical task sets: • A (baseline): a deliberately minimal one-line seed prompt (e.g. “Solve the math problem. Give the final answer inside \boxed{} .”). • B (GEPA): the best prompt found by official GEPA, seeded from the minimal prompt. • C (SkillOpt): the minimal prompt plus the best skill document trained by official SkillOpt, seeded from a minimal one-line skill. • D (manual): a manually engineered prompt written with standard prompt-engineering practice, used as-is. 4Figure 1: Framework pipeline. A single YAML configuration drives an optimization phase (top: optimizer runners calling the reflection model and emitting versioned artifacts) and an evaluation phase (bottom: deterministic scoring of the four conditions feeding the statistics and reporting layer) against one frozen target at a time; all target traffic passes through one OpenAI-compatible adapter with timeout, retry, and configured dual-NIC failover (see §4 for the role of each layer). The diagram’s abbreviated “Qwen 3.8” label denotes the Qwen3.8-27B target. Accordingly, C–A estimates the deployed SkillOpt-condition effect relative to the minimal baseline; it need not isolate accepted edits because condition C also includes the skill wrapper even when the optimizer accepts no change. The minimal seed is a design choice, not an oversight: in a pilot with a hand-tuned seed (§6.7), neither optimizer identified an improvement over the curated seed, providing little opportunity to study their behavior when substantial textual-context changes remain possible. Seeding from a minimal prompt increases the opportunity for both methods to modify the textual context; condition D controls for the alternative of simply paying a human to write the prompt. 

4.3 Metrics and scoring 

Scoring is fully deterministic (no LLM judge). For HotpotQA we use SQuAD-style normalization (lowercase, strip articles and punctuation, whitespace tokenization) and compute exact match 

EM ∈ { 0, 1} and token-level F1 

F1 = 2P R P + R , P = |pred ∩ gold ||pred | , R = |pred ∩ gold ||gold | .

For MATH-500 we extract the last \boxed{...} expression (with a fallback to a “final answer” pattern), normalize LaTeX (fraction shorthand, \text{} wrappers, currency and percent symbols, spacing), and score exact match of the normalized answers, with a numeric-equality fallback at tolerance 10 −6.Each task also receives a format-compliance score f (HotpotQA: a non-empty answer of at most 120 characters; MATH: a parseable boxed answer) and a robustness score r (1 if the call succeeded, 

0 on error). The framework’s composite score is the fixed convex combination 

s = 0 .45 q + 0 .20 f + 0 .20 c + 0 .15 r, (1) 5where quality q is F1 (HotpotQA) or EM (MATH-500) and the exact-match/correctness component 

c is EM. On MATH-500, q and c are not independent: both contain exact-match information, so the fixed composite intentionally gives benchmark correctness a combined weight of 0.65 . The composite score is a framework-level operational metric rather than a benchmark-standard metric. Accordingly, benchmark-native quality metrics — F1/EM for HotpotQA and EM for MATH-500 — are treated as the primary measures of task performance; the composite is reported as a complementary measure that also captures format compliance and call robustness. The weights in Eq. 1 were fixed at framework-design time, before the reported test evaluation, and were not tuned on test performance; their purpose is to provide a common operational score across tasks rather than to replace benchmark-native metrics. 

4.4 Headroom 

Let sbase be the baseline score of a (model, task-distribution, serving-stack) configuration and smax 

its practical ceiling : the best end-to-end score reachable by varying only textual context while weights, decoding parameters, and scoring remain fixed. Define the headroom 

H = smax − sbase . (2) Any text-space optimizer’s true gain is bounded by H. Importantly, smax , and therefore H, is generally unobservable in a finite experiment: observing a better prompt establishes only a lower bound on the available headroom. Headroom should therefore be interpreted here as a conceptual property of the (model, task, scoring) configuration rather than as a directly estimated latent quantity. Signals consistent with H ≈ 0 are nonetheless observable: a strong baseline, hand-tuned prompts that fail to improve, and optimizer validation gains that do not transfer. The replication motivates three analytically distinct layers. Content/ reasoning headroom 

concerns substantive correctness among usable responses: did the artifact help solve content pre-viously answered incorrectly? Answer-realization/evaluator-alignment headroom concerns convert-ing available content into a complete, parseable, appropriately formatted final answer. Serving-completion/reliability headroom concerns whether an assigned request completes and yields a response under the realized serving conditions. These are interpretive layers rather than separately identified causal latent variables, and the primary end-to-end estimand can mix them. HotpotQA shows limited measurable improvement from the evaluated procedures on either target. DeepSeek MATH primarily exhibits an answer-realization/evaluator-alignment difference. Qwen v2 MATH instead shows an outcome-level correctness gain among jointly successful calls at matched baseline/GEPA completion; sequential timing and the 12 recorded timeouts remain descriptive serving limitations rather than an explanation for that gain.

Per-condition means are reported with nonparametric bootstrap 95% confidence intervals [5] (re-sampling tasks, ≥5,000 replicates). Condition comparisons use a paired sign-permutation test [7]: for conditions X, Y evaluated on the same tasks, the null hypothesis is that the per-task deltas 

dt = sY (t) − sX (t) are symmetric around zero; we randomly flip the signs of the nonzero deltas ( 10 4

permutations) and report the two-sided tail probability of the observed sum. Pairing on identical task sets removes between-task variance, which dominates in QA/math benchmarks, and thus gives far more power than unpaired comparisons at the same n.All primary hypothesis tests compare each optimized condition with the minimal baseline on the benchmark-native quality metric: B vs. A and C vs. A on F1 for HotpotQA, and B vs. A and C vs. A on EM for MATH-500. Other pairwise comparisons are treated as exploratory and reported with unadjusted p-values unless otherwise stated. As a familywise sensitivity analysis, we apply Holm’s procedure to the eight primary B–A and C–A tests across two models and two benchmarks. We also estimate exploratory model-by-condition interactions as differences between the within-model paired effects, using task-level bootstrap intervals and sign-permutation tests. These interactions mix target-model and serving-stack differences and are interpreted as heterogeneity, not as the causal effect of model identity. For Qwen MATH, task-level intervals and permutation p-values are conditional on the realized serving trajectory and do not capture uncertainty induced by temporally correlated endpoint failures. 

# 5 Experimental Setup 

5.1 Datasets and splits 

HotpotQA [21]: multi-hop QA in the distractor setting (10 paragraphs, 2 gold + 8 distractors). Examples are drawn from the official development set of the distractor setting — the Hugging-Face hotpot_qa dataset, config distractor , split validation , containing 7,405 examples (file 

hotpot_dev_distractor_v1 ) — before deterministic shuffling and partitioning. MATH-500 [12]: the 500-problem competition-math subset of MATH [10] popularized by Lightman et al. [12], from 

HuggingFaceH4/MATH-500 . Both are shuffled with a fixed seed (42) and split sequentially: train 150 / validation 100 / test 500 (HotpotQA) and train 150 / validation 100 / test 250 (MATH-500). Thus the “test” subsets reported in this paper refer to our held-out partitions of these datasets, not to additional official benchmark splits. Sequential slicing guarantees that enlarging the test set never touches train/val. GEPA uses the train split for reflection minibatches and the validation split for candidate scoring; SkillOpt uses train for rollouts and a selection subset of the validation split (HotpotQA 100, MATH 50) for gating. The test split was not used for optimizer candidate selection or artifact construction.

6.8 Qualitative analysis of the optimized artifacts 

The winning GEPA MATH prompt (full text in §A) is a study in metric alignment rather than mathematical insight. Its content: output only the boxed answer (associated with lower observed latency); preserve exact values over decimals ( 12\pi , not 37.7); wrap multiple-choice answers as 

\boxed{\text{(C)}} exactly as the gold labels do; a handful of domain reminders (cylindrical-coordinate surfaces, a determinant–area identity). GEPA discovered, from execution traces alone, which formatting conventions the scorer rewards — legitimate gains under the benchmark’s own rules, and a caution for anyone reading EM deltas as pure reasoning improvements. 

Item-level audit of the MATH-500 gain. To characterize what the +5 .6 EM actually consists of, we audited every test item whose EM changed between conditions A and B (Table 7). Of 250 paired items, 209 are correct under both conditions and 19 wrong under both; 18 flip from wrong to correct and 4 from correct to wrong (net +14 , i.e. +5 .6 points). Inspecting the 18 improvements: in 12 cases the baseline produced no parseable \boxed{} answer: each call exhausted the 4,096-token output budget and the retained visible response is empty, so its underlying reasoning cannot be independently inspected. GEPA’s boxed-only rule yielded a clean final answer. In the remaining 6 cases the baseline’s boxed value was mathematically equivalent to the gold answer but formatted differently than the scorer expects (e.g. 106 vs. 106ˆ\circ , E vs. \text{(E)} , a coordinate tuple vs. a pmatrix ). The retained audit supplied no observed case of GEPA correcting demonstrably incorrect mathematical reasoning. Conversely, 3 of the 4 regressions are cases where GEPA’s answer was missing or truncated, and 1 is a genuinely different (wrong) value. The observed EM gain is therefore dominated by answer-completion and format alignment rather than by new mathematical capability — consistent with the latency and token-count reductions. 17 0 5 10 15 20 25 30 35

Qwen v2 reruns both optimization and held-out evaluation from the frozen protocol in a separate namespace. # 7 Discussion 

HotpotQA exposes both an exact no-intervention outcome and a transfer reversal. 

Across two target-serving configurations, neither optimizer improves the one-line HotpotQA baseline. Qwen v2 GEPA is the cleanest no-intervention case: the optimizer selects the seed, so the deployed 22 intervention is exactly zero. SkillOpt is different. Its Qwen v2 validation improvement is large, yet held-out F1 falls significantly; DeepSeek also exhibits a harmful accepted edit. These outcomes do not establish that SkillOpt generally overfits, but they show that an optimization-time success and a useful deployed intervention are distinct. 

Apparent gains occupy three distinct layers. DeepSeek MATH primarily exhibits answer-realization/evaluator-alignment improvement: with successful calls in both A and B, the obtained GEPA artifact increases benchmark EM by enforcing a complete, benchmark-compatible final answer. Qwen v2 GEPA also improves correctness among jointly successful calls, with only one timeout in each condition, so its end-to-end gain is not a completion-rate artifact. The retained records support an outcome-level gain, not a claim that hidden reasoning itself improved. Content/reasoning, answer-realization/evaluator alignment, serving completion, latency, and token economy all matter to deployed utility, but they support different scientific claims. 

Evaluator actionability may matter in addition to headroom. Both benchmarks use deterministic scoring, so their cross-benchmark difference cannot be attributed simply to evaluator determinism. MATH-500 instead provides a comparatively low-ambiguity relationship among a problem, a canonical final answer, and exact-match feedback. This can make some evaluation failures actionable for text-space optimization: an artifact can encode reusable constraints concerning answer completion, exact form, canonical representation, and output discipline. This interpretation is especially consistent with the DeepSeek MATH GEPA audit, where the +5 .6-point EM difference is attributable primarily to answer realization and evaluator alignment rather than newly corrected mathematical reasoning. HotpotQA scoring is also deterministic, but semantically reasonable answers may vary lexically from the reference span, and changes in qualifiers, verbosity, or phrasing can alter EM/F1 without a corresponding change in substantive correctness. The harmful DeepSeek HotpotQA SkillOpt edit illustrates the risk: the realized artifact’s emphasis on qualifiers and full phrases passed the selection gate but was associated with longer answers and lower held-out F1. Because similar completeness instructions also occur in the broader GEPA prompt, this result does not isolate that rule as the cause. The observed pattern is therefore consistent with the hypothesis that optimization depends not only on available headroom but also on evaluator actionability—the extent to which feedback provides a low-ambiguity signal that can be translated into generalizable textual interventions. Because benchmark and task structure, optimizer budget, gate metric, and selection-set size differ here, the study does not isolate evaluator actionability causally. 

Optimizer effects depend on task, method, and trajectory. No optimizer is uniformly superior. GEPA produces the strongest MATH-500 condition for both targets, but selects no new Qwen HotpotQA intervention. SkillOpt improves Qwen MATH modestly while degrading Qwen and DeepSeek HotpotQA. One optimization trajectory per cell cannot distinguish an algorithm-level tendency from search variance, so the appropriate unit of conclusion is the observed optimizer–target– task run. 

Selection-noise estimates are diagnostics, not gates. Repeated evaluation on n ∈ [50 , 100] 

can make several-point improvements unsurprising under a centered empirical null. The DeepSeek runs illustrate a useful warning: three development gains fail to transfer and one accepted edit is harmful. Qwen v2 makes the warning sharper: HotpotQA SkillOpt records a large selection gain but a significant held-out loss, whereas MATH GEPA transfers positively. Practical deployment therefore requires evaluation data untouched by optimization, explicit failure accounting, and replication across 23 optimizer seeds and time blocks; independent confirmation additionally requires a fresh external or study-level holdout. A small gate gain alone is insufficient. 

Implications for benchmark practice. Reports should separate at least five quantities: assigned-task end-to-end score, completion rate, correctness among usable responses, answer-realization failures, and latency both over assigned tasks and successful calls. Randomizing or interleaving conditions over time would reduce association between prompt and transient server state. Where failures remain, paired status-transition tables make the source of aggregate differences auditable. These practices matter especially for slow local inference, where a timeout can dominate benchmark outcomes and optimization budgets span days. 

# 8 Limitations 

Two target models remain a small sample, and each uses a different engine, quantization, and serving host. Within each experiment all conditions target the same checkpoint and stack, but cross-target interactions combine model and system differences. Both benchmarks are English, single-turn, and deterministically scored; agentic and tool-use settings remain untested. A single reflection model (Claude Opus 4.8) is used throughout, and a different reflector may change search efficiency. The two benchmark configurations differ in optimizer budget, gate metric, and selection-set size. Cross-benchmark contrasts therefore motivate the headroom and evaluator-actionability hypotheses but do not constitute a controlled causal test of either. For MATH-500, the exact-match evaluator includes normalization and numeric equivalence but not full symbolic equivalence. DeepSeek GEPA’s net gain is attributable to answer completion and format alignment rather than newly solved mathematics. Qwen v2 conditions were evaluated sequentially rather than randomized or interleaved. The run completed without resume, circuit-breaker recovery, or endpoint substitution, but 12 assigned calls timed out (1/1/9/1 under A/B/C/D). Task-level confidence intervals and permutation tests are therefore conditional on the realized serving trajectory and do not model uncertainty from temporally correlated server load. The earlier Qwen execution required recovery and endpoint substitution; it is retained as sensitivity evidence rather than merged into v2. Qwen v2 reused the study’s previously evaluated test identifiers. Those items were excluded from v2 optimization and frozen before v2 evaluation, but v2 is not an independent confirmation on unseen study-level test data. Moreover, its primary designation is post hoc and based on operational completeness. Because three optimized artifacts changed, v1–v2 differences confound search trajectory, artifact content, and serving state. They should not be read as a pure estimate of serving-stack variance. The success-conditional analysis is also limited. Completion is affected by condition and possibly by problem difficulty, so restricting to jointly successful calls is a post-treatment selection. It is a mechanism-oriented sensitivity analysis, not an unbiased replacement estimand for missing counterfactual responses. Finally, each optimization procedure was executed once (seed 42). The paired tests and bootstrap confidence intervals quantify uncertainty over evaluation examples conditional on the optimized artifact obtained in that run; they do not quantify optimizer-run variance. Multi-seed replication would be required to establish the stability of the optimization outcome across independent searches and is left for future work due to the substantial computational cost of local target-model rollouts. 24 9 Conclusion 

RQ1: The absence of measurable HotpotQA optimizer improvement under the evaluated procedures replicates across the two target-serving configurations. Qwen v2 strengthens this conclusion for GEPA through an exact no-intervention result, while SkillOpt’s accepted edit significantly harms held-out F1. RQ2: Textual headroom is most informative when separated into content/reasoning, answer-realization/evaluator alignment, and serving-completion/reliability. The observed DeepSeek MATH difference belongs primarily to the second layer. Qwen v2 MATH GEPA improves correctness with nearly identical completion coverage, while lowering observed response time and increasing token use; these are distinct outcome, latency, and resource trade-offs rather than a single efficiency claim. RQ3: Optimizer effects are target- and trajectory-dependent, while cross-model comparisons are inseparable from serving-stack differences in this study. RQ4: Small adaptive validation gains remain weak evidence of held-out benefit, and selection-noise diagnostics cannot substitute for held-out, failure-aware evaluation. The cross-benchmark pattern further motivates evaluator actionability as a hypothesis for future controlled study: optimization may depend not only on available headroom but also on how directly evaluation feedback can be translated into generalizable textual interventions. Future work should interleave conditions across time, replicate optimizer searches over seeds, evaluate each model across multiple serving stacks, and extend the design to tool-use tasks where skills may offer structural advantages. The immediate recommendation is narrower: before deploy-ment, measure assigned-task performance, response success, correctness among usable responses, format/realization failures, and latency both overall and among successful calls. Do not collapse these mechanisms into a single claim of improved accuracy or reasoning.

# Skill Solve the math problem. Give the final answer inside \boxed{}. ## Verification - After computing an answer, sanity-check it against the diagram and problem constraints before finalizing. - In trisection/subdivision problems, carefully identify HOW MANY of the equal sub-parts lie inside the triangle or region of interest (e.g., a cevian angle may span 2 of 3 trisected parts, not 1). Count the sub-angles from the figure rather than assuming one part each. - For an interior intersection point of cevians, the triangle formed uses the angles 

30 adjacent to that point; recompute using the correct multiples of the base sub-angle. ## Method - Before computing, look for a key structure/identity and pick an efficient method over brute force: special triangles (e.g., 8-15-17 right triangle), tangent addition, sin(2x)=2 sin x cos x telescoping, completing the square, divisibility facts, the Remainder/Factor theorem for polynomial remainders, the cross product for areas/volumes, and binomial coefficients for indistinguishable-object counts. - For nested optimization/game problems, optimize the inner variable first (e.g., vertex of a quadratic), then the outer. - To classify or identify an object (conic type, etc.), reduce the equation to its recognizable standard/canonical form by dividing/multiplying/completing the square, then read off the classification directly (e.g., dividing to \frac{(x-h)^2}{a}+\frac{y^2}{b}=1 identifies an ellipse). - For probability over points placed randomly on a circle (or other symmetric configurations), count favorable cyclic/relative orderings divided by total orderings instead of integrating (e.g., two chords intersect iff the four endpoints alternate). - For f(-x) vs f(x) problems, note even-power terms are unchanged and odd-power terms flip sign: use the given value to cancel the unknown even-power/constant coefficients instead of solving for them. - Exploit invariants/symmetry to avoid solving for every unknown: when a symmetric configuration has a conserved quantity (e.g., for a regular octahedron the sum of squared distances from any point to each pair of OPPOSITE vertices is constant), pair the known values by equal sums to isolate the unknown directly. - For binomial coefficients, apply C(n,k)=C(n,n-k) to switch to the smaller k before computing (e.g., C(31,28)=C(31,3)). - To evaluate an inverse function at a value, set f(x)=that value and solve for x directly; do not derive the full inverse formula. - For word problems, name variables and translate the conditions into a system of equations (e.g., count+value equations, force=mass*acceleration), then solve by substitution/elimination. - When a defining equation/constraint is given (e.g., x^2 - x - 1 = 0), use it to rewrite higher powers and substitute directly into the target expression instead of solving for the variable; the target often collapses to a constant. - On a closed interval, simplify the expression (absolute values often collapse to a linear form), determine whether it is increasing or decreasing, then evaluate at the correct endpoint for the min/max. - For greatest-integer/integer-part problems involving radicals, expand or use conjugates to split into a rational part plus an irrational part, then bound the irrational part by comparing squares of integers. - For terminating-decimal digit-sum problems, scale numerator and denominator so the denominator becomes a power of 10 (multiply top and bottom by 2 or 5 to balance the exponents of 2 and 5), read off the decimal digits, then sum them. - For sum-of-squared-distances from a point P to the vertices, use the identity PA^2+PB^2+PC^2 = GA^2+GB^2+GC^2 + 3*PG^2 (G = centroid); combine with known facts (e.g., G divides GH so PG is a fixed fraction of GH, and OH^2 = 9R^2-(a^2+b^2+c^2)) to collapse to a closed form.
