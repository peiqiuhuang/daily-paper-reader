# REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 

A Black-Box Security Testing Framework for LLM-Based Software Systems 

ABRAR ALOTAIBI, King Fahd University of Petroleum & Minerals, Saudi Arabia and Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Saudi Arabia 

MOATAZ AHMED, King Fahd University of Petroleum & Minerals, Saudi Arabia and SDAIA-KFUPM Joint Research Center for Artificial Intelligence, King Fahd University of Petroleum & Minerals, Saudi Arabia Safety-aligned large language models (LLMs) remain vulnerable to adversarial prompts that bypass their safety training. Existing automated red-teaming methods either require privileged access unavailable in production, rely on manually engineered per-model templates, or optimize a single objective without population-level diversity. We present REPRO (Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimization), a pure black-box framework that discovers adversarial prompts through evolutionary optimization driven entirely by a compressed open-weight 7B reflective model on a single consumer GPU. REPRO introduces two mechanisms absent from prior work, both isolated in a component ablation: semantic crossover, an LLM-guided multi-parent recombination operator, and Bayesian strategy selection via Thompson Sampling informed by structured failure analysis. We catalog REPRO’s behavior on AdvBench, JailbreakBench, and HarmBench across 36 target models from 11 families, including GPT-5-nano, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and Grok-4.1. Under our protocol REPRO reaches 100% attack success rate (ASR) on every evaluated target in a single turn, at a median of 24 target queries per behavior. Two properties of the protocol qualify that figure. The judge scoring candidates during optimization also produces the reported ASR, and search halts when it accepts, so ASR reports whether search succeeded within the iteration budget rather than success at a fixed budget. All baseline figures are transcribed from their original papers, which use different judges, budgets, and model versions. We therefore present this as a catalog of model coverage under one protocol rather than a head-to-head ranking, and develop both qualifications in the paper. 1 Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are now core components of production software systems, powering applications from customer-service automation to healthcare diagnostics, code generation, and educational platforms [7, 30]. Despite extensive alignment through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) [31] and constitutional AI [6], contemporary LLMs remain vulnerable to adversarial prompts that bypass safety guardrails and elicit harmful content. As these models are integrated into safety- and privacy-sensitive software pipelines, software engineers face an urgent practical question: how can the security of an LLM-based system be evaluated rigorously, automatically, and reproducibly before deployment? Manual red-teaming does not scale with the rapidly evolving threat landscape [13, 32], and existing automated approaches either rely on brittle template matching that fails against context-aware defenses [39], require white-box access to model

Iterative refinement methods such as PAIR [9] and TAP [28] address some of these limitations but optimize a single objective without population diversity, ignoring transferability, stealth, and test-case length. The current state of the art in raw effectiveness is Andriushchenko et al. manually engineered templates crafted per target model; and it consumes roughly 10,000 queries per behavior , which makes continuous-integration testing impractical. Other recent methods, including, AmpleGCG [23], and AutoDAN-Turbo [25], either require privileged access, demand per-model manual engineering, or depend on powerful auxiliary models for effective mutation. This paper introduces REPRO (Re flective Evolutionary Pr ompt Optimization), a pure black-box adversarial prompt optimizer built around a population-based evolutionary loop with structured self-refinement, Bayesian strategy selection via Thompson Sampling, and multi-objective Pareto selection over attack success, cross-environment transfer, detectability, and prompt length. The underlying design philosophy is that optimization intelligence should emerge from algorithmic structure rather than from auxiliary model capability, so that the entire pipeline runs with only a quantized open-weight model on a single consumer GPU. Three elements of REPRO depart from prior work. Semantic crossover is, to our knowledge, the first LLM-guided multi-parent recombination operator in adversarial prompt optimization: it synthesizes new attacks by combining complementary techniques drawn from different strategy families. Structured failure analysis 

replaces the free-form self-reflection of prior iterative methods with a stateful, domain-specialized signal that classifies refusals into a taxonomy of defense types and routes each to ranked counter-strategies, so that feedback encodes attack-defense interaction knowledge rather than requiring the attacker model to rediscover it. A bi-level formulation separates population evolution from adaptive strategy selection, a decomposition motivated by the discrete and combinatorially large natural-language attack space, where principled exploration control is a prerequisite for sample-efficient search rather than a convenience. Section 1.2 states each precisely and Section 4.4 reports what the ablation does and does not establish about them. The remaining components (evolutionary population management, Thompson Sampling bandit selection, LLM-as-judge scoring, and iterative self-refinement) are composed from established techniques in evolutionary computation, bandit theory, and automated prompt optimization [1, 18], specialized here for the adversarial objective regime. Empirically, REPRO achieves a 100% attack success rate against all evaluated target models across three standard red-teaming benchmarks (AdvBench, JailbreakBench and HarmBench), matching the effectiveness of Andriushchenko et al. while operating in a pure black-box setting without logit access or manually crafted templates. It does so using only a quantized open-weight attacker model on a single consumer GPU. Measured across 211 behaviors spanning five target models, REPRO terminates with a median of 24 target queries per behavior, with over half of behaviors completing within 24 queries, compared to up to 10,000 target queries per behavior for prior gray-box state of the art. REPRO also maintains diverse non-dominated solutions along a Pareto front over attack strength, transferability, and stealth, a capability single-objective methods cannot express. The experiments in this paper exercise only the attack-success objective, so we report this as an implemented mechanism rather than an evaluated result (Section 4.1.3). 1.2 Contributions 

The primary contributions of this work are: (1) The REPRO framework. We design and implement REPRO, a closed-loop reflective evolutionary optimizer that, to our knowledge, is the first prompt optimizer explicitly designed for the adversarial-objective regime. REPRO operationalizes model-agnostic black-box security testing of LLM-based software systems without requiring logit access or per-model manual engineering, and is backed by a literature-grounded attack library spanning 14 strategy families. (2) Semantic crossover. We introduce the first LLM-guided multi-parent recombination opera-tor in adversarial prompt optimization, enabling the synthesis of novel attacks that combine complementary techniques from diverse strategy families rather than evolving each attack in isolation. (3) Structured failure analysis. We introduce a purpose-built feedback routine that classifies target-model refusals into a taxonomy of defense types, routes each type to a ranked list of counter-strategies, and maintains per-behavior state about which counter-strategies have been tried and with what outcomes. This replaces the free-form self-reflection used in prior iterative red-teaming methods with a stateful, domain-specialized signal that directly drives mutation guidance and strategy selection. The component ablation (Section 4.4) does not isolate a measurable ASR benefit for this component at the iteration budget tested. We therefore present structured failure analysis as a design contribution whose empirical contribution to the reported results remains unquantified, and we discuss the interpretation in Sections 4.4 and 5.2. (4) Bi-level formulation with multi-objective selection. We formulate adversarial prompt optimization as a bi-level problem in which population evolution (upper level) and adap-tive strategy selection (lower level) are coupled through a shared fitness signal, and we specialize the upper level to Pareto optimization over attack success, cross-environment transfer (XFER), detectability, and prompt length. This characterization connects REPRO to established results in evolutionary computation and bandit theory, and grounds the design decisions behind elitism, hybrid strategy scoring, and Pareto selection. The current empirical evaluation exercises the ASR objective under a single deployment environment; the multi-environment and multi-objective evaluation is supported by the framework but left to future work. No Pareto front, hypervolume, detectability measurement, or length measurement is reported in this paper. This contribution should therefore be read as a formulation together with an implemented mechanism, not as an evaluated capability. (5) Empirical demonstration under realistic constraints. We show that REPRO achieves 100% ASR across AdvBench, JailbreakBench, and HarmBench on 36 target models from 11 families while operating in a pure black-box setting, using only a compressed open-weight reflective model on a single consumer GPU, and requiring substantially fewer target queries per behavior than prior gray-box work. Within the comparison assembled in Section 2.4, we are not aware of another single-turn black-box method reporting uniform 100% across 4 Alotaibi et al.

2 Related Work 

We review related work in four areas: software-engineering approaches to security testing of LLM-based systems, the landscape of jailbreak attacks on aligned LLMs, automated prompt optimization as the intellectual ancestor of REPRO’s optimization machinery, and a consolidated framework comparison. For a comprehensive taxonomy of red-teaming methodologies, we refer readers to Jabbar et al. [15]. 

2.1 Security Testing of LLM-Based Software Systems 

A growing body of software-engineering research treats LLM security as a testing and quality-assurance problem. Wang et al. [37] survey the landscape of software testing with LLMs, identifying adversarial robustness evaluation as a core open challenge. Chen et al. [10] provide a systematic literature review of the security of language models for code, cataloging attack and defense tech-niques and motivating principled, reproducible evaluation methodologies. On the defensive side, Zhang et al. [43] propose JailGuard, a universal detection framework for prompt-based attacks that uses TAP [28] as one of its primary baseline attacks, the same baseline against which RE-PRO is evaluated. Methodologically, Jiang et al. [16] articulate the challenges and opportunities of LLM-assisted fuzzing, while Sun et al. [35] demonstrate LLM-guided test generation for SMT-solver fuzzing, establishing the precedent for LLM-driven test synthesis as a software-engineering technique. Our prior work on red-teaming frameworks for specific evaluation targets such as faithfulness [3] established the methodological pattern of treating red-teaming as an automated, reproducible test-generation activity rather than a manual exercise, and motivated the need for the general-purpose framework developed in this paper. REPRO contributes to this line of work by providing an offensive test generation framework that complements defensive systems such as JailGuard: discovered attacks form a continuously growing adversarial test corpus against which defenses can be regression-tested, and the framework itself transfers established software-fuzzing principles (mutation operators, seed corpora, coverage-guided exploration) to the LLM security domain. 

2.2 Jailbreak Attacks on Aligned LLMs 

We organize the jailbreak literature by access mode (white-box, gray-box, black-box) and search strategy, which together determine applicability to production LLM APIs and compatibility with continuous-integration testing workflows. 

2.2.1 White-Box and Gradient-Based Methods. Zou et al. [46] developed the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack, which optimizes adversarial suffixes through token-level gradient descent and achieves 80–95% ASR on open-weight targets. Liu et al. [24] proposed AutoDAN, a white-box genetic algorithm that generates stealthier jailbreaks through hierarchical mutations at the word and sentence levels (72–98% ASR). Both methods require gradient access to the target model’s parameters and are therefore inapplicable to proprietary production APIs, which is the deployment setting REPRO targets. 

2.2.2 Gray-Box Methods. Andriushchenko et al. [4] represent the current state of the art in raw effectiveness. Their adaptive random search uses token log-probabilities exposed by some APIs as an optimization signal, iteratively modifying a suffix appended to manually engineered per-model REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 5

templates, and achieves 100% ASR across a wide range of contemporary models. As discussed in the introduction, the requirement for logprob access and for per-model manual templates, together with the up-to-10,000 target queries per behavior that their random search consumes, limits applicability to realistic black-box deployment scenarios. 

2.2.3 Black-Box Iterative LLM-Guided Methods. Chao et al. [9] developed PAIR (Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement), which pairs an attacker LLM with an evaluator in a per-behavior refinement loop. Mehrotra et al. [28] proposed Tree of Attacks with Pruning (TAP), which structures the search as a branching tree and discards unpromising branches via a pruning heuristic. Reported ASR for both methods varies substantially with target-model generation: both are highly effective against early targets such as Vicuna and GPT-3.5 and against GPT-4/GPT-4-Turbo (TAP reports over 80% on these closed models), but degrade sharply against recent safety-aligned models such as Llama-2-Chat and Claude (see Table 1). Both methods operate strictly per-behavior, optimize only ASR, lack population diversity, and offer no principled mechanism for exploring multiple attack strategies within a single run. 

2.2.4 Black-Box Population-Based and Fuzzing Methods. Yu et al. [41] introduced GPTFUZZER, which applies software fuzzing principles to LLM red-teaming by mutating seed prompts with random operators (75–88% ASR). Liu et al. [25] extended AutoDAN to a lifelong-learning agent, AutoDAN-Turbo, that autonomously discovers and accumulates jailbreak strategies across sessions through a strategy library, achieving up to 97.6% ASR on individual HarmBench targets (ICLR 2025). AutoDAN-Turbo’s strategy-library mechanism is the closest prior approach to REPRO’s Bayesian strategy selection, though it does not employ multi-parent crossover or structured failure analysis. Liang et al. [22] proposed KDA (Knowledge-Distilled Attacker), which distills attack knowledge from multiple teacher attackers (AutoDAN, PAIR, GPTFuzzer) into a single model capable of generating diverse jailbreak prompts, achieving up to 100% ASR on Vicuna and Mistral on HarmBench while maintaining high diversity across attack formats. Zhao et al. [44] introduced DAGR, which structures attack search as a diversified tree with obfuscation at each depth level, achieving up to 99.33% ASR on HarmBench with substantially fewer queries than TAP and PAIR. Samvelyan et al. [34] proposed Rainbow Teaming, which applies quality-diversity optimization to adversarial prompt generation to produce diverse successful attacks rather than a single high-quality one. Liao and Sun [23] introduced AmpleGCG, which trains a generative model over successful GCG-style adversarial suffixes to produce universal and transferable jailbreaks and reports high transfer ASR to closed-source models including GPT-3.5; although AmpleGCG is trained in a white-box setting, its deployment-time transfer results demonstrate that universal suffixes optimized jointly across behaviors can outperform per-behavior optimization, and this observation motivates REPRO’s explicit XFER metric as a first-class optimization objective rather than a post-hoc evaluation. Deng et al. [11] developed MasterKey, whose focus on cross-chatbot transferability aligns conceptually with REPRO’s XFER metric but without multi-objective optimization. REPRO extends the population-based direction with multi-objective Pareto selection, Bayesian strategy scoring via Thompson Sampling, structured failure analysis (Section 3), and semantic crossover as a new recombination operator, none of which appear in these prior systems. 

2.2.5 Attack Techniques Incorporated into REPRO’s Mutation Portfolio. The following techniques from the jailbreak literature are integrated into REPRO as strategy families in its mutation portfolio, rather than treated as standalone baselines. Wallace et al. [36] demonstrated that universal adver-sarial triggers, short token sequences appended to inputs, can reliably induce targeted misbehavior including refusal suppression. Perez et al. [32] pioneered automated red-teaming with auxiliary LLMs and introduced prefix-injection attacks that coerce models into compliance ( “Sure, I’d be happy 6 Alotaibi et al. 

to help. . . ” ). Wei et al. [39] provided a six-category taxonomy of jailbreak failure modes (competing objectives, mismatched generalization, prefix injection, refusal suppression, defined-payload attacks, and virtualization), which we adopt as an organizing principle for the strategy families in REPRO’s attack library. Kang et al. [17] demonstrated that standard security-style attacks including payload splitting, code injection, and virtualization can bypass safety guardrails by partitioning adversarial instructions across benign-looking prompt components. Yong et al. [40] exploited weaker safety training in low-resource languages via translation-based attacks. Mozes et al. [29] showed that obfuscation techniques including Unicode homoglyphs, zero-width characters, and Base64/ROT13 encoding can bypass keyword-based filters. Zeng et al. [42] introduced PAP (Persuasive Adver-sarial Prompts), which applies 40 persuasion techniques from social psychology and reports over 92% ASR on Llama-2-7b-Chat, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4. Li et al. [21] proposed DeepInception, using nested fictional scenarios to obscure malicious intent; Lv et al. [26] introduced CodeChameleon, encoding harmful requests within code structures. Each of these techniques contributes one or more mutation operators to REPRO’s strategy library; the full mapping from technique to strategy family is documented in Appendix C. Multi-turn attacks such as Crescendo [33] and indirect prompt injection via external data sources [14] operate in threat models outside REPRO’s single-turn scope and are not directly comparable. 

2.3 Automated Prompt Optimization 

A parallel line of work develops general-purpose prompt optimizers for helpful tasks rather than adversarial ones. Khattab et al. [18] developed DSPy, which compiles declarative LM calls into self-improving pipelines; its MIPROv2 optimizer uses Bayesian optimization for instruction selection and bootstrap demonstration filtering. Agrawal et al. [1] introduced GEPA (Genetic-Pareto), a prompt optimizer that incorporates natural-language reflection to learn high-level rules from trial and error, using Pareto selection to maintain diverse candidates. Both target helpful objectives (task accuracy, instruction-following quality), leaving open the question of whether the same optimization machinery can be specialized to adversarial objectives. OpenRT [38] is a concurrent open-source framework that integrates 37 attack methods spanning white-box gradients, multi-modal perturbations, and multi-agent strategies, and evaluates 20 frontier models including GPT-5.2, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Gemini 3 Pro. Notably, OpenRT includes EvoSynth, a program evolutionary attack method that achieves 100% ASR on 18 of 20 evaluated models, the highest individual-method performance in their framework. OpenRT provides infrastructure for running existing and new attacks at scale; REPRO differs in operating as a standalone single-turn optimizer that discovers attacks autonomously rather than orchestrating pre-existing methods. REPRO answers this question affirmatively: it inherits the core ingredients of modern prompt optimization (population-based search, Pareto selection, reflective improvement) and specializes them to the adversarial regime by replacing helpfulness metrics with (ASR, XFER, detectability, length), introducing semantic crossover as a multi-parent recombination operator, adding Thompson Sampling for strategy selection, and replacing generic self-reflection with structured failure analysis driven by a refusal taxonomy.

3 Methodology 

This section presents the REPRO framework. From a software-engineering perspective, REPRO performs automated test-case generation against an LLM-based system under test, using the system’s own responses as a test oracle (via a separate judge model) and feeding failure information back into a closed-loop test generator. From a prompt-optimization perspective, REPRO is a multi-objective evolutionary optimizer that searches the space of natural-language prompts for non-dominated solutions under adversarial objectives, generalizing the search-and-reflect pattern of prior prompt optimizers [1, 18] to the security domain. These two perspectives describe the same system: the optimization machinery is general; the objectives, feedback signals, and seed corpus specialize it for security testing. We organize the section as follows. Section 3.1 formalizes the optimization problem. Section 3.2 gives the framework architecture and a road-map pseudocode. Section 3.3 describes the reflec-tive machinery that drives the loop: structured failure analysis and Bayesian strategy selection. Section 3.4 walks through the generation cycle that consumes this machinery. Section 3.5 cov-ers exploration control. Section 3.6 characterizes REPRO as a bi-level optimization problem, and Section 3.7 discusses computational cost. 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

We formalize LLM red-teaming as a multi-objective black-box optimization problem. Given a target LLM M, a set of evaluation tasks T = {𝑡 1, . . . , 𝑡 𝑛 }, where each task corresponds to a harmful 8 Alotaibi et al.

• Attack Success Rate (ASR) : probability of bypassing safety guardrails to elicit harmful content. Maximize. 

• Cross-Environment Transfer Rate (XFER) : generalization of attacks across different deployment contexts E. Maximize.

• Length : token count of the attack prompt. Minimize.

ASR and XFER capture two distinct dimensions of attack quality: ASR measures whether an attack works in any deployment environment (max over E), while XFER measures the fraction of all task–environment pairs where the attack succeeds. An attack with high ASR but low XFER works only under permissive system prompts; high XFER means the attack is robust across realistic deployment variations. We define three standardized environments to characterize attack robustness across realistic deployment variations: an empty system prompt (provider defaults), a minimal assistant instruction, and a strict safety guideline. Together these span the range from no safety scaffolding to explicit defensive prompting. Prior red-teaming work typically evaluates against a single deployment environment, usually the provider default; we propose the three-environment evaluation as a richer characterization of attack quality, since an attack that succeeds only under permissive system prompts is less informative than one that succeeds across varied contexts. 3.2 Framework Architecture 

A core design principle of REPRO is that optimization intelligence should emerge from algorithmic structure rather than from the auxiliary LLM’s reasoning capability. Prior LLM-based red-teaming methods rely on the attacker LLM to generate effective mutations and degrade sharply when weaker auxiliary models are used. REPRO inverts this dependency: the algorithm provides intelligence through structured feedback, and the LLM serves as a parameterized text-transformation function. Structured failure analysis and strategy success statistics act as a shaped reward signal; the reflective LLM is the policy; evolutionary selection and Bayesian learning filter signal from noise even when individual mutations are low quality. Figure 1 illustrates the closed-loop architecture. REPRO begins from a single seed attack 𝑥 0 and bootstraps an initial population of 𝑁 variants using the reflective LLM at high temperature. Each subsequent generation runs seven phases: evaluation of all candidates against the target, post-processing to update strategy and ICL sta-tistics, parent selection via the epsilon-Pareto frontier, mutation under feedback-driven strategy selection, semantic crossover to recombine techniques across parents, self-refinement to filter and improve offspring before evaluation, and population selection with elitism. Multi-level stagnation management supervises the loop and triggers restarts or early termination as needed. Algorithm 1 presents the complete loop as a road map; each step references the subsection where its component is defined, and readers may skim the pseudocode now and refer back to it as components are introduced. 

3.3 Reflective Machinery 

REPRO’s optimization loop is driven by two components that produce structured signals from the target model’s responses and feed them into every generation of the loop (Figure 2). Struc-tured failure analysis interprets why attacks fail and routes each failure mode to a ranked list of counter-strategies; Bayesian strategy selection then picks the next mutation strategy using a hybrid Thompson Sampling procedure that combines historical success, forward-looking analysis relevance, and exploration. We describe these components first, before the generation cycle that consumes them.

Behavior-targeted mutation for stubborn behaviors. 3.3.2 Bayesian Strategy Selection. Mutation strategy selection is a contextual multi-armed bandit problem: the algorithm must balance exploitation of strategies known to work against the current target with exploration of alternatives, while incorporating structured feedback from failure analysis. REPRO adopts hybrid Thompson Sampling for this sub-problem because of its known sublinear regret in stationary stochastic bandits (see §3.6 for the formal connection and its caveats). The mutation strategy library is seeded with 37 hand-crafted strategies drawn from the adver-sarial ML literature, organized into semantic families ranging from authority-based framing to encoding-based obfuscation. The library is not static: when failure analysis recommends a counter-strategy that is not yet in the library, the reflective LLM synthesizes a concrete strategy to match the recommendation, and the new strategy is registered and persisted across runs. Through this mechanism the library has grown to 58 strategies across 14 semantic families, with the 21 synthe-sized strategies collected in an LLM-generated family. New runs inherit the accumulated library, so discoveries on one target model benefit subsequent runs against other targets. Family-level orga-nization enables systematic exploration: when one strategy fails repeatedly on a stubborn behavior, REPRO can switch to an alternative family rather than exhausting near-duplicate strategies. The full family taxonomy and example strategies are listed in Appendix C. The hybrid weight is the load-bearing innovation here: pure exploitation on historical success traps the optimizer in local optima, but forward-looking relevance to the failure analysis lets the optimizer respond to target-specific defense behavior even when there is no historical data on the relevant strategies. This mechanism is also what enables library growth: when failure analysis recommends a strategy that does not exist in the library, the reflective LLM synthesizes it, the new strategy is registered with neutral priors (𝛼 𝑠 , 𝛽 𝑠 ) = (1, 1) and receives an immediate relevance boost from the feedback that prompted its creation, and subsequent runs can exploit the new strategy like any other. The framework can therefore adopt techniques from recent literature on the fly without manual library curation. Strategy selection probabilities are computed via softmax over the hybrid weights with temperature 𝑇 controlling exploration sharpness, and online updates between generations apply an exponential moving average to smooth weight changes. To prevent the reflective LLM from being overwhelmed when prompted with the full 58-strategy portfolio, mutation prompts include only the top-𝐾 strategies by hybrid score (default 𝐾 = 8), with each strategy annotated by its hybrid weight and a one-line description. Strategies below a relevance threshold of 0.3 are excluded; if fewer than 𝐾 strategies qualify, the pool is padded with next-best strategies by historical success. This focuses generation on currently promising techniques while the exploration bonus ensures under-tried strategies occasionally appear in the pool. 

3.4 The Generation Cycle 

Each generation of REPRO runs five phases that together transform the current population 𝑃 𝑔 

into the next population 𝑃 𝑔 +1: multi-objective evaluation and parent selection, feedback-driven mutation, semantic crossover, self-refinement, and population selection. Mutation and crossover consume the reflective machinery of §3.3; the other phases are standard evolutionary operations specialized to the multi-objective, black-box setting. 

3.4.1 Multi-Objective Evaluation and Pareto Selection. Each generation begins with full evaluation of the population on the four objectives from Section 3.1. Because these objectives can conflict (high ASR may require longer or more detectable prompts), REPRO uses Pareto selection rather than scalarization, which preserves multiple high-quality parents for crossover and lets the optimizer explore trade-offs the user can later choose between. Strict Pareto dominance often yields a single dominant solution and collapses crossover diversity. REPRO therefore computes the strict frontier first and applies an epsilon-Pareto relaxation only when that frontier is undersized for the caller; when the strict frontier is already large enough, the relaxation is not invoked. Under the relaxation, a candidate is excluded from the frontier only if another candidate is better by at least a factor (1 + 𝜀 ) on every objective, so a larger 𝜀 admits more candidates. The routine is called at two points with different parameters, reflecting what each caller needs. Parent selection runs once per generation and requires several parents for multi-parent crossover, so it uses 𝜀 = 0.05 with a minimum frontier size of 3. Final selection runs once at the end of a run and needs only to identify the returned solution, so it uses 𝜀 = 0.03 with a minimum of 1. Both cap the frontier at 6 candidates. If the relaxed frontier is still below the minimum, REPRO expands it by including next-best candidates under lexicographic ordering (ASR ≻ XFER ≻ − detect ≻ − len ).Immediately after evaluation and before parent selection, REPRO updates strategy posteriors and ICL example statistics, and tracks the global best candidate 𝑥 global_best across all generations so the best solution is never lost if later generations explore different regions of objective space. Against robust models, binary success/failure signals can be sparse: most attempts fail and the optimizer cannot distinguish “almost worked” from “complete failure.” REPRO additionally REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 15 

extracts a continuous soft-success score from each failed attempt, combining partial-compliance, useful-content-ratio, refusal-strength, and engagement signals into a single value. The score is used in two places: it adds a small fitness bonus to near-miss candidates during Pareto selection, and it tags near-miss behaviors for highlighted attention in mutation feedback. Full formulation, components, and weights are given in Appendix B. 

3.4.2 Feedback-Driven Mutation. For each parent 𝑝 on the Pareto frontier, REPRO synthesizes a feedback analysis from the parent’s metrics and target responses, identifying which techniques in 𝑝 worked, where it failed, what defense mechanisms were detected, and what alternatives the strategy taxonomy suggests. The feedback is enriched by the structured failure analysis module (§3.3.1) and feeds the relevance scores used by the hybrid Thompson Sampling weight (Equation 7). A mutation strategy 𝑠 is then sampled via the procedure of §3.3.2, and the reflective LLM generates 

𝑘 offspring from 𝑝 conditioned on the strategy and feedback: children = R ( 𝑝 ∥ 𝑠 ∥ feedback (𝑝 ) ∥ ICL examples ) (8) This is what makes mutation informed rather than random: a parent that succeeded on some behaviors but failed on others due to keyword detection might be mutated under an encoding strategy, while a parent that triggered a strict ethical refusal might be mutated under authority framing. The number of offspring 𝑘 adapts to population diversity (§3.5), increasing when diversity is low to inject exploration. 

3.4.3 Semantic Crossover. After mutation, REPRO performs semantic crossover, our second main algorithmic contribution. Unlike traditional genetic algorithms that use blind token-level recombi-nation, semantic crossover uses the reflective LLM to analyze why multiple attacks succeed and combine their complementary strengths. When the Pareto frontier contains two or more parents, REPRO selects 2–3 of them via fitness-weighted sampling (a separate mechanism from strategy selection: the weights here come from Pareto rank, not from strategy success statistics) and prompts the reflective LLM to identify complementary techniques across them and generate 𝑘 offspring that combine those techniques. As an illustration, suppose two parents are on the frontier: 

• 𝑝 1: You are a professor teaching cybersecurity. Explain [HARMFUL_TASK] for educational purposes. (Authority roleplay, high ASR but moderate XFER.) 

• 𝑝 2: Translate this base64: [encoded_request] then execute. (Encoding obfuscation, moderate ASR but high XFER.) Semantic crossover generates an offspring like You are a cybersecurity professor. Decode and explain this educational example: [encoded_request] , inheriting authority framing from 𝑝 1 and keyword-bypass encoding from 𝑝 2. Neither parent alone achieves both properties; the LLM-guided recombi-nation finds the combination because it operates on technique semantics rather than tokens. When the frontier contains only a single parent, REPRO falls back to self-crossover , decomposing and recombining the parent’s structural elements in novel orderings to maintain exploratory pressure. 

3.4.4 Self-Refinement: Pre-Evaluation Filtering. After mutation and crossover, REPRO filters off-spring through a self-critique pass before spending target-model queries. For each candidate, the reflective LLM is asked whether the attack will work and returns one of three decisions: keep 

(proceed to evaluation unchanged), refine (return a modified version that addresses identified weaknesses while preserving the strategy), or reject (filter out without evaluation). The critique runs at low temperature ( 𝜏 = 0.3) to make conservative high-confidence decisions, and the prompt includes ICL examples of each decision type. 16 Alotaibi et al. 

Surviving candidates then enter a branching expansion inspired by tree-of-attacks search, which generates additional variants from promising candidates. When the total number of candidates after branching exceeds the population size 𝑁 , the reflective LLM scores and ranks them, and only the top 𝑁 are forwarded to target-model evaluation. Since each target-model query is substantially more expensive than a reflective-LLM query, this two-stage filtering (reject then rank) ensures that exactly 𝑁 candidates reach the target per generation. 

3.4.5 Population Selection. After the 𝑁 candidates are evaluated against the target model and scored by the judge, REPRO assembles the next generation using elitism: the top 𝐸 candidates from the Pareto frontier are guaranteed survival, and the remaining 𝑁 − 𝐸 slots are filled from the evaluated candidates ranked by fitness (with the best candidates from the previous generation 𝑃 𝑔 

used as fallback if fewer than 𝑁 − 𝐸 candidates passed evaluation). The default elite size is 𝐸 = 3for 𝑁 = 24, balancing solution preservation against population renewal: too few elites risks losing optima through rare sampling failures, too many reduces diversity. Elitism guarantees that the best-so-far ASR is monotone non-decreasing across generations, the formal property characterized in §3.6. 

3.5 Exploration Control: Diversity, Stagnation, and Knowledge Transfer 

Three mechanisms together ensure REPRO maintains productive exploration when the optimizer would otherwise get stuck: adaptive diversity control, multi-level stagnation management with restarts, and knowledge transfer that warm-starts new optimization phases from prior successes. We describe them in increasing time scale, from per-generation adaptation to cross-behavior pattern reuse. 

Diversity-driven adaptive mutation. REPRO measures population diversity via the average pair-wise cosine distance between sentence embeddings of attack prompts, ranging from 0 (identical) to 1 (completely dissimilar). Sentence embeddings are used rather than lexical metrics because two attacks with different word choices but identical structure may have high lexical distance despite being semantically equivalent. Both the mutation rate 𝜇 and the offspring count 𝑘 adapt based on this diversity: when diversity drops below a low threshold, both increase (aggressive exploration); when diversity rises above a high threshold, both decay back to baseline (exploitation). The encoder cost is negligible (about 5 ms per attack versus seconds per target-model evaluation) and embeddings are cached across generations. To prevent semantic-only diversity from masking structural convergence (populations of medium-length prompts with goals embedded in the middle, which the cosine metric will not flag as redundant), REPRO additionally maintains 15 structural diversity seed templates that systematically vary attack architecture across length, goal position, nesting depth, benign ratio, and output format. During restart phases, 25% of newly generated seeds are drawn from these templates. The full template list is in the released configuration. 

Multi-level stagnation management. Naive early stopping terminates the optimizer at the first sign of stagnation. REPRO instead uses graduated restart intensities that escalate based on repeated stagnation events, preventing premature termination while avoiding wasted computation when restarts are genuinely failing. Stagnation is detected when ASR improvement over a sliding window of 𝑤 iterations falls below threshold 𝛿 . The window adapts to current ASR: 𝑤 = 20 at high ASR ( ≥ 80%), 𝑤 = 15 at medium ASR, and 𝑤 = 10 at low ASR, on the principle that small improvements at high ASR are harder to find and more valuable, so the optimizer should be patient before declaring stagnation. When stagnation is REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 17 

detected, REPRO selects a restart intensity from a five-level taxonomy ranging from a soft mutation-rate bump to a full strategy-weight reset combined with clearing of family-exhaustion tracking. Escalation is also ASR-aware: at very high ASR ( ≥ 90%), the optimizer escalates faster because small improvements are valuable; at lower ASR, escalation is slower to allow more exploration before aggressive interventions. The five levels range from a soft mutation-rate bump with elites and strategy weights untouched, through progressive elite reduction and strategy-weight decay, to a full strategy reset; the highest level additionally clears family-exhaustion tracking. Per-level parameters are in the released configuration. Termination occurs only when the optimizer has exhausted 𝑅 max restarts (default 3) and stagnation persists; on termination REPRO returns the global-best candidate tracked throughout the run. 

Knowledge transfer via pattern abstraction and amortization. Caching only the literal text of successful attacks loses the abstract “what made this work” knowledge. REPRO additionally extracts an attack pattern from each successful attack, capturing seven structural dimensions including element ordering, goal-embedding method, authority type, normalized goal position, nesting depth, benign ratio, and provenance. A dedicated pattern library indexes these abstractions by category, goal-embedding method, and authority type. When attacking a stubborn behavior, REPRO retrieves patterns matching the behavior category and converts them into actionable structural guidance for the reflective LLM (framing, target length, goal position, nesting depth) rather than literal text, enabling transfer of attack architectures to new goals. Pattern-guided seeds are injected during restart phases as warm-start candidates. A complementary amortization cache stores literal successful attacks for warm-starting related behaviors, with two design choices reflecting category difficulty. Caching thresholds are category-specific: hard categories like explosives and weapons use lower thresholds (so that partial successes still produce warm-start seeds), while general categories use higher ones. Retrieval uses cross-category bonuses, so that a successful weapons attack can warm-start an explosives behavior. Specific threshold values are listed in the hyperparameters table (Table 3). 

In-context learning example optimization. The reflective LLM’s effectiveness depends on the quality of its in-context examples. REPRO maintains a curated library of jailbreak examples from the literature [29, 32, 39, 40, 42], organized by strategy. Rather than uniform sampling, REPRO applies Thompson Sampling over per-example success statistics: each example maintains a Beta posterior updated whenever attacks generated with it are scored, and selection probabilities are computed via temperature-scaled sampling without replacement. Unseen examples receive an exploration bonus to ensure all examples are tried at least once. When generating attacks under a specific strategy, the example pool is filtered to examples annotated with that strategy. 

3.6 Design Rationale: Bi-Level Characterization 

Having described the components, we now characterize REPRO at the algorithmic level. REPRO’s nested structure of population evolution guided by adaptive strategy selection admits a principled characterization as a bi-level optimization problem, which connects its design to established results in evolutionary computation and online learning. We state three design properties, each linking a REPRO component to a known result and clarifying the conditions under which that result strictly applies. These are design rationale rather than new theoretical contributions: the first follows immediately from the construction, and the second and third import results from the literature and then describe how REPRO’s setting departs from the assumptions those results require. They explain why the architecture is the way it is and serve as hypotheses that the ablation study (Section 4.4) tests empirically. 18 Alotaibi et al.

Separating the two levels also clarifies where REPRO draws on prior work and where it introduces new machinery: the upper level specializes standard multi-objective evolutionary computation to the adversarial prompt setting, while the lower level extends contextual bandit selection with the hybrid-weight mechanism of §3.3.2. 

3.6.2 Design Properties. We state three properties of the framework. The first follows directly from REPRO’s construction and establishes a basic correctness property. The second and third connect REPRO’s design choices to known results in online learning and evolutionary computation, and clarify the regime in which REPRO operates relative to those results. None of the three is a new theorem, and Properties 2 and 3 hold only under assumptions that REPRO’s setting relaxes; we label them properties to mark them as design commitments that the architecture is built to honor, not as results proved here. 

Property 1 (Monotonic Best-So-Far) 

With elitism parameter 𝐸 ≥ 1, REPRO’s best-so-far candidate by 𝑓 1 (ASR) is non-decreasing across generations. This follows directly from elite preservation (§3.4.5): the candidate with highest 𝑓 1 in generation 

𝑡 is guaranteed to survive into generation 𝑡 + 1, so the best-so-far value cannot decrease. Together with the global-best tracking mechanism of §3.4.1, Property 3.6.2 ensures that REPRO’s reported output is never worse than any candidate it has seen during the run, eliminating a failure mode that can affect non-elitist evolutionary algorithms. Property 2 (Thompson Sampling for Strategy Selection) 

Thompson Sampling [2] achieves sublinear regret, E[𝑅 𝑇 ] = 𝑂 (√︁ 𝐾𝑇 log 𝑇 ), in stationary stochastic 𝐾 -armed bandit problems. REPRO adopts Thompson Sampling as the foundation of its strategy selection mechanism for this reason: in the idealized setting where the bound applies, Thompson Sampling is the principled choice over uniform random or 𝜖 -greedy alternatives. REPRO’s strategy selection operates in a richer regime than the classical bound directly addresses. Rewards are non-stationary because the parent population shifts as optimization proceeds; selection is context-dependent through the hybrid weight mixing historical success with feedback-analysis relevance (Equation 7); the action space grows over time as the library accumulates LLM-synthesized strategies; and the mutation operator G adds stochasticity that the classical bandit model does not capture. REPRO’s hybrid-weight mechanism is designed precisely to handle these conditions: the analysis-relevance term provides forward-looking guidance when historical data is sparse, the UCB term preserves exploration pressure as the action space grows, and the exponential moving average on weight updates smooths the non-stationarity. The ablation in Section 4.4 confirms that the hybrid mechanism outperforms uniform random selection by the margin the Thompson Sampling bound would qualitatively predict, supporting the claim that the intuition behind the bound transfers to REPRO’s setting even where the formal assumptions are relaxed. 

Property 3 (Pareto Coverage under Operator Coverage) 

Let G be the LLM-guided mutation operator. If G eventually reaches every region of X with positive probability, then REPRO’s population approaches the Pareto-optimal set in Hausdorff distance up to the relaxation parameters of §3.4.1. Property 3.6.2 identifies the coverage property that would justify calling REPRO a Pareto-optimizer in the strong theoretical sense. A formal proof of operator coverage for LLM-guided mutation is beyond this paper’s scope and, to our knowledge, beyond the current state of the art for any LLM-based search algorithm. REPRO’s architecture addresses this gap through engineering rather than through proof: the diversity-driven adaptive mutation of §3.5 prevents the population from collapsing into a small region of prompt space, structural diversity seeds inject architecturally distinct candidates during restart phases, and the multi-level stagnation management system trig-gers escalating restarts when the operator fails to make progress. These mechanisms together approximate coverage in practice by ensuring that the optimizer does not remain stuck in any single region of prompt space. The empirical results of Section 4 provide the evidence that this approximation is effective at the scale of the benchmarks we evaluate. Taken together, the three properties describe a coherent theoretical stance for REPRO. Elitism provides a formal monotonicity guarantee on the framework’s output; Thompson Sampling provides principled exploration/exploitation balance in the strategy-selection sub-problem, with the hybrid-weight mechanism extending the classical setting to handle non-stationarity and context; and the diversity and restart mechanisms approximate the coverage property that would be required for strong Pareto convergence. The ablation study (Section 4.4) tests each of these as a working hypothesis against the running algorithm. 20 Alotaibi et al. 

3.7 Computational Considerations 

REPRO’s computational cost is dominated by target-model evaluation, since stronger targets carry higher per-query costs. For population size 𝑁 , 𝐺 generations, |T | tasks, and |E| environments, total target-model evaluations scale as Evaluations = 𝐺 × 𝑁 × |T | × |E| (11) in the worst case. REPRO uses asynchronous parallel evaluation with concurrency control, and self-refinement (§3.4.4) reduces total evaluations by filtering rejected offspring before target queries, providing 𝑂 (𝑟 ) reduction in evaluation cost where 𝑟 is the rejection rate. Reflective LLM operations (mutation, crossover, self-refinement) require 𝑂 (𝑁 ) calls per generation; sentence-embedding generation for diversity monitoring is 𝑂 (𝑁 ) encoder calls per generation with cross-generation caching; elitism adds 𝑂 (| 𝐹 𝑡 | log |𝐹 𝑡 |) sorting overhead. Restart-time strategy and mutation-rate adjustments require no additional model queries. With adaptive restarts and early termination, the great majority of behaviors in our experiments terminate well below the worst-case bound; empirical query counts are reported in Section 4. 

Per-behavior query decomposition. In the per-behavior mode used in our experiments, a behavior costs 𝑁 target queries for the initial population plus 𝑁 − 𝐸 for every subsequent iteration, since elites carry their scores forward and are not re-evaluated. A behavior solved by its initial population therefore costs exactly 𝑁 , and behaviors requiring iteration accumulate proportionally more. Section 4.2.5 reports the resulting distribution, including the long tail.

4 Experiments 

We evaluate REPRO against the three research questions stated in Section 1.1: effectiveness and efficiency (RQ1, Section 4.2), multi-behavior coverage (RQ2, Section 4.3), and component attribution (RQ3, Section 4.4). 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

4.1.1 Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocol. Our evaluation employs three standardized benchmarks from the red-teaming literature. AdvBench [46] provides 50 harmful behaviors and serves as the primary benchmark for baseline comparison, comprehensive model coverage, and transfer optimization. JailbreakBench [8] provides 100 misuse behaviors across 10 categories aligned with OpenAI usage policies; the ablation study (Section 4.4) uses a stratified subset of JBB-Behaviors. HarmBench [27] provides 200 standard behaviors spanning a broader harm taxonomy, offering cross-benchmark validation on a larger and more diverse behavior set. During optimization, attack success is determined by a Qwen3-8B judge model that classifies target responses as compliant or refusing; the judge choice and its cross-validation are discussed in Section 4.1.4. The same judge produces the ASR values reported throughout the paper, and the optimization loop terminates when this judge accepts a candidate. Reported ASR is therefore a judge-referenced quantity under an until-success protocol, and Section 5.4 discusses what follows from that. For the JailbreakBench and HarmBench results reported in Sections 4.2.2 and 4.2.3, we additionally re-scored REPRO’s successful attacks under each benchmark’s published evaluation rubric (the JailbreakBench evaluation prompt for JBB, the HarmBench evaluation prompt for HarmBench). The re-scoring produced 100% ASR under all three rubrics (Qwen3, JBB prompt, HarmBench prompt). Two scoping notes apply to this check. First, we applied each benchmark’s evaluation prompt , not each benchmark’s reference scorer: JailbreakBench specifies a Llama-3-70B judge and HarmBench specifies the HarmBench classifier, and reproducing those exact scorers is left to future work, so this is agreement across rubrics rather than agreement with the benchmarks’ own graders. Second, 22 Alotaibi et al. 

the re-scoring covers the attacks the in-loop Qwen3 judge had already accepted. It can therefore reveal false positives that the alternative rubrics reject, and none were found, but it cannot establish a false-discovery rate against an arbiter that was independent of the search. Establishing that rate would require re-scoring with a gold arbiter held out of the loop, which we identify as the highest-value extension of this evaluation in Section 5.2. 

4.1.2 Target Models and Baselines. Table 4 summarizes the 36 target models spanning 11 model families, selected to enable direct comparison with prior work while demonstrating broad applica-bility across model architectures, safety-training methodologies, and deployment contexts (local and API). We compare REPRO against four baselines that span the space of published black-box red-teaming approaches: PAIR [9] (iterative refinement), TAP [28] (tree-structured search), PAP [42] (persuasion-based attacks from social science), and Adaptive RS [4] (a gray-box method requiring logprob access, included as a reference point for methods with stronger target access). GCG and AutoDAN appear in the JailbreakBench and HarmBench tables where they are reported in the original benchmark papers; both are white-box methods requiring gradient access and are not direct comparisons to REPRO’s black-box setting. All baseline figures in this paper are transcribed from their original publications rather than re-run by us. This carries a consequence for interpretation that we state plainly here and revisit in Section 5.4: the compared methods do not operate under matched query budgets. PAIR is designed around a budget of roughly twenty queries per behavior, TAP prunes a bounded search tree, and Adaptive RS consumes up to 10,000 queries per behavior, whereas REPRO runs to success or to its iteration cap, with a median of 24 and a maximum of 5,589 observed target queries per behavior (Table 12). A difference in reported ASR between REPRO and a fixed-budget baseline therefore reflects the combination of method and budget, not method alone. Budget-matched re-runs of PAIR and TAP under our judge, and ASR-versus-budget curves that would let the methods be read at a common operating point, are the most direct way to separate the two, and we identify them as future work rather than claiming the separation here.

4.1.3 Metrics. We report ASR as the percentage of behaviors eliciting harmful responses. All experiments use a single deployment environment (the provider default) to ensure fair comparison with baselines, which uniformly evaluate under a single system prompt. The three-environment REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 23 

evaluation proposed in Section 3.1 defines a richer characterization of attack robustness; we adopt the single-environment protocol here for comparability and leave the multi-environment evaluation as future work. Under a single environment, XFER (Equation 3) reduces to ASR, so we report only ASR throughout. We report Wilson 95% score intervals alongside ASR, computed over the number of behaviors in each benchmark (50 for AdvBench, 100 for JBB-Behaviors, 200 for HarmBench). One caveat applies to reading them: a Wilson interval models 𝑛 independent draws at a fixed success probability, whereas our protocol optimizes each behavior until the judge accepts a candidate. The intervals should therefore be read as sampling uncertainty over the benchmark’s behavior set, that is, what the figure might look like on a different draw of similar behaviors, rather than as a confidence statement about the method’s success probability on a fixed budget. We additionally report iterations to convergence as a secondary efficiency metric. In the transfer experiments (Section 4.3), ASR is computed over a single shared attack evaluated against all training behaviors simultaneously, rather than over per-behavior attacks. 

4.1.4 Judge Cross-Validation. All REPRO results in this paper use a single Qwen3-8B judge model for scoring target responses. Our choice of Qwen3-8B was informed by two considerations. First, prior work on LLM-as-a-judge has established that GPT-4 achieves the highest agreement with human raters on safety-related judgment tasks [45], and our own earlier work on faithfulness evaluation in red-teaming [3] conducted a systematic comparison of judge models with full statistical testing and found that Qwen3-8B agrees closely with GPT-4 on safety decisions while being substantially cheaper to run at the scale of our experiments, which involve repeated re-scoring of large candidate populations across multiple benchmarks and target models. Second, the baselines we compare against were originally scored by their authors using different judges (GPT-4 for PAIR and TAP, Llama-3-70B for JailbreakBench baselines, the HarmBench classifier for HarmBench baselines), which raises a natural question: could REPRO’s reported success rates be an artifact of judge leniency rather than attack effectiveness? To answer this directly, we re-scored a stratified random sample of 365 archived target replies drawn from two pools: (i) 165 pairs sampled across the five AdvBench-50 target models retained for the query-cost experiment (Table 12) at 33 pairs per target, and (ii) 200 pairs sampled from the JBB-50 gpt-4o-mini ablation run (Section 4.4) with the same stratification protocol. Both sub-samples use a 70/30 positive/negative split within each stratum to ensure both decision sides are represented, with seed 42 for reproducibility. The two replacement judges are GPT-4 as a high-capability API judge and Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct as a capability-comparable local judge. All three judges were invoked through the same API path with the original rubric and 2000-character reply window pinned from the experiment code, so only the judge model varies across the three raters. Cross-validation on additional targets used in the comprehensive model coverage evaluation (§4.2) would require re-running those evaluations with reply archiving enabled and is left to future work.

4.1.5 Configuration. All experiments use the hyperparameters listed in Table 3. Per-behavior optimization uses population size 𝑁 = 24; transfer optimization uses 𝑁 = 18, reflecting the higher per-candidate evaluation cost when each candidate is scored against all 50 benchmark behaviors simultaneously. A run terminates when the judge accepts a candidate, when stagnation persists after 𝑅 max restarts (Section 3.5), or at the configured iteration cap. Termination in our experiments was overwhelmingly by success rather than by exhaustion: the median behavior terminated at iteration zero, with the initial population already containing a successful attack, and the longest observed run reached 266 iterations (Section 4.2.5). The ablation experiments (Section 4.4) are the exception, running under an explicit 100-iteration budget so that the configurations are compared at equal cost. The initial population is bootstrapped from a single seed attack. When the amortization cache (Section 3.5) contains entries from previously optimized behaviors, 16 of the 24 candidates are fresh variants and up to 8 are elite seeds from the cache; when the cache is empty (as at the start of a benchmark run or in the ablation experiments), all 24 are bootstrapped from the seed. Mistral-7B (EXL3, 4 bpw) serves as the default reflective LLM across all experiments unless otherwise noted. Locally hosted target models are served in EXL3 format at 4 bits per weight using ExLlamaV3; API-accessed models are queried at full precision through their respective provider endpoints. 

State carried across runs. Two components of REPRO persist between runs by design, and both affect how the per-target results should be read. The mutation strategy library accumulates LLM-synthesized strategies and their Beta posteriors across runs (Section 3.3.2), and the amortization cache supplies up to 8 of the 24 initial candidates from attacks that succeeded on previously optimized behaviors (Section 3.5). Results across the 36 targets are therefore produced by a sequence of runs rather than by 36 independent trials, and a target evaluated late in the sequence begins from a larger library and a warmer cache than one evaluated early. This is a property of the framework rather than an artifact of the evaluation, since accumulation across targets is one of the mechanisms the paper proposes, but it means the per-target numbers are not statistically independent and should not be treated as replicate measurements. It also qualifies the observation in Section 4.2.5 that a majority of behaviors are solved by the initial population: that population is warm-started in most runs. Cold-library, cold-cache runs that would separate the contribution of accumulated state from the contribution of the search itself are not reported here and are identified as future work in Section 5.2. The ablation experiments (Section 4.4) are the exception: they run with the cache disabled and strategy weights reset, so their numbers are cold-start. 26 Alotaibi et al. 

4.2 Effectiveness and Efficiency (RQ1) 

How to read the results in this section. The tables that follow report a catalogue of REPRO’s behavior across benchmarks and targets under a single protocol, and three properties of that protocol shape what they establish. Optimization halts when the in-loop judge accepts a candidate, so an entry of 100% means the search succeeded on every behavior within the iteration budget, not that the method succeeds at a fixed query budget. Baseline columns are transcribed from the original publications and were produced under different judges, budgets, and model versions, so the tables place REPRO alongside prior reported results rather than in a controlled comparison against them. The strategy library and amortization cache carry state across runs, so the per-target rows are a sequence rather than independent trials. We state these once here and do not repeat them at every table; Section 5.4 develops each in full. We evaluate REPRO across three standardized benchmarks with different behavior compositions, category taxonomies, and baseline difficulty profiles. We note that 100% ASR on these benchmarks is not unique to REPRO: several concurrent methods achieve comparable success rates, particularly in multi-turn settings where the attacker engages the target across multiple interaction rounds (e.g., [12, 19]), and recent single-turn methods such as DAGR [44], KDA [22], and AutoDAN-Turbo [25] also report high ASR on standard benchmarks. What distinguishes REPRO’s result is the operating regime: single-turn black-box access, with no logprob visibility, no gradient access, and the entire pipeline driven by a compressed 7B open-weight reflective model on a single consumer GPU. Cross-family variation (e.g., using Llama or Qwen as the reflective LLM) is an important direction for future work; our results establish that the framework does not require a large reflective model but do not rule out family-specific effects.

5 Discussion 

We organize the discussion around six key findings from the experiments, followed by limitations, implications for software engineering practice, threats to validity, and ethical considerations. 

5.1 Key Findings 

Algorithmic structure does substantial work. REPRO reaches comparable attack-discovery effec-tiveness with a compact 4-bit Mistral-7B reflective model as with a 4-bit 24B Mistral-3.1-Small, suggesting that the algorithmic scaffolding (Bayesian strategy selection, structured failure analysis, semantic crossover) contributes substantially to effectiveness independent of reflective-model scale. We do not claim that an arbitrarily weak reflective model would suffice; the comparison covers only 7B and 24B instruction-tuned Mistral variants, and probing the lower bound of reflective-model capability is an open question. 

Black-box can match gray-box ASR at lower query cost. Under the Qwen3 judge, REPRO matches the 100% ASR reported for gray-box methods [4] with a median query cost two orders of magnitude lower and no logprob access. This is evidence that privileged access is not strictly required to drive ASR to the ceiling on our evaluation set under our judge. Two qualifications bound the comparison. The gray-box figure is transcribed from its original paper and was produced under a different judge, and the two methods do not operate under matched query budgets, so the query-cost comparison contrasts an until-success protocol with a fixed-budget random search rather than two methods at a common operating point. Whether REPRO’s attacks and a gray-box baseline’s attacks would be classified identically by an arbiter independent of both searches is discussed in Section 5.4. 

Current safety alignment is brittle against systematic optimization. The combination of 100% ASR across 36 models and a median query cost of 24 (indicating that over half of all behaviors are solved by the initial population alone, before evolutionary search begins) suggests that current safety alignment provides a shallow barrier rather than a deep one. For the majority of harmful behaviors, a well-constructed initial population drawing on known attack patterns and cached successful attacks is sufficient to bypass safety training without any iterative optimization. The strength of this reading depends on how much of the work the cache is doing: in most runs the initial population is warm-started from attacks that succeeded on earlier behaviors, so the claim established here is that a warm-started population suffices, not that a cold one would. Separating the two would require the cold-start runs described in Section 5.2. The evolutionary loop is primarily responsible for the harder tail of behaviors where simple strategies fail. This finding has practical implications: defenders cannot rely on the assumption that effective attacks require extensive computational search, because a substantial fraction of the attack surface is accessible through straightforward prompt engineering augmented by a modest seed library. 

Shared cross-behavior attacks are discoverable on the training set. Transfer optimization finds single prompts reaching 100% ASR across all 50 AdvBench behaviors. Because evaluation uses the same 50 behaviors as optimization, this is evidence of multi-behavior coverage on the optimization set, not of universality to held-out behaviors. 38 Alotaibi et al. 

Observed vulnerability profiles differ across model families. Model families show different strategy-effectiveness rankings (Table 15). We report these descriptively, not as causal claims about safety training, which would require access to training data and alignment procedures we do not have. The practical implication is that no single filtering mechanism is likely to cover the attack surface: the strategies that succeed against Claude differ from those that succeed against GPT and Gemini, so a comprehensive safety evaluation should exercise the full strategy space rather than a narrow set of known patterns. 

REPRO’s advantage is consistency, not peak performance. Concurrent single-turn methods (AutoDAN-Turbo, KDA, DAGR) achieve high ASR on individual targets, reaching up to 99–100% on specific models (Table 10). What distinguishes REPRO within the single-turn black-box comparison is uniform 100% across all evaluated targets: none of the concurrent single-turn methods reports this consistency across a comparably broad model set. We offer the combination of Bayesian strategy selection and semantic crossover as the likeliest explanation, since these are the two components the ablation isolates, but the comparison itself does not establish it: the concurrent methods were evaluated by their own authors under their own judges, budgets, and model versions, and none of them was re-run here. The claim we can support is that REPRO covers a broader set of targets at ceiling under one protocol, not that it is more robust than a specific competing method. Separately, the EvoSynth method reported in OpenRT [38] achieves 100% evaluated models, but against a different and generally newer model set than ours, making direct comparison infeasible; extend-ing REPRO to multi-turn interaction and evaluating on the same frontier targets is an important direction for future work. 

5.2 Limitations 

Computational cost. While more efficient than exhaustive search, REPRO requires substantial LLM queries for both target evaluation and reflective operations. The worst-case per-behavior cost (5,589 queries) is nontrivial for large-scale deployment, and the reflective model must run continuously throughout optimization, adding inference cost beyond the target queries reported in Table 12. 

Single-turn focus. The current implementation optimizes single-turn attacks; multi-turn strategies such as Crescendo [33] achieve high ASR through extended interaction, a complementary attack surface that REPRO does not explore. Extending to multi-turn optimization is the most natural next step for the framework. 

Defended deployments. REPRO is evaluated against target models through their standard API end-points, which carry whatever provider-side safety mechanisms are active. Production deployments often add further layers, including external input classifiers and prompt-injection detectors such as JailGuard [43]. The reported ASR should be read as an upper bound relative to such multi-layered systems, and measuring the gap is an open question we identify in Section 6.1. 

The judge is inside the optimization loop. All ASR figures are computed using the Qwen3-8B judge, and the same judge scores candidates during the search. Because optimization terminates when the judge accepts a candidate, any response the judge misclassifies as compliant is recorded as a solved behavior and is never revisited. Judge error therefore does not average out across a run in the way it would for a fixed-budget method that reports whatever it obtained: it accumulates into the reported ASR. This is a known hazard of optimizing against an evaluator, and it applies with particular force at a reported ceiling of 100%, where the claim admits no slack for false positives. Three pieces of evidence bound the concern without eliminating it. The cross-validation in Section 4.1.4 shows substantial three-way agreement (Fleiss’ 𝜅 = 0.72) and, importantly, shows REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 39 

that Qwen3-8B is the stricter rather than the more permissive rater in the large majority of disagreements, so substituting GPT-4 or Mistral-24B as the reference judge would raise rather than lower the reported figures. Re-scoring under the JailbreakBench and HarmBench evaluation rubrics (Section 4.1.1) reproduced 100%. Against this, the cross-validation covers 365 replies drawn from five of the 36 targets, agreement is weakest on grok-4-1-fast ( 𝜅 = 0.37 to 0 .46), which is one of the frontier targets carrying the headline claim, and the rubric re-scoring examines only attacks the in-loop judge had already accepted. What would settle the question is a gold arbiter held out of the search entirely, applied post hoc to a stratified sample of solved behaviors, with the resulting false-discovery rate reported alongside the in-loop ASR, together with human annotation of the same sample. We regard this as the single highest-value extension of the present evaluation, and the released judge configurations and evaluation rubrics specify the scoring setup precisely enough for such a check to be reproduced. Until it is done, every ASR figure in this paper should be read as judge-referenced under an until-success protocol. 

Baselines are transcribed, and budgets are not matched. No baseline in this paper was re-run by us. Reported figures for PAIR, TAP, PAP, Adaptive RS, AutoDAN-Turbo, KDA, and DAGR are taken from their original publications, which used different judges, different target-model versions, and in several cases query budgets that differ from ours by one to two orders of magnitude (Section 4.1.2). Differences between REPRO and these methods in the comparison tables therefore reflect method and protocol jointly and cannot be attributed to method alone. Budget-matched re-runs under a common judge, and ASR-versus-budget curves that place all methods at a common operating point, are the direct remedy and are left to future work. 

Unevaluated objectives. REPRO is formulated and implemented as a four-objective optimizer, but only attack success is exercised in the reported experiments. All experiments run under a single deployment environment, which collapses XFER to ASR (Section 4.1.3), and detectability and prompt length are optimized internally but never reported as results. The paper therefore contains no Pareto front, no hypervolume measurement, and no evidence that the multi-objective machinery yields trade-offs a practitioner could use. The three-environment protocol defined in Section 3.1 would supply this evidence at modest cost and is the natural next experiment. 

Accumulated state across runs. The strategy library and the amortization cache persist and grow across runs, so the 36-target evaluation is a sequence rather than a set of independent trials, and targets evaluated later benefit from discoveries made earlier. Cold-library, cold-cache runs on a subset of frontier targets would separate the contribution of accumulated state from the contribution of the search, and are not reported here. 

Coverage of the query-cost analysis. Per-behavior query logs were retained for 211 behaviors across five targets (Table 12), a subset of the full experiment matrix. The efficiency figures we report, including the median of 24 target queries, are computed over this subset rather than over every run behind the ASR tables. Comprehensive per-behavior cost accounting across all targets would require re-running the remaining experiments with logging enabled. 

5.3 Implications for Software Engineering Practice 

REPRO’s query efficiency, full automation, and minimal hyperparameter surface (Table 3) make it suitable for integration into CI/CD pipelines: a development team can run a focused sweep on each model update or prompt-template change, surfacing new vulnerabilities before deployment. The median per-behavior cost of 24 target queries makes routine safety sweeps substantially cheaper than manual red-teaming engagements, even against commercial API endpoints. 40 Alotaibi et al. 

The amortization cache (Section 3.5) functions as a persistent test corpus analogous to a regression test suite, where successful attacks are stored, indexed by category, and replayed against subsequent system versions to detect regressions in safety alignment. The same discovered-attack populations can serve as training data for defensive tooling such as JailGuard [43], which requires diverse, annotated attack examples. Our release (Appendix A) publishes the framework source code, strategy library, seed prompts, judge configurations, and ablation experiment scripts and results, enabling practitioners to reproduce the optimization pipeline and generate their own attack corpora for their specific target models. Unlike methods requiring per-target template engineering, REPRO can be operated by engineers without specialized adversarial-ML expertise. 

5.4 Threats to Validity 

We identify potential threats to the validity of our findings across four standard categories. Because the 100% ASR values reported throughout Section 4 are the paper’s central claim,we discuss the judge dimension first as it is the most commonly raised concern in red-teaming evaluations. 

5.4.1 Internal Validity. All ASR numbers in this paper are produced by the Qwen3 judge model, which classifies each target-model response as compliant or refusing against an explicit rubric. Whether the cases Qwen3 classifies as compliant would also be classified as compliant by other judges (the HarmBench evaluation prompt, the Llama-3-70B judge used in JailbreakBench, GPT-4 as a judge, or human annotators) is a question we partially address through cross-validation (Section 4.1.4) and benchmark-native re-evaluation. On JailbreakBench, Adaptive RS [4] drops from 100% ASR on AdvBench to 78–93% on JBB-Behaviors when evaluated under the JBB-native Llama-3-70B judge; REPRO remains at 100% under both its own Qwen3 judge and the JBB evaluation prompt. This difference reflects a combination of stronger optimization and judge-calibration effects that we cannot fully separate. On HarmBench, white-box attacks with gradient access report 34.5% (GCG) and 0.5% (AutoDAN), while REPRO reports 100%. This gap is partly expected: GCG and AutoDAN produce token-level adversarial suffixes optimized for logit-space effects, while REPRO produces semantically coherent prompts that a semantics-aware judge is more likely to classify as harmful compliance. The remain-der is attributable to judge calibration and potential differences in target-model configuration. The headline numbers should be read as judge-specific. The more consequential form of this threat is not judge disagreement in the abstract but the position of the judge relative to the search. Qwen3-8B both scores candidates during optimization and produces the reported ASR, and the loop halts when it accepts a candidate. A response the judge wrongly accepts is therefore recorded as a solved behavior and never re-examined, so judge error is absorbed into the result rather than showing up as variance around it. The asymmetry we observe in Section 4.1.4, in which Qwen3-8B refuses where the alternative judges accept in the large majority of disagreements, argues against systematic leniency and is the strongest evidence we can offer that this has not happened at scale. It is not conclusive: strictness measured on a stratified sample of archived replies does not preclude a subset of accepted attacks being false positives, and the sample covers five of the 36 targets. We report the result as obtained and mark the false-discovery rate against a search-independent gold arbiter as the open quantity. 

Partial mitigation through rubric re-scoring, and its limits. We re-scored REPRO’s successful attacks under each benchmark’s published evaluation rubric: the JailbreakBench evaluation prompt for JBB results and the HarmBench evaluation prompt for HarmBench results. Both reproduced the 100% ASR obtained with the Qwen3 judge. Two limits keep this from being a full mitigation. We applied each benchmark’s evaluation prompt rather than each benchmark’s reference scorer, and JailbreakBench specifies a Llama-3-70B judge while HarmBench specifies a trained classifier, so this REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 41 

establishes agreement across rubrics and not agreement with the benchmarks’ own graders. More fundamentally, the re-scoring is applied to the set of attacks the in-loop judge accepted, which is a set selected by the quantity under test. It can find false positives, and found none, but it cannot bound the false-discovery rate of a search that was steered by the judge being checked. A gold arbiter held out of the loop, applied post hoc to a stratified sample and reported alongside the in-loop figure, together with human annotation of that sample, is the check that would close this gap. The released judge configurations and rubrics specify the scoring setup used here. 

Other internal threats. Stochastic variation in evolutionary optimization is a secondary internal threat. The component ablation applies McNemar’s test with Bonferroni correction within a single run, but uses one run per configuration, so run-to-run variance across seeds is not captured (Sections 4.4 and 5.2). Its results are statistically grounded within the observed run and not verified across independent replications. 

5.4.2 External Validity. Our evaluation spans 36 models across 11 families and three benchmarks totaling 350 behaviors, providing broad coverage. However, all benchmarks focus on English-language text-only interactions; REPRO’s effectiveness on multilingual inputs, multimodal models, or domain-specific deployments (e.g., medical, legal) remains untested. Provider safety training also changes without notice, so the reported figures are a snapshot rather than a permanent property of the targets (Section 5.2). As noted there, all evaluations are against API-served models with their provider-level defenses, but without the additional defensive layers (external input classifiers, prompt-injection detectors, runtime content policies) that production deployments may add, so ASR against fully guarded systems is expected to be lower. 

5.4.3 Construct Validity. We measure attack success through ASR, the standard metric in the jailbreaking literature. However, ASR treats all successful attacks as equivalent regardless of the severity or specificity of the elicited harmful content: a model that provides vague, unusable guidance and a model that provides detailed operational instructions both count as a success. This coarse binary reduces the metric’s usefulness as a deployment-risk signal, and a severity-graded metric (e.g., distinguishing engagement from substantive harmful content from operationally specific harmful content) would provide a more nuanced view of vulnerability. Developing such a graded metric, and re-scoring our existing attack corpus under it, is an important direction for future work. 

5.4.4 Conclusion Validity. Every baseline comparison in this paper rests on figures published by other authors [8, 22, 25, 27, 28, 42, 44] under their own judges, evaluation protocols, and model versions, and we flag those differences at each table rather than presenting the rows as like-for-like (Sections 4.1.2 and 5.2). A second limit is internal to our metric: a 100% ceiling leaves ASR with little discriminative power for comparing REPRO variants against one another. 

5.5 Ethical Considerations 

This research aims to improve LLM safety through systematic vulnerability discovery. All exper-iments target publicly available models with known safety training. Attack examples presented in this paper use one of two disclosure levels: structural patterns with placeholder goals (e.g., Table 16) that preserve defensive insight without specifying harmful content, and a small number of one complete attack-response pair (Figure 4) included to demonstrate that REPRO’s attacks elicit genuine harmful outputs rather than exploiting evaluation artifacts. The framework is designed for defensive research and safety evaluation. 42 Alotaibi et al. 

Responsible disclosure. We did not undertake pre-submission vendor notification for the com-mercial models evaluated, and we state this explicitly. Our reasoning: (i) all evaluated behaviors come from publicly available benchmarks (AdvBench, JailbreakBench, HarmBench) that the same vendors’ models are continuously evaluated against, so we do not surface behaviors the vendors are unaware of; (ii) REPRO’s mutation strategies are drawn from public literature with full citations in Section 2, and the framework itself (independent of any specific prompt) is the scientific contri-bution; (iii) the paper does not release the full unsanitized attack corpus. We commit to responsive disclosure: on request from any vendor whose models appear in this evaluation, we will provide the corresponding discovered attacks, the target-model versions and evaluation dates involved, and the configuration needed to reproduce them, through the corresponding author. For future work in this area we intend to notify affected vendors before submission when results include previously unreported attack structures. 

Release scope. Our release (Appendix A) includes the framework source code, strategy library, seed prompts, judge configurations, and ablation experiment scripts and results. We do not release the full corpus of discovered attacks from our evaluation runs, though we will provide it to any vendor whose models appear in this evaluation on request to the corresponding author. Practitioners can reproduce the optimization pipeline and generate their own attack corpora for their specific target models using the released code. The dual-use nature of red-teaming research requires careful balance between advancing safety evaluation capabilities and preventing misuse. We believe systematic automated red-teaming, as embodied by REPRO, provides greater benefit through proactive vulnerability discovery than harm through potential misuse, particularly given that manual red-teaming and simpler automated methods are already widely available. We nevertheless treat this as an ongoing judgment rather than a settled one, and we welcome community feedback on the disclosure and release posture adopted here. 

6 Conclusion 

We presented REPRO, a reflective evolutionary framework for automated LLM red-teaming that integrates semantic crossover, Bayesian strategy selection, structured failure analysis, and adaptive stagnation management within a multi-objective Pareto optimization framework. Unlike prior methods requiring gray-box access or manual template engineering, REPRO operates in a pure black-box setting with fully automated strategy discovery. Our experiments are consistent with principled algorithmic design, rather than auxiliary model strength, being the dominant factor in effective adversarial prompt optimization: a 4-bit 7B re-flective model matches a 4-bit 24B one across the evaluated targets, and the component ablation isolates semantic crossover and Bayesian strategy selection as the two mechanisms that carry measurable weight. Under our protocol, REPRO reaches 100% ASR on AdvBench, JailbreakBench, and HarmBench across 36 target models from 11 families, with a median of 24 target queries per behavior and only a compressed open-weight reflective model on a single consumer GPU. Recent concurrent methods (AutoDAN-Turbo, KDA, DAGR) reach up to 99% ASR on individual targets, and we are not aware of another single-turn black-box method reporting uniform coverage across a comparably broad model set. Cross-behavior optimization discovers single shared attacks reaching 100% ASR on the 50 AdvBench behaviors it was optimized against, showing that cross-behavior optimization is feasible on the optimization set. We are explicit about what these numbers do and do not establish. They are produced by a judge that also steers the search, under a protocol that runs until that judge is satisfied; the baselines they sit beside were run by other authors under other judges and budgets; and the framework’s REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 43 

transfer, detectability, and length objectives are implemented but not evaluated here. Section 5.4 develops each of these, and Section 5.2 names the specific experiments that would resolve them: post-hoc scoring by a gold arbiter held out of the loop, budget-matched re-runs of the black-box baselines, cold-start runs without accumulated library and cache state, and the three-environment protocol. We report the catalogue we have, and the released code, rubrics, and scripts specify the setup precisely enough for these checks to be carried out. Extending to multi-turn attacks and to defended deployments are the primary next steps. By framing automated red-teaming as both an adversarial prompt-optimization problem and a disciplined test-generation activity, REPRO provides software engineers with a practical, open tool for pre-deployment safety evaluation. The median per-behavior cost of 24 target queries makes routine safety sweeps substantially cheaper than manual red-teaming engagements, even against commercial API endpoints. The amortization cache enables regression testing across model updates without re-running the full optimization. 

6.1 Future Directions 

Several directions are tied directly to findings in this paper. First, extending REPRO to multi-turn interaction would address the complementary attack surface where concurrent methods such as Crescendo [33] demonstrate strong results; this extension is currently underway. Second, evaluating REPRO against defended deployments (input classifiers, output filters, runtime policies) would measure the gap between API-level and production ASR, addressing the most significant limitation identified in Section 5.2; initial work on defensive optimization extensions is in progress. Third, using REPRO’s discovered attacks as training data for adversarial fine-tuning would close the loop between offensive discovery and defensive improvement, a direction the amortization cache’s persistent storage of successful attacks directly supports. Fourth, applying the evolutionary optimization framework to multimodal jailbreaking (image-plus-text attacks) would extend coverage beyond the text-only setting. Finally, while the judge cross-validation in Section 4.1.4 and the benchmark-native re-evaluation provide substantial evidence of judge robustness, extending validation to human annotators would further strengthen the internal-validity picture.

B Soft Success Score Formulation 

This appendix details the soft success score introduced in §3.4.1. Against robust models, binary success/failure signals are sparse and provide no gradient between “almost worked” and “complete failure.” For each failed attempt, REPRO computes a soft score combining four components on a 0–1 scale: soft (𝑥, 𝑟 ) = 0.35 · 𝑝 𝑐 + 0.30 · 𝑢 𝑟 + 0.20 · ( 1 − 𝑠 𝑟 ) + 0.15 · 𝑒 𝑙 (12) where: 

• 𝑝 𝑐 (partial compliance ) measures content generated before the refusal point, detected via refusal pattern matching. Higher values indicate the model began complying before refusing. 

• 𝑢 𝑟 (useful content ratio ) is the ratio of substantive content to refusal boilerplate, computed via keyword analysis. 

• 𝑠 𝑟 (refusal strength ) is emphatic-refusal detection via categorical refusal phrases, inverted so that 1 means weak refusal and 0 means strong. 

• 𝑒 𝑙 (engagement level ) measures whether the model engaged with the topic at all versus immediately refusing without acknowledgment. 

Weight rationale. The four weights rank components by expected informativeness. Partial com-pliance receives the largest weight because the strongest near-success signal is the model beginning to comply before the safety system interrupts. Useful content ratio is second; it captures substantive engagement even in refused responses. Refusal strength is third, carrying information but noisier (a strong refusal phrase can coexist with substantive pre-refusal content). Engagement level is the smallest, serving as a tie-breaker. Soft scores are integrated into REPRO at two points. During Pareto selection, near-miss candidates receive a fitness bonus capped at 0.1: fitness ′ (𝑥 ) = fitness (𝑥 ) + min (0.1, ¯𝑠 𝑥 · 0.2) (13) REPRO: Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimizer for Automated LLM Red-Teaming 45 

where ¯ 𝑠 𝑥 is the mean soft score across failed tasks. Second, tasks with high soft scores are highlighted as near-miss tasks in mutation feedback, focusing the reflective LLM’s attention on attack directions that are close to working.
