Effectively eliciting the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) at inference time remains an important challenge. Existing studies address this challenge through context engineering, which shapes user queries, or fixed steering, which directly intervenes in model computation. However, predefined contexts and fixed intervention strategies may not adequately accommodate the diverse knowledge and reasoning requirements of individual queries. This raises a natural question: Can internal steering in LLMs be made query-adaptive? In this work, we investigate whether query-induced internal activations can guide adaptive steering in LLMs. Our pilot studies reveal subject- and layer-dependent neuron activation patterns and show that amplifying subject-relevant neurons can improve model performance. Based on these findings, we propose Q-NeuS , a query-adaptive neuron steering framework that dynamically determines which neurons to modulate and by how much for each input. Specifically, Q-NeuS constructs a compact subject-guided candidate neuron space and employs a lightweight Query-Adaptive Gate to generate neuron-wise modulation coefficients. To learn effective intervention strategies, we introduce Neu-GRPO , which adapts group-relative optimization to train the Query-Adaptive Gate using the relative performance of multiple neuron intervention configurations. The base LLM remains frozen throughout training and inference, and no task or subject labels are required at inference time. Extensive experiments across multiple LLMs and benchmarks demonstrate that Q-NeuS consistently improves model performance and exhibits broad applicability across model scales and strong cross-benchmark transferability.

## 1 Introduction 

Exploring and eliciting the latent capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) is crucial for understanding and improving their latent potential. [1–3]. Existing studies largely follow two directions (As illustrated in Fig. 1). The first operates on model inputs through context engineering [4–6]. While effective, these methods often rely on manually designed or task-specific contexts and are sensitive to how well the provided context matches each query. The second directly manipulates internal representations through steering [7–9]. However, existing steering methods typically target predefined tasks, capabilities, or behaviors, with the steering target or direction identified in advance. Such predefined interventions are difficult to generalize to heterogeneous queries that may require different knowledge and reasoning processes. Despite operating at different levels, both paradigms still rely on an intervention strategy specified in advance rather than adapting it to the current query. This raises a natural question: Can internal steering in LLMs be made query-adaptive? 

To investigate this question, we first examine whether the internal activations of LLMs provide informative signals for query-adaptive intervention. We conduct a series of preliminary experiments to characterize neuron activation patterns across various domains and evaluate the effects of intervening on highly activated neurons. We observe that 1) highly activated neurons exhibit clear subject- and layer-dependent structures, and 2) amplifying subject-relevant neurons can improve model performance, while the intervention effects vary substantially across layers and subjects. Based on these pilot results, we introduce Q-NeuS, a query-adaptive neuron steering framework that identifies and modulates neurons according to the internal state induced by each query. Specifically, we first construct a subject-guided candidate neuron space from training data to constrain the intervention search space. We then introduce a lightweight Query-Adaptive Gate that takes the internal activations as input and dynamically generates neuron-wise modulation coefficients, allowing different queries to induce different steering patterns. To learn effective intervention strategies, we further propose Neu-GRPO, which adapts GRPO to the neuron intervention setting and optimizes the Query-Adaptive Gate by comparing the relative performance of multiple neuron intervention configurations. During inference, the method requires no task or domain labels, and the modulation pattern is determined solely by the internal activation state of the current query. Notably, Q-NeuS trained only on MMLU-Pro transfers effectively to multiple unseen benchmarks, demonstrating broad applicability across model scales and strong cross-benchmark transferability. 

## 2 Related Work 

Activation Steering for LLMs. Activation steering has emerged as an effective approach for controlling LLM behavior by directly modifying internal activations without updating the base model parameters [7, 8, 10]. Prior work has demonstrated its effectiveness in behavior control and reasoning enhancement [11], typically using techniques such as Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) [7] or Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) [12] to construct or identify steerable representations. More recent studies explore conditional and adaptive steering [13–15]. However, these methods remain constrained by a preconstructed intervention space, where the available steering directions or primitives are identified in advance. Unlike these methods, Q-NeuS performs query-adaptive neuron modulation based on query-induced activations, enabling fine-grained intervention beyond predefined steering spaces.

## 3 Preliminary Study 

In this section, we investigate whether LLM activations support query-adaptive intervention by relating subject-level queries to neuron activations. We conduct experiments with Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct on all 14 subjects of MMLU-Pro, splitting each subject into training/validation/test sets (70%/10%/20%) for neuron identification, layer selection, and final evaluation, respectively. For each layer, we extract the MLP activations at the final prompt token immediately before the output projection and rank neurons by their mean activation over the training examples. We then multiply the activations of the top 100 neurons by 1.5 and those of the next 400 neurons by 1.2, while leaving the remaining neurons unchanged. We independently intervene on each layer and retain those that improve validation accuracy. Finally, all selected layers are jointly intervened on and evaluated on the held-out test set. More details are provided in Appendix A. 

Observation 1. Different subjects exhibit distinct neuron activation patterns. We examine the organization of highly activated neurons across subjects by selecting the top 500 neurons in each layer and computing pairwise overlap. As shown in Fig. 2, semantically related subjects exhibit higher overlap and form clear clusters, while substantial sharing remains even across dissimilar subjects. This suggests that LLMs rely on both shared computational components and subject-specific neuron subsets. As shown in Appendix A, this organization varies across depth: shallow layers are largely shared across subjects, intermediate layers exhibit stronger subject-specific structure, and the distinction weakens again in deeper layers. These results indicate that neuron organization is both subject-dependent and layer-dependent, motivating layer-aware and query-adaptive intervention. 3Query  “Which force keeps

Observation 2. Amplifying subject-relevant neurons improves model performance. As shown in Tab. 1, jointly intervening on the 21 positive-gain layers selected on the validation set improves test accuracy from 31.56% to 32.92% (+1.36 pp), demonstrating that selectively amplifying subject-relevant neurons can effectively improve model performance. However, the gains vary substantially across layers and subjects, with some subjects even experiencing performance degradation under joint intervention. These results suggest that while neuron-level intervention is effective, a fixed intervention strategy cannot consistently accommodate the heterogeneous requirements of different queries, motivating query-adaptive neuron steering. 

Motivation. These results show that modulating subject-relevant neurons can effectively improve model performance, while the optimal intervention varies across layers and subjects. However, our pilot study relies on manually specified neuron counts, fixed modulation coefficients, and predetermined layer selection, limiting its ability to adapt to individual queries. To address this limitation, we introduce Q-NeuS, which employs a learnable gating mechanism to dynamically determine which neurons to modulate and by how much according to the internal activation state of each query. 

## 4 Q-NeuS 

Based on these pilot findings, we introduce Q-NeuS, a query-adaptive neuron steering framework. During training, subject-guided candidate neurons are explored through multiple rollouts, and a Query-Adaptive Gate is optimized with Neu-GRPO using their relative performance. At inference time, the Query-Adaptive Gate dynamically modulates all FFN neurons across different Transformer layers based on the internal activations induced by each query, without requiring subject labels. 44.1 Query-Adaptive Neuron Gating

4.2 Neu-GRPO 

To learn query-adaptive neuron modulation policies of Q-NeuS, we introduce Neu-GRPO , which adapts GRPO to neuron-level intervention. For each training query, we construct multiple neuron intervention configurations and evaluate their relative task performance. The resulting group-relative feedback is then used to optimize Q-NeuS, while keeping the base LLM frozen.

## 5 Experiment 

5.1 Experimental Setup  

> Models.

We evaluate Q-NeuS on seven LLMs spanning different model sizes and families: Qwen2.5-3B [20], Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, Qwen3-32B [21], Ministral3-8B, and Ministral3-14B [22].  

> Datasets and Evaluation.

We use MMLU-Pro [23] , SuperGPQA [24] , and WildSci [25] as our primary benchmarks. For in-domain evaluation, we partition each benchmark into training (70%), validation (10%), and test (20%) splits, train Q-NeuS on the training split, and report results on the held-out test split. To assess cross-benchmark generalization, we directly evaluate the model trained on MMLU-Pro on GPQA-Main, GPQA-Diamond [26] , SuperGPQA, LiveBench [27] , AGIEval [28] , GSM8K [29] , and ARC-Challenge [30] , without using any training data from these target benchmarks. Accuracy is used as the primary evaluation metric.  

> Baselines.

We compare Q-NeuS with three baselines. Base is the original LLM without intervention. Fix 

follows our preliminary study, selecting highly activated neurons from training-set mean activations, applying predefined modulation coefficients, and selecting intervention layers on the validation set. Implementation Details. We set the candidate neuron threshold to τ = 0 .1. The Query-Adaptive Gate uses a hidden dimension of 2,048 and a layer embedding dimension of 32 , with a maximum modulation coefficient of 1.7. For each query, we construct K = 4 rollouts and sample 60% of the candidate neurons in each layer. In Neu-GRPO, we set λm = 1 .0 and λs = 0 .1 for the margin reward and intervention strength penalty, respectively. Q-NeuS is trained with Adam at a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4 for up to 3 epochs. All main experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A800 GPUs. 

5.2 Main Results 

Overall Performance across Models and Benchmarks. We train and evaluate Q-NeuS separately on MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, and WildSci using seven LLMs. As shown in Tab. 2, Q-NeuS improves performance across all evaluated models and benchmarks, with gains spanning model scales from 3B to 32B parameters. The improvements extend beyond the Qwen family to the Ministral family, indicating that the effectiveness of Q-NeuS is not tied to a specific model family or task. This broad applicability is attributed to the query-adaptive design of Q-NeuS, which generates neuron-wise modulation from query-induced internal activations without relying on fixed steering patterns or modifying the base model parameters. These results demonstrate the broad applicability of query-adaptive FFN neuron modulation across model scales, architectures, and task domains. 

Cross-Benchmark Generalization. We train Q-NeuS only on MMLU-Pro and directly evaluate it on the other datasets in Tab. 3. Across most benchmarks, performance improves or remains comparable to the base model, suggesting that the learned neuron modulation policy can transfer to other knowledge-intensive reasoning tasks. However, transfer gains on LiveBench are relatively limited, with only small improvements or occasional declines across models. This may be attributed to the larger distribution shift from MMLU-Pro: LiveBench includes tasks such as coding and data analysis, whose formats and required capabilities are less represented in the subject-oriented MMLU-Pro training data. As a result, both the candidate neuron space and the learned modulation policy may transfer less effectively to these different reasoning patterns. 5.4 Case Study 

We compare Fix and Q-NeuS on five queries: an original chemistry question (Q1), its paraphrase (Q2), a chemistry question involving a different concept (Q3), a question from a related subject, mathematics (Q4), and a question from an unrelated subject, economics (Q5). Fig. 4 presents the overlap among the top-100 most strongly modulated neurons together with the corresponding answer correctness. Fix applies a fixed 9subject-specific intervention, resulting in identical top-100 modulated neurons for Q1–Q3. Although related subjects exhibit higher overlap than unrelated ones, the differences remain limited. It indicates that Fix captures coarse subject-level relationships but cannot adapt its intervention to individual queries within the same subject. Q-NeuS exhibits three notable properties: 1) It produces query-specific neuron patterns even within the same subject: the overlap between Q1 and Q3 is 0.88 despite both being chemistry questions. 2) It preserves a high overlap of 0.96 between Q1 and its paraphrase Q2 and answers both correctly, whereas Fix fails on Q2 despite succeeding on Q1, demonstrating robustness to prompt reformulation. 3) The selected neuron sets reflect semantic relationships across queries: relative to Q1, the overlap is 0.81 for the related-subject query Q4 but drops to 0.45 for the unrelated-subject query Q5, yielding a clearer distinction than Fix. Q-NeuS answers all five queries correctly, while Fix succeeds only on Q1. These results illustrate that Q-NeuS can adjust neuron-level interventions to individual queries while preserving similar modulation patterns for semantically equivalent inputs. Appendix C further shows that these query-specific overlap patterns vary across Transformer layers and exhibit clear layer-dependent structure, consistent with our preliminary findings. 

## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we investigate query-adaptive neuron steering for LLMs. Our preliminary study shows that highly activated neurons exhibit subject- and layer-dependent patterns, and that amplifying subject-relevant neurons can improve model performance. Motivated by these findings, we propose Q-NeuS, which dynamically determines which neurons to modulate and by how much from query-induced internal activations. Q-NeuS combines a subject-guided candidate neuron space with a lightweight Query-Adaptive Gate and introduces Neu-GRPO to optimize neuron modulation through group-relative feedback while keeping the base LLM frozen. Extensive experiments across seven LLMs and multiple benchmarks demonstrate that Q-NeuS improves performance across model scales and architectures and transfers effectively to multiple unseen benchmarks with diverse knowledge and reasoning requirements. These results show that query-induced internal activations can provide effective signals for adaptive intervention, enabling LLMs to better elicit their existing capabilities without updating the base model parameters or requiring task labels at inference time.

## A Additional Analysis of the Preliminary Study 

A.1 Neuron Overlap Patterns across Layers 

To further analyze the organization of subject-related neurons across different layers, we compute the overlap matrices of the Top-500 highly activated neurons across subjects at several representative layers, as shown in Fig. 5. Overall, different layers exhibit clearly distinct organizational patterns. In shallower layers, such as layer 5, nearly all subject pairs exhibit relatively high overlap rates, suggesting that highly activated neurons in shallow layers largely reflect shared representations across subjects, with limited subject-specific differentiation. In intermediate layers, such as layers 15 and 25, subjects with similar semantic content or reasoning patterns tend to exhibit higher neuron overlap, whereas more dissimilar subjects show substantially lower overlap, forming clearer block-like structures. This differentiation is most pronounced at layer 25, indicating stronger subject-related neuron organization in intermediate layers. In deeper layers, such as layer 35, the overall overlap across subjects increases again, while the block structure becomes less distinct. These observations further indicate that highly activated neurons exhibit different organizational structures across layers. 

A.2 Overall Activation Magnitude across Layers 

To further examine how internal activations vary with network depth, we compute the mean absolute activation of all FFN neurons at each Transformer layer for samples from different subjects. As shown in Fig. 6, different subjects exhibit highly similar layer-wise trends: the overall activation magnitude is relatively low in shallow layers, increases gradually through the intermediate layers, and becomes stronger in deeper layers. In contrast, the differences in mean activation magnitude across subjects within the same layer are relatively small, with noticeable divergence appearing only in some deeper layers. This suggests that subject-related differences are not primarily reflected in the overall activation magnitude. Combined with the neuron overlap analysis in the previous subsection, these results suggest that subject-related differences are more likely to manifest in the specific composition and distribution patterns of activated neurons. 

A.3 Layer-wise Intervention Effects 

We further analyze the intervention effects across different layers in the preliminary study. During validation, the 36 Transformer layers exhibit substantially different responses to neuron intervention. Among them, 21 layers achieve positive accuracy gains, while the remaining layers show only limited improvements or performance degradation. Among all single-layer interventions, layer 18 yields the largest improvement, increasing accuracy by 1.17 percentage points. In addition to the layer-selection results reported in the main text, we further examine the setting without layer selection. Specifically, we remove the validation-based layer selection and directly apply joint neuron intervention to all 36 Transformer layers. Under this setting, the model accuracy decreases from the baseline of 31.56% to 29.41%, corresponding to a drop of 2.15 percentage points. In contrast, jointly intervening on the 21 layers selected on the validation set achieves an accuracy of 32.92%. This comparison shows that even though intervention on some individual layers can yield positive gains, indiscriminately extending the intervention to all layers can substantially degrade overall performance. Therefore, simply increasing the intervention coverage does not necessarily lead to better performance.
