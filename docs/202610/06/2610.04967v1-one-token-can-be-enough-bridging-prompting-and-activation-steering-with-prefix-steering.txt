# Abstract 

Prompting guides language model behavior through the initial context, whereas activation steering often intervenes throughout generation. A natural question is whether steering can produce effects on subsequent computation similar to those of prompting. Under fixed-state attention assumptions, we establish sufficient conditions for single- and multi-token steering to match prompt-induced attention-head outputs, and characterize how changes in input rep-resentations affect this match and its approximation error. This attention-level connection leads us to ask whether, at the behavioral level, steering can also guide subsequent generation through a brief initial intervention. We study Prefix Steering, which applies existing steering directions and operators over a short span starting at the final prompt token, with no further direct intervention afterward. We examine how intervention duration and strength jointly shape the control–capability trade-off. Across four models and five tasks, intervention over a short span, even a single token, often retains much of full steering’s behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities, offering a trade-off competitive with, and in some settings better than, prompting and alternative steering-strength policies. Prefix Steering also remains effective on final-answer formatting tasks after reasoning, suggesting that a brief initial intervention can influence behavior expressed well after steering ends. These findings challenge the common practice of steering every generated token and motivate a more dynamical view of activation steering, in which a brief intervention can alter the trajectory of subsequent generation without continued intervention. ## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) support a wide range of tasks [33, 43], but their deployment often requires control over behavioral properties such as safety, tone, and output format [3, 27, 57]. Two common approaches are prompting, which guides generation through instructions and demonstrations in the input context [35], and activation steering, which directly modifies the model’s internal repre-sentations [31, 44, 53, 60]. However, eliciting a target behavior can come at the cost of the model’s underlying task capabilities, making the trade-off between behavioral control and capability preservation a central concern [20, 24, 52]. To understand this trade-off, we consider how prompting and activation steering introduce control into the model. Prompting specifies control through discrete input tokens, whose effects propagate across layers and reach subsequent token positions through causal attention [10, 12, 28]. Activation steering directly modifies continuous representations, allowing explicit choices of intervention direction, strength, layer, and token position [29, 32, 39, 44, 51, 60]. Yet this additional flexibility does not necessarily yield a better trade-off [41]: prompting remains competitive with, and in some settings outperforms, commonly used steering methods when generation quality is taken into account [10, 20, 52]. These observations motivate us to examine how the two approaches are connected and whether steering can adopt aspects of prompting’s control mechanism to better preserve capabilities. To explore this connection, we focus on how their effects reach subsequent computation through causal attention. We first examine when modifying existing token representations can reproduce the attention effects of adding prompt tokens. This perspective also motivates examining how control is introduced over time. As illustrated in Figure 1, prompting introduces control through the initial context, yet can guide behavior later in generation. An early activation intervention may similarly influence subsequent generation through the representations it modifies and the generated context it helps shape [4, 50]. This suggests that Prefix Steering, which applies existing steering directions and operators over a short span starting at the final prompt token, with no further direct intervention afterward, could retain behavioral control while reducing interference with general capabilities. We therefore ask: 

® Research Questions 

Q1. When can activation steering reproduce the attention-level effects of prompting? 

Q2. Can Prefix Steering retain behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities? To address the first question, we analyze when modifying existing token representations can reproduce the attention effects of adding prompt tokens. We first show that, under sufficient conditions, steering one existing token and adding one prompt token produce exactly the same attention-head output. We then characterize the input-representation changes that preserve this match and derive an approximation-error bound that grows linearly with distance from this set. Extending the analysis to multiple prompt and steered tokens reveals additional matching constraints, which can narrow the set of changes preserving exact matching and make accurate approximation more difficult. Experiments in the model’s actual attention computation support these predictions: matching errors grow with distance from the matching set, while increasing the number of prompt or steered tokens generally makes matching less accurate. Motivated by this attention-level connection, we address the second question through Prefix Steering and examine how duration and strength jointly shape the control–capability trade-off, including when stronger steering at one position can approximate weaker steering over multiple positions at the attention-output level. Across four models and five tasks, using additive steering [44] and COAST [24], short interventions, even at a single token, often yield a more favorable control–capability Pareto frontier than full steering, preserving more general capability at comparable behavioral control. Their trade-offs remain competitive with prompting and adaptive steering-strength policies [34, 47]. Their effectiveness on final-answer formatting after reasoning further suggests that a brief intervention can shape behavior well after steering ends. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We establish an attention-level connection between prompting and activation steering, deriving sufficient conditions for exact output matching and an error bound that grows linearly with distance from the matching set, with experiments supporting this geometric picture. 

> 3

• We study Prefix Steering using existing steering directions and operators, and characterize how intervention duration and strength interact, including conditions under which stronger steering at one position can approximate weaker steering over multiple positions. • Across the evaluated models, tasks, and steering methods, we show that short interventions, sometimes at a single token, retain much of full steering’s behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities. Their effectiveness on final-answer formatting after reasoning suggests that lasting behavioral control need not require continued intervention. 

## 2 Related Work 

Activation steering. Activation steering controls model behavior by modifying intermediate represen-tations at inference time [5, 14, 44, 50, 58–60]. Existing methods use behaviorally relevant directions for activation addition, directional ablation, or geometry-aware transformations [1, 2, 9, 18, 23, 31, 42, 55]. Many commonly used methods repeatedly intervene during autoregressive generation [14, 31]. Along-side steering effectiveness, recent work studies how to preserve generation quality and limit changes unrelated to the target concept [20, 24]. 

Prompt-based control. Natural-language prompting controls model behavior through instructions and demonstrations in the discrete token space [22, 30, 37, 43, 49], whereas activation steering directly modifies continuous internal representations. Related approaches also exploit continuous representations, including learned prefixes and latent reasoning [7, 8, 13, 15, 19]. Prompting introduces its control signal through the input context, which subsequent tokens can attend to during generation, providing a natural motivation for studying the lasting effects of early activation interventions. 

Connecting prompting and activation steering. Empirical comparisons show that prompting remains a strong baseline and can offer a more favorable trade-off between control effectiveness and generation quality than activation steering, particularly in concept-injection settings [10, 20, 52]. Recent work connects the two through activation interventions that imitate prompting or follow prompt-induced attention contributions [10, 12]. Closely related, Bao et al. [4] train steering vectors for interventions restricted to a few prompt tokens. We study Prefix Steering using existing steering directions and operators, and establish a local attention-level connection: under fixed-state assumptions, modifying one token’s representation can reproduce the attention-head output induced by an added prompt token. Across evaluated models, tasks, and methods, short interventions, sometimes on one token, often retain much of full-generation steering’s behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities.

## 4 An Attention-Level View of Prompting and Steering 

Prompting adds token representations, while activation steering modifies existing ones. Both influence subsequent computation through causal attention [6, 26, 45, 46], motivating us to ask whether modifying an existing representation can match the attention output induced by an added token. We begin with single-token matching and extend it to multiple prompt and steered tokens.

## 5 Balancing Control and Capability with Prefix Steering 

Prior studies [20, 52] have found that prompting can offer a more favorable control–capability trade-off than steering throughout generation. A key difference lies in when control is introduced: prompting acts only through the initial context, whereas full steering intervenes at every generated token. Our attention-level analysis in the above section shows that steering tokens in the current context can reproduce prompt-induced attention outputs, suggesting that steering need not persist throughout generation to exert a prompt-like influence. Like a prompt, an early intervention may guide later tokens through the representations it modifies and the context it helps shape. This perspective suggests that steering may act less as a continuous control signal and more as an intervention that redirects the model’s subsequent generation trajectory. We therefore study Prefix Steering, which applies an existing steering direction over a short span starting at the final prompt token, and examine at the behavioral level whether it retains the control of full steering while better preserving general capabilities. The steering directions constructed in the previous section depend on the specific prompt, context, and input representation. However, as shown in Appendix B.1, these constructed directions are highly correlated with directions estimated by DiM. We therefore follow common practice and use DiM-based steering directions throughout this section.

We therefore select values from {0.01 , 0.1, 1, 10 } for additive steering and target angles from {10 ◦, 30 ◦, 50 ◦, 70 ◦, 90 ◦} for COAST based on the control–capability trade-off. 

5.2 Evaluation Across Models, Tasks, and Steering Methods 

We next evaluate short steering spans across models, tasks, and intervention methods, including final-answer formatting after reasoning, where control is required well after steering ends. Using additive steering [44] and COAST [24], we compare Prefix Steering with full steering, prompting (Appendix C.2), and unsteered baselines on Qwen3 1.7B and 14B [54] and OLMo 3 7B and 32B [25]. Tasks cover safety [21], sentiment [38], politeness [11], and two instruction-following tasks on MATH-500 [16]: IF-Boxed requires the final answer in \boxed{} , whereas IF-Plain requires it in plain text. Capability is measured by MMLU-Pro accuracy [48] for concept control and MATH-500 accuracy for formatting. Figure 5 shows that one- and five-token prefixes often retain much of full steering’s behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities. Compared with prompting, the benefits are task-dependent: prompting performs well on sentiment, politeness, and boxed-answer formatting, whereas steering achieves stronger control on safety and plain-answer formatting in our evaluation. The contrast be-tween the two formatting tasks may reflect a model preference for enclosing mathematical answers in 

\boxed{} : the boxed-answer instruction aligns with this tendency, whereas the plain-answer instruction requires overriding it. Prompting alone may therefore be less effective when the requested behavior conflicts with a model’s preferred response format, highlighting the value of activation steering in such settings. Full steering can achieve stronger control in some cases, but short prefixes often provide a more favorable balance between control and task accuracy. The final-answer formatting results further demonstrate that Prefix Steering’s influence is not confined to the opening of a response: brief initial interventions can guide how the final answer is expressed after intervening reasoning, well after steering 

10 ends. This extends the refusal example in Section 5.1, showing that early steering can affect behavior expressed later in the response. 

Comparison with other strength policies. The preceding results show that short steering spans can improve the control–capability trade-off. We next examine whether similar benefits can be obtained by reducing intervention strength over time rather than stopping after a short prefix. On OLMo 3 7B, we compare one- and five-token Prefix Steering with constant full steering, linear and exponential decay, and the adaptive policies DAS [34] and ACT [47]. These comparisons assess whether a simple fixed cutoff remains competitive with gradual or adaptive strength adjustment. Appendix B.3 provides implementation details. Figure 6 shows that short prefixes offer competitive control–capability trade-offs across the five tasks. Single-token steer-ing generally preserves more capability, while extending the prefix to five tokens provides stronger control at some capabil-ity cost. Full steering and other strength policies achieve higher control in several settings, but do not consistently improve both control and capability over short pre-fixes. Prefix Steering therefore offers a simple alternative when capability preser-vation is a priority. Its effectiveness is also consistent with the early concentra-tion of intervention strength reported for DAS [34].  Takeaway 

Brief initial steering, sometimes at a single token, often retains much of full steering’s behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities. Duration and strength jointly shape this trade-off, and effects on final-answer formatting after reasoning show that behavioral control can persist well beyond the intervention window. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We connected prompting and activation steering by establishing conditions under which modifying existing token representations matches prompt-induced attention outputs under fixed-state assumptions. Motivated by this connection, we studied Prefix Steering using existing steering directions and operators. Across the evaluated models and tasks, brief initial interventions, often at a single token, retain much of full steering’s behavioral control while better preserving general capabilities. Their effectiveness on final-answer formatting after reasoning further suggests that behavioral control can persist beyond the intervention itself. Together, these findings support brief initial steering as a practical approach to guiding subsequent generation. More broadly, our results challenge the common practice of applying activation steering at every generated token: for many behaviors, continued intervention may be unnecessary once an initial intervention has shifted the model onto a desired generation trajectory. This raises a more fundamental mechanistic question of how the effect of a brief intervention is carried forward after steering stops. One possibility is that steering alters a persistent latent or belief-like state that governs subsequent generation; another is that its effect is propagated through the generated tokens themselves, which then serve as context for later computation. Our attention-level analysis provides a local connection between prompting and steering, but relies on fixed-state assumptions and does not establish their equivalence 

> 11

during free generation, while our behavioral experiments do not distinguish between these mechanisms. Understanding how altered representations and generated context jointly sustain behavioral control could provide a more dynamical account of why activation steering works and when continued intervention is necessary. Future work could investigate these mechanisms and develop reusable steering directions informed by the matching construction. Since the appropriate intervention duration and strength remain task-dependent, evaluation on longer generations and tasks requiring control at multiple stages could further clarify when a brief prefix suffices and when continued intervention is needed.

Our work studies activation steering as a tool for bidirectional behavioral control, allowing target behaviors to be strengthened or suppressed. Such methods are dual use: they may help suppress undesirable behavior, but may also be used to strengthen harmful behavior or produce unintended changes in other capabilities. Our experiments evaluate these effects in controlled benchmark settings and do not establish that a steered model is safe or suitable for deployment. Applying these interventions therefore requires careful evaluation of both behavioral control and capability changes.

## A Experimental Details 

Concept-control datasets. Direction construction and evaluation use disjoint data. For each concept, we construct a DiM direction from 100 positive and 100 negative training sentences, using the residual-stream activation at the final prompt token. • Safety. We construct safety directions using LLM-LAT/benign-dataset as the positive set and 

LLM-LAT/harmful-dataset as the negative set [36], and evaluate safety behavior on Harm-Bench [21]. • Sentiment. We construct sentiment directions using the SST-2 training split and evaluate on its validation split [38]. • Politeness. We construct politeness directions using the PoliteGuard training split and evaluate on its test split [11]. We retain only the polite and impolite labels. 

Instruction-following tasks. We use MATH-500 [16] to test whether interventions at the beginning of generation can control final-answer formats after reasoning: “The answer is \boxed{} ” and “The answer is . . . ”. Both tasks share a partition of 50 problems for direction construction, 50 for intervention layer and strength selection, and 400 for evaluation, with no overlap. For each format and candidate layer, we construct a DiM direction [31] from final prompt token activations, subtracting the mean for problems without the format instruction from the mean for the same problems with it appended. 

16 Steering duration. Prefix Steering starts at the final prompt token and ends after the specified number of steered positions; full steering continues throughout generation. Single-token Prefix Steering modifies only the final prompt token. 

Intervention layer and strength. We reserve 50 test examples as a shared tuning set and exclude them from final evaluation. For each model–concept pair and intervention operator, we jointly select the intervention layer and strength on this tuning set. Within each operator, full steering and Prefix Steering use the same layer. We consider nine candidate layers at 10% , 20% , . . . , 90% of the model’s depth. We normalize each direction to unit ℓ2 norm. For additive steering, we use a coefficient c = s¯r,where s ∈ { 0.01 , 0.1, 1, 10 } and ¯r is the mean ℓ2 norm of residual-stream activations at the candidate layer over the positive and negative samples used for DiM construction. For COAST, we consider target angles θ ∈ { 10 ◦, 30 ◦, 50 ◦, 70 ◦, 90 ◦} between the steered hidden state and the steering direction, setting the cosine-similarity parameter to α = cos θ. We select the layer–strength combination maximizing concept score while retaining at least 80% of the unsteered general score. 

Generation and evaluation protocol. We use Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite as the LLM judge, with task-specific prompts described in Appendix C.1. We use greedy decoding with limits of 512 generated tokens for concept-control tasks, 1024 for MMLU-Pro, and 4096 for MATH-500, with the same decoding settings across methods within each task. For concept-control tasks, we evaluate only elicitation: safe behavior on HarmBench prompts [21], and positive sentiment and politeness on held-out negative-class inputs. For MATH-500, we evaluate adherence to the target final-answer format. We measure capability using MMLU-Pro multiple-choice accuracy [48] for concept-control tasks and MATH-500 answer accuracy for final-answer formatting.
