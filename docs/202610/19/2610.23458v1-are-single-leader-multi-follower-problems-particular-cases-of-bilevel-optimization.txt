Are Single-Leader-Multi-Follower Problems Particular Cases of Bilevel Optimization? 

Didier Aussel, David Salas, Martin Schmidt 

Abstract. For more than 20 years, single-leader multi-follower (SLMF) prob-lems have been treated as a more difficult class of problems in comparison to bilevel optimization. In this work we challenge this common belief, studying whether SLMF problems can be reformulated as equivalent bilevel optimization problems. We consider both optimistic and pessimistic formulations. For the optimistic case the answer is positive: We derive an equivalent bilevel problem by representing the followers’ equilibria as fixed points of the solution mapping of a single optimization problem. For pessimistic problems, the situ-ation is different. We show that every optimistic trilevel problem admits an equivalent pessimistic SLMF formulation with two followers. Conversely, for every pessimistic SLMF problem verifying a mild attainability assumption we establish an equivalent optimistic trilevel reformulation. The reformulations are easy, readily tractable, and they preserve convex and linear data. Thus, unless NP = Σ p 

> 2

, we deduce as byproduct that multiple followers induce an unexpected complexity gap between optimistic and pessimistic approaches, which is not the case when considering one follower alone. 

1. Introduction 

Bilevel optimization deals with optimization problems in which some variables are required to solve another optimization problem. These problems model hierar-chical interactions between two decision makers, the leader and the follower. The leader makes a decision first and anticipates the optimal response of the follower, whose problem depends on the leader’s decision. If several followers interact non-cooperatively at the lower level, one obtains a single-leader multi-follower (SLMF) problem. Each follower then solves an optimization problem that also depends on the strategies of the other followers. The resulting lower-level problem is a Nash equilibrium problem or, if the followers’ feasible sets depend on the other followers’ strategies, a generalized Nash equilibrium problem (GNEP). Hence, SLMF problems extend the usual single-leader single-follower (SLSF) setting of bilevel optimization. While simply quoted by Stackelberg (1934) in his pioneering work, such combina-tions of hierarchical optimization and equilibrium problems have been studied in the seminal work of Pang and Fukushima (2005), and can be traced back to Sherali et al. (1983). For an overview of single- and multi-leader-follower games, including existence results, reformulations, and solution methods, we refer to Aussel and Svensson (2020). A tutorial on solving SLMF problems using SOS1 reformulations is given in Aussel et al. (2025). A central issue of these models is that the followers’ equilibrium need not be unique. In the optimistic case, the leader selects the most favorable equilibrium among those satisfying the upper-level constraints. In the pessimistic case, the leader accounts for the worst equilibrium outcome and requires the coupling constraints to hold for every equilibrium. This distinction already

SLMF problems appear more difficult than their SLSF counterparts because the leader must anticipate a system of mutually dependent optimization problems. In general, minimizing the sum of the followers’ objectives over their jointly feasible strategies does not characterize the equilibria of the game. Moreover, convexity of each follower’s problem does not ensure convexity of the equilibrium set, and even uniqueness of each individual best response does not ensure uniqueness of the equilibrium. These properties make the analysis and solution of SLMF problems challenging. However, they do not settle whether SLMF problems are actually harder than bilevel problems. In particular, the question remains whether an SLMF problem can be reformulated as a bilevel problem while preserving properties such as convexity or linearity of the data. In recent years, several papers have shown that problem classes that are usually studied separately can be related by suitable reformulations. Zeng (2020) develops a scheme for pessimistic bilevel optimization based on a tight optimistic bilevel relaxation and a procedure for recovering a feasible optimal solution. This allows one to apply methods for optimistic bilevel optimization to pessimistic problems. For solvable bilevel problems with rational linear data, Henke et al. (2025) show that coupling constraints can be removed while preserving linearity and the globally optimal solutions after projection onto the original variables. Henke et al. (2026) prove corresponding results for pessimistic linear bilevel problems and also derive optimistic reformulations without coupling constraints. Finally, Lefebvre et al. (2025) show that, under suitable assumptions, GNEPs can be reformulated as Nash equilibrium problems by adding one player. These reformulations preserve linear or convex structure for the respective problem classes. The common idea of these works is to use additional variables or players to obtain a formulation that allows one to apply theory and methods from another problem class. In this paper, we study whether SLMF problems with a lower-level GNEP can be reformulated as bilevel optimization problems. We first consider the optimistic case. Inspired by the use of copied follower variables in Zeng (2020), we represent the followers’ equilibria as fixed points of the solution mapping of a single optimization problem. We then introduce a copy of the followers’ strategy vector at the upper level and link both copies by equality constraints. This yields an equivalent optimistic bilevel problem without any assumption on the structure of the problem. We show that the reformulation preserves convex and linear data. For explicitly encoded rational linear data, the reformulation can be constructed in polynomial time and has polynomial encoding length. For pessimistic SLMF problems, we derive an equivalent optimistic trilevel optimization problem under an attainability assumption on truncated constraint-violation functions. The truncation allows us to handle unbounded constraint violations, and we show that the attainability assumption always holds under linear data. We also prove the reverse result: every optimistic trilevel problem can be reformulated as a pessimistic SLMF problem. To this end, we encode the feasible region of the middle- and lower-level problems through a two-player GNEP and impose optimality by robust comparisons with its equilibria. Both reformulations preserve linear data and can be constructed in polynomial time with polynomial encoding length for rational linear data. We also discuss the assumptions and modifications needed to preserve convex data. Finally, we analyze the complexity of SLMF problems with rational linear data. 6. Conclusions 

In this paper, we studied the relationship between SLMF problems and bilevel optimization. We showed that every optimistic SLMF problem admits an equivalent optimistic bilevel reformulation. For pessimistic SLMF problems, we established reformulations to and from optimistic trilevel optimization, with an attainability assumption required in the forward direction. Under rational linear data, this assumption always holds and the reformulations preserve linearity and have poly-nomial encoding length. The associated decision problems are NP-complete in the optimistic case and Σp

> 2

-complete in the pessimistic case, even without coupling constraints and with only two followers. These results clarify when methods for bilevel optimization can be applied to SLMF problems and when an additional optimization level is needed. A natural next step would be to investigate the computational performance of the proposed reformulations. It is also of interest to identify subclasses of pessimistic SLMF problems that admit reformulations as linear bilevel problems despite the complexity obstruction for the general class.
