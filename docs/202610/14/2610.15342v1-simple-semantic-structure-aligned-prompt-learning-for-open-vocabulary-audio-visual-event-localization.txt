# SIMPLE: Semantic Structure-Aligned Prompt Learning for Open-Vocabulary Audio-Visual Event Localization 

Hanyu Xuan, Haoran Xu, Min Yin, Shuo Wang, Member, IEEE, Cheng Zhao, Kun Li, Member, IEEE, Fei Wang, Zhiliang Wu, Member, IEEE, Guanghui Yue, Senior Member, IEEE, Hehe Fan, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Open-vocabulary audio-visual event localization aims to infer explicit segment-level categories for both seen and unseen audio-visual events. Existing solutions rely heavily on hand-crafted prompt templates, whose predictions are highly sensitive to subtle wording variations. Learnable prompts remove this manual tuning burden, but when trained solely on seen events, they tend to overfit to seen categories and distort the inter-event semantic topology required for generalization to unseen categories, a failure we term semantic topology drift. In this paper, we propose SIMPLE, a Semantic Structure-alIgned ProMPt LEarning framework that aligns cross-category semantic topology between learnable and hand-crafted prompts across asynchronous audio and visual streams. Specifically, our SIM-PLE combines Learnable Continuous Prompt Tuning (LCPT) with Semantic Topology Consistency Regularization (STCR). The LCPT automates prompt engineering through data-driven optimization, while the STCR module aligns the category-wise similarity distributions of learnable and hand-crafted prompts under random category subset sampling to alleviate semantic topology drift. Comprehensive experiments and analyses on the OV-AVEBench demonstrate that our SIMPLE framework achieves state-of-the-art performance on the seen and unseen splits, while using roughly half the trainable parameters and roughly one-third the training time. 

Index Terms —open-vocabulary, prompt learning, cross-modal alignment, semantic topology drift.

Inspired by this human perceptual ability, the task of Audio-Visual Event Localization (AVEL) [3]–[26] has been proposed to simultaneously identify the semantic cate-gories and temporal boundaries of audio-visual events within untrimmed videos. In contrast to uni-modal machine percep-tion [27], AVEL requires joint modeling of asynchronous audio and visual streams to predict both event categories and their temporal intervals, making it a fundamental yet challenging problem in multi-modal video understanding [28]– [31]. Since the pioneering work of Tian et al. [3], a wide range of methods [4]–[7], [9]–[11], [13]–[21], [23]–[25] have been proposed. Although these methods have achieved promising performance, they are inherently limited to closed-set task settings. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), such methods can recog-nize only the audio-visual event categories encountered during training, which severely hinders their generalization to test videos containing unseen audio-visual event categories. To mitigate this limitation, Yu et al. [32] investigated the AVEL task under an open-set setting. To the best of our knowledge, this remains the only study dedicated to this particular problem setting. Specifically, open-set AVEL is capable of inferring novel audio-visual events not observed during training and recognizing these unseen events as the “unknown ” category, instead of assigning them to pre-defined known event categories. Nevertheless, as depicted in Fig.1(b), this open-set approach [32] is unable to infer the fine-grained semantic categories of these unseen events. To further alleviate this bottleneck, several studies [33]–[37] have explored the AVEL task under the zero-shot setting. Notably, as shown in Fig. 1(c), existing zero-shot AVEL methods [33]–[37] operate at the whole-video level, predicting only a single event category per video. Consequently, they forego fine-grained temporal boundary estimation and thus cannot localize audio-visual events to their respective temporal segments. To bridge this research gap, Zhou et al. Compared with the closed-set setting, open-vocabulary AVEL exhibits stronger generaliza-tion to unseen categories. Distinct from the open-set setting [32], it predicts concrete semantic labels for unseen events instead of assigning them a generic unknown tag. In contrast to the whole-video zero-shot setting [33]–[37], open-vocabulary AVEL supports fine-grained, segment-level temporal localiza-tion of audio-visual events. Specifically, to enable open-vocabulary recognition, Zhou et al. [38] constructed hand-crafted textual descriptions for each candidate audio-visual event category. These fixed tex-tual prompts are then fed into the text encoder of a pre-trained Multi-modal Large Language Model (MLLM) [39]– [44]. Final category predictions are generated via dynamic similarity matching across audio, visual, and text embeddings, enabling the model to classify arbitrary event categories [45]. Nevertheless, we argue that this strategy rooted in hand-crafted prompt templates for in-context learning [46] exhibits sev-eral inherent limitations. As demonstrated in Fig.2, the pre-dictions generated using such prompts are highly sensitive to minor wording variations, which can lead to substantial performance fluctuations [47]. Despite extensive trial-and-error tuning, identifying a universally optimal prompt template remains an intractable challenge. Moreover, these static textual descriptions lack the flexibility to adaptively capture complex audio-visual semantics [48]. To address these limitations, we introduce Learnable Con-tinuous Prompt Tuning (LCPT). Building upon CoOp [48], LCPT generalizes continuous prompt tuning from uni-modal image classification to bi-modal temporal event localization in the open-vocabulary scenario, while freezing the parameters of pre-trained MLLMs. It parameterizes the context components of prompts as learnable continuous vectors, rather than discrete text tokens whose embeddings remain fixed during down-stream prompt tuning. This design automates prompt engineer-ing via data-driven gradient-based optimization, and harnesses the rich pre-trained knowledge encoded within MLLMs for task-specific cross-modal alignment [49]. Unlike CoOp [48], which optimizes the context exclusively for visual input, our LCPT learns a unified context across asynchronous audio and visual streams. However, under the open-vocabulary setting, learnable prompts trained exclusively on seen event categories ex-hibit limited generalization ability to unseen event categories. In contrast, hand-crafted prompts maintain stable and con-sistent semantic distributions across different categories, as they are free from task-specific parameter updates and can faithfully preserve the intrinsic semantic topology embedded in pre-trained MLLMs. Motivated by these crucial observa-tions, we introduce Semantic Topology Consistency Regu-larization (STCR) to mitigate semantic topology drift and boost cross-category generalization capability. Specifically, we first leverage Qwen2-7B [53] to generate category names for synthetic novel events, which serve as simulated unseen events throughout training. Notably, all synthetic categories are generated entirely without access to test-set information and strictly verified to be fully disjoint from the benchmark’s real unseen categories, eliminating any risk of test-set information leakage. For both seen and synthetic novel categories, the STCR module aligns the pairwise similarity relationships between text embeddings from learnable prompts with those from hand-crafted prompts. By constraining and preserving the intrinsic cross-category semantic topology, STCR facilitates effective semantic transfer from seen categories to simulated unseen categories, which naturally generalizes to real unseen categories during inference and thus boosts the generalization performance of open-vocabulary AVEL task. A. Audio-Visual Event Localization 

AVEL aims to temporally localize and classify events in untrimmed videos by jointly exploiting audio and visual modalities. As shown in Fig. 1, existing AVEL methods fall into four distinct settings: 

1) Closed-set AVEL: Since Tian et al. [3] first formalized AVEL task, most existing studies have focused on the closed-set assumption. Existing AVEL approaches can be broadly classified into three categories: 

Audio-visual fusion : A core challenge in AVEL lies in ef-fectively integrating complementary information across audio-visual modalities. Wu et al. [4] proposed dual-attention match-ing to achieve bidirectional alignment of audio and visual features. Xu et al. [5] integrated audio-guided spatial-channel attention [54] with relation-aware aggregation to emphasize event-relevant visual regions. Duan et al. [6] iteratively fed the fused audiovisual representations into both modalities to enable progressive multi-modal fusion. Xuan et al. [7] designed a discriminative cross-modality attention [55] net-work to address temporal inconsistency in AVEL. Shen et al. [9] further explored fine-grained audiovisual interactions to facilitate adaptive feature fusion. Zhao et al. [10] incorporated event-level semantic information to strengthen multi-modal event representation and modeling. Shaar et al. [11] developed a training-free score-level fusion framework equipped with a within-video label-shift adjustment. Zhang et al. [13] modeled bidirectional temporal dependencies and transferred CLIP-based image-text knowledge to the audiovisual event domain. 

Interference suppressing : A complementary research thrust aims to disentangle foreground events from background in-terference. Xia et al. [14] applied dual-level cross-modal suppression across temporal and event dimensions. Zhou et al. [15] identified highly relevant audio-visual pairs via similarity-based propagation to learn more discriminative event repre-sentations. Departing from conventional strategies that treat

Multi-modal temporal modeling : Another research stream focuses on precise temporal prediction and coherent segment modeling for audio-visual events. Wu et al. [24] further enhanced event-level temporal consistency through multi-modal feature fusion and adaptive continuity sampling. Zhou et al. [25] extended AVEL to dense local-ization within long untrimmed videos, leveraging cross-modal consistency and multi-temporal granularity collaboration to achieve finer-grained timestamp prediction. 

2) Open-set AVEL: Under the open-set setting, models are required not only to recognize events from known categories, but also to identify unseen events and treat them as “unknown” rather than forcibly assigning them to predefined classes. To date, this setting remains largely unexplored in the AVEL lit-erature. To the best of our knowledge, Yu et al. [32] presented the sole work addressing this challenge. They proposed an ev-idential learning-based framework that quantifies classification uncertainty, enabling effective separation of unknown events from both known events and background interference.

[35] embedded audio-visual semantics into hyperbolic space to better capture the hierarchical structure of unseen cat-egories. Mo et al. Despite these advances, existing methods primarily target video-level recognition. 

4) Open-vocabulary AVEL: Open-vocabulary AVEL de-fines a more general paradigm, where models are required to temporally localize events and predict semantic labels for both seen and unseen event categories. Zhou et al. [38] pioneered this task and constructed the OV-AVEBench benchmark. Yu et al. [57] further extended this paradigm to dense event localization within long untrimmed videos. They presented a one-stage framework that jointly generated foreground pro-posals and conducted semantic-aware classification for unseen events. Yang et al. [58] exploited heterogeneous-graph-based hyperbolic representations to model segment- and video-level hierarchical semantics, improving cross-modal consistency and generalization for unseen categories. Cho et al. [59] explored decision-layer adaptation with frozen multi-modal encoders, adopting agent-discovered symbolic rules and video-aware in-ference policies to boost seen-unseen classification calibration. 

B. Prompt-based Learning 

Prompt-based learning [60] adapts frozen MLLMs, such as CLIP [61], via task-oriented prompt conditioning, cov-ering both training-free in-context inference and learnable soft-prompt tuning schemes. 

1) In-context Learning: Several studies [38], [50], [51], [62] employ hand-crafted prompts for in-context learning [46] to establish semantic connections across text, audio, and visual modalities. As summarized in the notes of Fig.2, these hand-crafted templates cover single-modality descriptions for audio or video, as well as cross-modal formulations that describe audio-visual events and their contextual co-occurrence. Such fixed textual templates serve as guidance for MLLMs to perform semantic matching and localization of target events 

[event i]. Nevertheless, all hand-crafted templates suffer from a shared limitation: their performance depends on how well their wording matches the encoder’s pre-training distribution. Manually engineered prompts are inherently brittle, and no single template yields optimal performance across all event categories and datasets. 

2) Prompt Tuning: Prompt tuning [63] optimizes lightweight soft-prompt embeddings while freezing pre-trained encoders, providing a parameter-efficient paradigm for adapting MLLMs to downstream tasks. Zhou et al. [48] replaced hand-crafted prompts with a set of learnable context vectors optimized on few-shot labeled samples. While achieving substantial performance gains on seen classes, this approach suffers from overfitting to training classes and degraded generalization on unseen classes. Zhou et al. [64] conditioned prompts on image features to generate image-adaptive prompts, which yield improved generalization toward unseen classes. Khattak et al. [65], [66] extended adaptability to downstream tasks by adding prompts to both text and image inputs. Hanif et al. [51] learned prompt context within the text feature space instead of the input token embedding space. A variety of regularization techniques have been investi-gated to learn generalizable prompts. Zhu et al. [67] avoided gradient conflicts with CLIP’s general knowledge via orthogo-nal projection of prompt-tuning gradients. Yao et al. [68] min-imized the distance between text embeddings from learnable and hand-crafted prompts to preserve the general knowledge. Zhang et al. [69] regarded hand-crafted and learnable prompts as dual views and fused task-specific and general semantics via a mutual-information estimator. Chen et al. [70] maximized the Pearson correlation coefficient between pairwise similarity matrices of original and learnable text embeddings to preserve class topology. Bulat et al. [71] introduced a text-to-text cross-entropy loss to align embeddings from learnable and hand-crafted prompts of the same class. Cho et al. [72] en-forced uniform text embedding distribution on a hyper-sphere to reduce inter-class overlap and cluster same-class image embeddings for cross-modal alignment. Jung and Lee [52] introduced similarity alignment regularization to improve the generalization of CLIP-based prompt learning by preserving inter-class semantic relationships. III. M ETHODOLOGY

A. Experimental Setting 1) Dataset: We conduct comprehensive experiments on the OV-AVEBench benchmark [38], a benchmark tailored for the open-vocabulary AVEL task. It removes highly similar and rarely-occurring real-world events from the original 309 VGGSound [77] event categories and retains 67 common real-world event categories. These events cover five major semantic groups: human activities, animal activities, musical instruments, vehicles, and other real-life audio-visual scenes. The dataset contains 24,800 videos in total, with 13,182 samples for training, 5,798 for validation, and 5,820 for testing. Under the open-vocabulary setting, 46 out of the 67 categories serve as seen event categories available during training, while the remaining 21 unseen categories appear only in the validation and testing phases. To rigorously evaluate the model’s generalization capability toward unseen event categories, the ratio of videos containing seen events to those containing unseen events is kept at roughly 3 : 7 within both validation and test splits. 

2) Evaluation Metrics: To guarantee reproducibility and fair comparison, we adopt the same seen/unseen split, data partitions, and evaluation protocol as the original baseline study [38]. Concretely, four metrics are used to assess per-formance on seen, unseen, and total test splits: segment-level accuracy (Acc .), segment-level F1-score (Seg .), event-level F1-score ( Eve .), and their average ( Avg .). Acc . quantifies the proportion of correctly classified one-second segments. Seg .is the harmonic mean of precision and recall over all test segments, where each segment is treated as an independent sample. Eve . evaluates performance at the event instance level. Consecutive segments with identical predictions are merged into event proposals. A prediction is marked as a true positive if its IoU with the corresponding GT event exceeds 0.5. 3) Implementation Details: All extracted embeddings are projected into a shared 1024-dimensional space. Each modal-specific temporal layer uses a hidden dimension of 256, one attention head, a feed-forward network with an intermediate size of 512, a dropout rate of 0.1, and post-layer normalization. We insert P = 16 learnable context vectors initialized from 

N (0 , 0.02) after the start-of-sequence token. In total, the number of trainable parameters is approximately 4.2M. To enhance semantic diversity for regularization, we incorporate 

Cn = 170 synthetic novel event category names. We fix the random sampling size K in Eq. 5 to 64 and the regularization weight λ in Eq. 8 to 0.1. For model training, we employ the Adam optimizer with 

β1 = 0 .9 and β2 = 0 .999 . The initial learning rate is set to 

5 × 10 −5, paired with a weight decay coefficient of 5 × 10 −4.Training is conducted with a batch size of 16, and gradient clipping is applied with a maximum norm of 0.8. We adopt a multi-step scheduler that decays the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 at epochs 10, 20, and 30, respectively. The model is trained for up to 36 epochs, with early stopping triggered based on the Avg. metric evaluated on the validation split (5,798 videos covering all 67 seen and unseen categories). All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24GB memory.

D. Mechanism Analysis 

We provide cross-validated evidence for prompt-induced semantic topology drift from two complementary analytical perspectives. Consistent observations from both Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 reveal that hand-crafted prompts preserve intrinsic semantic structure of the category space. In contrast, uncon-strained learnable prompts induce semantic topology drift and disrupt this structure. Our proposed STCR largely restores these required semantic relationships, which is crucial for open-vocabulary generalization. More results and analyses are provided in Supplementary Materials. 

1) Semantic Topology Structure Analysis: As visualized in Fig. 6, hand-crafted prompts yield a stable semantic structure and preserve reasonable local-neighbor relationships across five semantic groups. For instance, the category of ambulance siren lies close to fire-truck siren while remaining distant from 

motorboat . Such consistent semantic patterns are also observed for the other four semantic groups. In contrast, learnable-only prompts severely distort this topological organization. For the five annotated triplets, semantically related categories are re-pelled from their anchor, while weakly related ones are drawn nearer. With the proposed regularization, semantic ordering is substantially improved, and the embedding topology moves closer to the hand-crafted prompt. 

2) Pairwise Semantic Relation Analysis: Fig. 7 exam-ines pairwise semantic relations for five representative semantically-associated category pairs within the affinity space. Under hand-crafted prompts, semantically-matched pairs yield high similarity values, such as fire truck siren 

and ambulance siren , people crowd and people clapping ,

playing bass guitar and playing electric guitar , and child speech and female speech . Learnable-only prompting weakens their directed pairwise similarities, while several irrelevant off-diagonal entries achieve higher similarity values. Our STCR partially recovers these degraded pairwise similarities.

V. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presents a lightweight semantic structure-aligned prompt learning framework tailored for open-vocabulary audio-visual event localization, which preserves meaningful inter-category semantic relationships by aligning the under-lying semantic topology. Our framework integrates a learn-able continuous prompt tuning module for automated prompt engineering and data-driven cross-modal alignment, as well as a semantic topology consistency regularization module to alleviate the semantic projection shift problem.
