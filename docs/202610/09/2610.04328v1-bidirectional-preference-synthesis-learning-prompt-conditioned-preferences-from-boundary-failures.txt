Abstract 

Correction-based offline preference pipelines commonly treat model failures only as rejected responses under the original prompt. This supervision is incomplete for boundary failures :responses that violate the given instruction yet coherently sat-isfy a nearby intent or constraint setting. We introduce Bidi-rectional Preference Synthesis (BPS), a data-construction method for standard DPO that makes this missing prompt de-pendence explicit. For each validated boundary failure, BPS keeps the conventional forward pair under the original prompt and adds a reverse pair under a synthesized achieved prompt ,so the same response is rejected where it is wrong and chosen where it is right, without changing the DPO objective, training a reward model, or requiring online sampling. On Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, BPS preserves original-side pairwise ranking while raising achieved-side ranking accuracy from 6.8% to 62.3% on held-out crossed anchors, with a similar shift under a Kimi-K2.6 cross-teacher probe. A blind human audit supports the intended reverse preference direction, and downstream evaluations show the clearest separation from Forward-DPO in multilingual multi-turn instruction following, with consis-tent capability-retention patterns on agentic/tool-use and code checks. 

# Introduction 

A rejected response in preference data is not always bad in itself. Consider a prompt asking for a Chinese answer in exactly two bullet points, with a required keyword and separator. A model may produce a fluent, factually correct Chinese answer, but use three bullets or omit the separator. Under the original prompt, this response should lose to a correction. Yet the same response could be better under a prompt whose constraints match what the model produced. We call such near misses boundary failures : outputs that violate the given prompt but coherently satisfy a related in-tent or constraint setting. They arise naturally in instruction-following tasks with verifiable constraints (Zhou et al. 2023; Pyatkin et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2024), where a response can solve the task while missing a format, lexical, language, or structural requirement. The same structure appears beyond surface constraints: a tool argument can be wrong for one request but correct for a related request, and a multilingual pattern can be inappropriate in one turn but useful in an-other (He et al. 2024; Qin et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2024). Boundary failures are therefore not merely negative exam-ples; they expose where preference should change with the prompt. Offline preference optimization methods such as DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023) and its variants (Azar et al. 2024; Ethayarajh et al. 2024; Meng, Xia, and Chen 2024; Hong, Lee, and Thorne 2024) are widely used because they avoid reward-model RLHF pipelines (Christiano et al. 2017; Sti-ennon et al. 2020; Ouyang et al. 2022) and online PPO-style rollouts. Their effect, however, is determined by the pref-erence pairs they receive. A typical pipeline samples failed rollouts, obtains corrected responses through rejection sam-pling (Touvron et al. 2023) or self-correction (Zelikman et al. 2022; Madaan et al. 2023), and trains on pairs where the correction is preferred under the original prompt. We call this forward-only supervision because each failure is used only as rejected under its original prompt. This is the right signal when the rejected response is incoherent, unsafe, or factually wrong. For a boundary failure, it records only that the response lost under Porig , not whether the rejection is prompt-specific. This missing context can matter even though DPO scores responses conditionally on the prompt. Because prompts share model parameters, repeatedly observing a coherent re-sponse mode only as rejected may influence its relative score in other contexts where that mode remains useful. We there-fore study boundary failures as a data-construction problem: preference data can encode not only which response is wrong, but also where it would be right. We propose Bidirectional Preference Synthesis (BPS), an offline construction method for standard DPO. # Related Work 

Preference optimization and DPO failure modes. Online RLHF methods train reward models from preference com-parisons and optimize policies with RL-style updates (Chris-tiano et al. 2017; Stiennon et al. 2020; Ouyang et al. 2022), but require rollouts, reward queries, and stability tuning. Di-rect or offline objectives such as SLiC-HF, RRHF, DPO, IPO-style objectives, KTO, SimPO, and ORPO (Zhao et al. 2023; Yuan et al. 2023; Rafailov et al. 2023; Azar et al. 2024; Ethayarajh et al. 2024; Meng, Xia, and Chen 2024; Hong, Lee, and Thorne 2024) avoid online RL, making data construction central: behavior depends on which responses are rejected under which prompts. Prior work shows that DPO can reduce loss by displacing likelihood away from rejected responses rather than learning calibrated prefer-ences (Razin et al. 2025); DPO-positive/Smaug addresses related objective-level issues (Pal et al. 2024). BPS is com-plementary: it keeps standard DPO but changes the data se-mantics by asking when a rejected response should also be positive. 

Reverse and input-side preference construction. RPO constructs reverse preference pairs by modifying instruction constraints so that an imperfect response becomes preferred on a related task (Huang et al. 2025). IOPO evaluates the same output under different inputs to make preference learn-ing more input-conditioned (Zhang et al. 2025b). BPS is closest to this line, but ties each reverse pair to an observed boundary failure: it starts from a student response that failed 

Porig , synthesizes a standalone prompt for what the response achieved, and keeps the reverse label only after validation. Unlike mechanical constraint reversal, BPS keeps the forward and achieved-prompt reverse pairs as a crossed anchor over the same responses. We use an RPO-style construction as a strong baseline to isolate the value of semantically matched achieved prompts. 

Data construction, self-correction, and hindsight relabel-ing. Offline pipelines use filtering, teacher feedback, re-jection sampling (Touvron et al. 2023), STaR (Zelikman et al. 2022), Self-Refine (Madaan et al. 2023), AI feed-back (Lee et al. 2024), or self-rewarding methods to pro-duce improved responses. BPS uses this correction signal but also asks whether a failure reveals a nearby instruction it already satisfies. This connects to hindsight experience re-play (Andrychowicz et al. 2017) and HIR (Zhang et al. 2023), but BPS does not replace failure with success: it preserves re-jection under the original prompt and preference only under a validated achieved prompt. 

Instruction following and tool use. Instruction tuning and synthetic instruction generation show that task spec-ifications shape model behavior (Wei et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022, 2023). Fine-grained instruction-following bench-marks (Zhou et al. 2023; Pyatkin et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2024) expose partial success over language, format, lexi-cal, and task-content constraints, while multilingual multi-turn benchmarks (He et al. 2024) test cross-turn stability. Tool-use and agentic methods and benchmarks (Schick et al. 2023; Patil et al. 2024; Yao et al. 2024; Qin et al. 2023; Patil et al. 2025) expose the same structure in action spaces, where an argument can be wrong for one request but correct for a nearby request. These settings naturally test prompt-conditioned preference learning.

Forward Execution Anchor 

The forward anchor records the ordinary correction signal while keeping the corrected response student-generated. For each candidate boundary failure (Porig , τ fail ), the teacher pro-duces a structured correction hint h rather than a replacement answer, and the student generates a correction conditioned on the original prompt, the failed response, and the hint: 

τperfect ∼ πθ0 (· | Porig , τ fail , h ) (2) During this retry, τfail is evidence for repair, not an instruction source. A correction is accepted only if it is non-degenerate and achieves r(Porig , τ perfect ) = 1 . The resulting forward pair is 

Porig : τperfect ≻ τfail .

In our main instantiation, this process yields 1,443 forward pairs. 

Reverse Intent Anchor 

The reverse anchor is added only when the failure can safely be interpreted as a correct response to another truthful in-tent. We use a conservative pipeline: outcome-summary el-igibility, achieved-prompt synthesis, and cycle-consistency validation. Failures that do not pass remain forward-only. 

Outcome-summary eligibility. Before generating any achieved prompt, the teacher summarizes what τfail accom-plishes and labels it eligible , forward-only , or reject-all . El-igible failures must be coherent, substantially complete, and mainly attributable to instruction mismatch (e.g., language, format, length, structure, or keyword constraints). We ex-clude factual, mathematical, or logical errors; hallucinations; unsafe content; degeneration; incoherence; and incomplete task content. 

Achieved prompt synthesis. For each eligible failure, the teacher synthesizes a standalone achieved prompt Pachieved 

under which τfail should be correct. This prompt cannot make the failure correct by merely deleting constraints; it must re-place violated constraints with the content, language, format, tone, length, and structure actually exhibited by τfail , while avoiding leakage, false content, meta-evaluation wording, or copy requests. We audit naturalness and leakage in the sup-plementary material. 

Cycle-consistency validation. A separate teacher judge validates the completed crossed anchor under Pachieved . A reverse pair is accepted only if the achieved prompt is non-leaky, τfail is coherent, truthful, safe, and complete, τperfect is less appropriate under Pachieved , and the preference is clear. Accepted examples form the reverse pair 

Pachieved : τfail ≻ τperfect .

We prefer precision over coverage: 282 of 316 candidates pass validation (89.2%). Prompt-template summaries and the validation rubric are provided in the supplementary material.

# Experiments and Analysis 

Setup 

Model. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Yang et al. 2025) as the student and a frozen copy as the DPO reference policy. Gemini-3.1-pro-preview is used only for offline hint generation, achieved-prompt synthesis, and validation. 

Data. Seed prompts are drawn from IFBench-train (Py-atkin et al. 2025); evaluation prompts are excluded, and our n-gram overlap checks find no exact or near-duplicate gen-erated Pachieved prompts in the evaluated prompt sets. After dual-temperature rollouts and filtering, we obtain 1,443 for-ward pairs and 282 reverse pairs (1,725 total, ρ ≈ 0.195 ). About 71% of initial failures have r ≥ 0.5.

Teacher usage and quality control. The teacher is not used for downstream scoring. The main construction run uses about 2,500 teacher calls / 5M tokens. Strict valida-tion accepts 282 of 316 pre-filtered reverse candidates and discards ambiguous cases. The Gemini held-out crossed-anchor probe is therefore treated as a reward-geometry di-agnostic rather than teacher-independent generalization; we add a Kimi-K2.6 synthesized-and-validated probe as a cross-teacher check. 

Training. All models follow the same standard-DPO train-ing configuration ( β = 0 .1) and three-epoch schedule; the main comparison uses the common step-100 checkpoint, matching the number of optimizer updates. Full hyperpa-rameters are in the supplementary material. 

Baselines. Base : Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 without further training. Forward-DPO (Step 60 / Step 100): Trained on 

Dforward only. Fwd-100 is the checkpoint matched to the main BPS comparison; Fwd-60 is an earlier checkpoint evalu-ated in the supplementary material. BPS (BPS-100): Trained on Dforward ∪ D reverse , with additional checkpoints used for training-dynamics analysis (Section ). Shuffled-Rev (Shuf): An ablation that keeps each reverse response pair and label direction but randomly permutes achieved prompts across examples, used only in analysis (Section ). Constraint-Rev 

(C-Rev): A strong RPO-inspired construction baseline that uses the same boundary-failure pool and reverse-pair budget as BPS, but creates reverse prompts by mechanically modi-fying violated constraints in the original prompt rather than synthesizing the achieved intent. 

Benchmarks. We evaluate instruction following (IF-Bench (Pyatkin et al. 2025), IFEval (Zhou et al. 2023), FollowBench (Jiang et al. 2024), Multi-IF (He et al. 2024)), reasoning and math (GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al. 2023), MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al. 2021)), code (Hu-manEval+ (Chen et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2023a), MBPP (Austin et al. 2021)), Chinese language (C-Eval (Huang et al. 2023)), agent/tool use (tau-bench (Yao et al. 2024), tau2-bench (Bar-res et al. 2025), ToolBench (Qin et al. 2023)), and broad rea-soning (MMLU-Pro (Wang et al. 2024), BBH (Suzgun et al. 2023)). FollowBench reports CSL/HSR/SSR in English and Chinese; Multi-IF averages eight languages over three turns. Full metrics and evaluation details are in the supplementary material. All models use the same decoding configuration and official/default evaluators. We focus on both downstream behavior and learned prefer-ence geometry. Section reports a separate function-calling instantiation of the BPS pipeline; because it uses different boundary data and checkpoint selection, its numbers are not mixed with the main IFBench-trained comparisons.

Function-calling pipeline instantiation. Density sensitivity shows the expected trade-off: ρ = 0 .10 gives a smaller correction, whereas ρ = 0 .30 reduces IFBench; the validation-determined ρ ≈ 0.195 lies between them (supple-mentary material). This interpretation aligns with the ablations: reverse la-bels work best when the prompt captures the response mode expressed by the failure. BPS therefore records correction and preservation as prompt-dependent facts about the same response pair. 

No label contradiction. Preferences are over prompt-response pairs: τperfect ≻ τfail under Porig , while τfail ≻ τperfect 

under Pachieved . BPS therefore teaches a prompt-conditioned boundary, not contradictory labels. 

Boundary failure selection. Near misses ( r ≥ 0.5) are suitable reverse candidates because they often remain co-herent despite localized constraint mismatches; 71% of our boundary failures satisfy this criterion. Most validated an-chors involve format/structure or lexical mismatches, and BPS raises achieved-side ranking accuracy across all ob-served types (supplementary material). 

Verbosity. Forward-DPO is also associated with substan-tially longer outputs: IFBench average length rises from 1,584 tokens (Base) to 5,920, while BPS is 2,565; on Tool-Bench, BPS stays closer to Base. This pattern is consis-tent with length being used as a constraint-coverage strat-egy (Dubois et al. 2025); full statistics are in the supplemen-tary material. 

# Conclusion 

Forward-only preference data can treat coherent boundary failures as intrinsically negative. BPS instead adds validated achieved-prompt reverse anchors, encoding where the same response is wrong and where it is right without changing the DPO objective. Across held-out reward probes and downstream evalua-tions, BPS counteracts cross-prompt rejection patterns while preserving the original preference boundary. Its clearest downstream separation appears in multilingual multi-turn in-struction following, with consistent capability-retention ev-idence from tool-use, agentic, and code checks. The results support encoding not only which responses are wrong, but also the prompts under which they are useful. 

# Supplementary Material Limitations 

Achieved-intent representation. BPS represents the achieved intent as a natural-language prompt so that the re-sulting data can be used by standard preference-optimization pipelines without changing the model architecture or DPO objective. In domains with formal action spaces, such as function calling or tool use, implementations may also store schemas or constraint lists alongside the achieved prompt for easier inspection. 

Offline construction. BPS shifts effort from online rein-forcement learning to offline data construction. Our imple-mentation uses teacher calls for correction hints, achieved-prompt synthesis, and validation, plus an optional human audit. These steps are independent of DPO training and can be batched, cached, or replaced with cheaper verifier models in larger runs. 

Domain adaptation. The same BPS recipe applies to both instruction following and function calling in our experi-ments, but each domain benefits from validation criteria that match its output structure. Instruction-following data uses constraint satisfaction and leakage checks, while function-calling data also checks parseability, schema validity, func-tion identity, and argument values. 

Objective scope. We study BPS with standard DPO to iso-late the effect of crossed boundary-failure pairs. The same data construction could be combined with objective-level im-provements such as DPO-positive/Smaug, RPO/IOPO-style variants, or other preference-optimization losses. 

# Ethical Statement 

This work studies an offline data-construction method for preference optimization. We do not use private user conver-sations or data from deployed systems. The only newly col-lected human judgments are task-focused annotations used for the achieved-prompt audit; the annotation task contains no personal or sensitive user content. The seed prompts, evaluation benchmarks, and model checkpoints used in this study are public research artifacts, and we use them only for research purposes under their respective licenses and terms of use. We do not redistribute third-party benchmark test sets or model weights. BPS constructs additional preference pairs by synthesiz-ing achieved prompts for boundary failures. Our pipeline keeps reverse pairs only after eligibility, safety, truthfulness, and non-leakage validation, and discards ambiguous cases. These filters are intended to prevent private, unsafe, hateful, deceptive, hallucinated, or factually incorrect outputs from being preserved as positives. For multilingual, culturally sen-sitive, or safety-relevant data, additional audit can be applied using the same blind-review protocol used in our experi-ments. Our experiments involve offline teacher-model inference and GPU fine-tuning. We report teacher-call and compute estimates to support reproducibility and to make the con-struction cost transparent.

# Human Audit of Achieved Prompts 

We conduct a blind audit of 200 held-out crossed anchors, split evenly between the Gemini-source and Kimi-source probes. Each anchor is judged by three annotators (600 to-tal judgments). For each anchor, annotators are shown the achieved prompt and two randomly ordered responses, with-out seeing the teacher label or whether a response is τfail 

or τperfect . They judge whether the achieved prompt is fully natural, whether τfail satisfies it, which response is preferred under the achieved prompt, and whether the prompt leaks response content. Table 21 reports per-anchor majority-vote results. Table 21 shows that the central supervision signal—the achieved-prompt preference direction—is highly reliable un-der blind audit: annotators prefer τfail under Pachieved in 99.0% of audited anchors, leakage is rare (0.5%), and 91.0% of achieved prompts are judged natural user requests by majority vote. At the annotator-judgment level, naturalness is 87.2% overall (523/600), with 95.7% for Gemini-source prompts and 78.7% for Kimi-source prompts. The stricter joint ac-ceptance measure is necessarily lower because it requires all four criteria simultaneously; importantly, reverse-preference agreement remains 100% for Gemini-source and 98% for Kimi-source anchors. Prompts judged less natural typically inherit explicit surface constraints such as exact paragraph counts, required keywords, forbidden words, fixed capitaliza-tion, or prescribed separators, rather than leaking response content. Thus, the audit strongly supports the reverse pref-erence direction and indicates that most achieved prompts remain plausible standalone requests.
