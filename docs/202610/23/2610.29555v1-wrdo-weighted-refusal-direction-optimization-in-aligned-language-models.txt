Innopolis University Aligned large language models are trained to comply with benign requests while refusing harmful ones. However, recent work shows that this refusal behavior is fragile and can often be disrupted by simple activation- or weight-space interventions. In particular, refusal has been shown to be largely mediated by a single residual-stream direction, typically computed as a difference-of-means vector between harmful and harmless prompts. However, this standard construction aggregates heterogeneous harmful prompts into one uniform average, potentially conflating distinct refusal-related phenomena. In this work, we study whether refusal interventions can be strengthened by learning how to combine prompt-specific refusal directions. We propose Weighted Refusal Direction Optimization (WRDO), a procedure that constructs a composite refusal direction from a bank of per-prompt directions using learned aggregation weights. This approach allows different harmful prompts to contribute unequally to the final intervention direction. We evaluate WRDO against prior single-direction methods on three open-weight language models using three guard judges and HarmBench as an external evaluator. We further measure the effect of the intervention on general model capability through standard utility benchmarks. Finally, we investigate a defense-oriented setting in which the same optimization machinery is used in reverse to restore refusal behavior in an abliterated model checkpoint. Our results show that weighted aggregation provides a more effective and flexible mechanism for manipulating refusal representations. Code is available at https://github.com/brain-lab-research/WRDO 

1 Introduction 

Large language models deployed in user-facing settings are aligned to follow benign instructions and to refuse harmful ones, with this behavior shaped by a combination of supervised fine-tuning, instruction tuning and preference optimization [1, 2]. The resulting refusal mechanism is, in practice, brittle under both prompt-level adversarial attacks [3, 4] and direct interventions on the model itself, which has motivated a mechanistic line of work that asks how refusal is represented internally and which components of the model implement it. A central finding of this line is that refusal is, to a first approximation, mediated by a single direction in the residual stream of the transformer [5]: ablating one difference-of-means vector, computed between activations on harmful and harmless prompts at a chosen layer, is sufficient to disable refusal in a broad set of open-weight aligned models. The same work establishes an equivalence between activation ablation and weight-space orthogonalization of the attention output and feed-forward down projections, which makes the intervention a one-shot edit of the model with no inference-time overhead, and public toolkits such as Heretic [6] operationalize this recipe end-to-end. The standard procedure averages every harmful prompt into a single axis. A disinformation prompt, a malware prompt and a privacy-violation prompt all enter the same mean, and the resulting direction is diffuse in the sense 1arXiv:2610.29555v1 [cs.LG] 21 Oct 2026 that it conflates phenomena that need not be aligned in the residual stream. A natural alternative is to retain a per-prompt direction ri for each training prompt and to combine them with a coefficient vector w. The simplest combination is the uniform average, r = normalize( P

ri/N ), but there is no a priori reason for every prompt to contribute equally. Performance-aware weighted aggregation is known to beat uniform averaging whenever the aggregated components differ in quality, from ensembling ranked classifiers [7] to federated averaging [8] and adapter selection [9], and per-prompt refusal directions are heterogeneous in exactly that way. In this work, we research the question of whether computing refusal direction using the aggregation weights yields a stronger intervention than the single-direction recipe. 

Our Contributions: 

• We propose Weighted Refusal Direction Optimization (WRDO), a procedure that obtains a refusal direction by learning aggregation weights w over a bank of per-prompt directions, together with two concrete instantiations of WRDO based on separable CMA-ES and a proposed GRPO variant with importance sampling (GRPO-IS). • We show empirically that the proposed WRDO instantiations outperform all considered prior single-direction baselines across three open-weight models and three guard judges, and that this superiority transfers to HarmBench ruling out judge-specific overfitting. • We obtain that WRD-GRPO-IS preserves the general capability of the intervened model substantially better than every other considered method on standard utility benchmarks. • We additionally study a defense setup that reuses the same machinery in reverse to restore refusal on an abliterated checkpoint, illustrating an alternative use of the proposed approach. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 formalizes the WRDO problem. Section 3 presents the two WRDO instantiations introduced in this work, namely an adaptation of separable CMA-ES and a proposed GRPO variant with importance sampling, together with the three single-direction baselines against which they are compared. Section 4 specifies the experimental setup, and Sections 5–6 report the empirical results, ablations and discussion.

Heretic. We additionally include the public Heretic toolkit [6] as a further baseline. Heretic applies the same weight-space orthogonalization of Equation 3 to the single Basic RD direction, but tunes the layerwise intensity schedule with its own internal Bayesian search rather than using the fixed schedule of Appendix B. There is no per-prompt candidate bank and no learned w; the baseline isolates the contribution of schedule tuning alone, independent of the per-prompt aggregation introduced by WRDO. We run the released pipeline out of the box, with default hyperparameters. 

RDO. We further include the gradient-based refusal-direction baseline of [10], denoted RDO. Instead of taking a difference of means as in Basic RD, RDO parameterizes a single refusal direction per layer and optimizes it directly with gradient descent against a refusal-suppression objective on the harmful training split, exploiting differentiability through the model’s hidden activations. We use the released implementation of Wollschläger et al. [10] unchanged: the comparison against Basic RD isolates the contribution of learning the direction itself. As with Basic RD and Heretic, there is no per-prompt candidate bank and no learned aggregation w.

RDO-CONE. Wollschläger et al. [10] also argue that refusal is mediated by a cone of directions rather than by a single line, and release a concept-cone construction alongside the single-direction RDO objective. This is the prior construction closest to the motivation of WRDO, since it likewise rejects the assumption that one difference-of-means axis captures refusal, so we include it as a separate baseline denoted RDO-CONE. It differs from WRDO in where the multiplicity is placed: RDO-CONE learns a set of mutually constrained directions that span a cone and ablates the whole cone, whereas WRDO keeps a fixed bank of per-prompt directions and learns only how to aggregate them into the composite of Equation 2. We again use the released implementation of Wollschläger et al. [10] unchanged, with the authors’ default cone dimensionality. 

3.2 WRD optimizers 

For optimizing the refusal-direction weights in the WRDO pipeline, we propose two distinct procedures. The first is a WRDO-tailored adaptation of separable CMA-ES, a well-known derivative-free optimizer suited to our low-dimensional setting. The second is a novel scheme based on group-relative policy optimization with importance sampling, designed specifically for the WRDO objective. 

WRD-CMAES. CMA-ES [11, 12] is a standard derivative-free optimizer for continuous black-box objectives, well suited to the noisy and gradient-free nature of the WRDO loss. In our setting we apply it to LJ of Equation 5: the search variables are the aggregation coefficients w of the candidate bank, the search space is described in Appendix A, and each black-box query corresponds to one full WRDO evaluation, i.e. building the composite direction r(w), intervening the model through the operator of Section 2.3, and measuring the resulting ASR on 4the validation batch. The concrete sampler choice, hyperparameter values and search-space bounds are listed in Appendix F. 

Relation to evolution strategies. Both instantiations belong to the evolution-strategy family [11, 13, 14]: a distribution over w is maintained, perturbed samples are scored by the black-box objective, and the distribution is moved towards the better-scoring ones. WRD-CMAES is its canonical member, and WRD-GRPO-IS reads as an ES with importance mixing, with the Gaussian rollout of Equation 11 as the search distribution and the weights evm as the mixing correction, so running both compares ES against RL under one objective and one budget. The analogy stops at the reward: NES-style updates see only the scalar fitness of each perturbation, whereas WRD-GRPO-IS routes it through token-level policy ratios and can therefore carry the KL term of Equation 8. 

WRD-GRPO-IS. The target loss function described in 2.4 is a non-differentiable function, so it is natural to consider a reinforcement learning approach to its optimization. To keep the intervened model close to the clean base model on benign prompts, WRD-GRPO-IS augments the bare WRDO objective of Equation 5 with a KL-divergence penalty against the policy πfθ evaluated on a random batch from Dbenign , yielding the method-specific objective

Discussion. First, in nearly every configuration the two learned WRDO instantiations (WRD-CMAES and WRD-GRPO-IS) outperform every prior single-direction baseline by a clear margin, establishing the superiority of the weighted-aggregation approach over the difference-of-means recipe, the Heretic toolkit and the gradient-learned RDO baseline. Second, the Uniform-avg WRD baseline is consistently dominated by the learned WRDO instantiations across the α grid, confirming that learning the aggregation coefficients w is what drives the gain, not the per-prompt bank alone.

5.4 HarmBench 

We additionally re-evaluate the considered methods through an external benchmark that was not used during optimization, in order to corroborate the significance of the attack-setup results with a training-independent evaluator and to control for potential overfitting to the guard judges of Section 4.3. Because HarmBench is designed primarily as an evaluator of instruction-tuned models, in this setup we restrict attention to the instruct variants of our model pool, additionally including Qwen/Qwen3-8B [21]. Discussion. The HarmBench evaluation confirms the superiority of the WRDO approach observed in Section 5.1: the learned WRDO instantiations attain the highest HarmBench ASR across the α grid. 5.6 Cross-judge transfer 

A cross-judge transfer analysis (Appendix H) identifies WildGuard as the most reliable optimization signal among the considered guards: interventions optimized against WildGuard transfer well to the other two judges, while the reverse direction is less stable. 

6 Conclusion 

We introduced Weighted Refusal Direction Optimization (WRDO), a procedure that constructs a refusal direction by learning aggregation coefficients w over a bank of per-prompt directions, and we presented two derivative-free 10 instantiations of the resulting black-box problem: a WRDO-tailored adaptation of separable CMA-ES and a proposed GRPO variant with importance sampling. Across three open-weight models, three guard judges and the HarmBench classifier, the two WRDO instantiations exceed all prior single-direction baselines and transfer cleanly to the defense setup. Among them, WRD-GRPO-IS is the most reliable: it preserves the general capabilities of the intervened model substantially better than every other method considered, and the cross-judge analysis singles out WildGuard as the most reliable optimization signal, so we recommend WRD-GRPO-IS optimized against WildGuard as the default configuration for both auditing and defense. 

7 Limitations 

The full protocol of Sections 5.1–5.5 covers three open-weight checkpoints of comparable size, in the 7–8B parameter range. A second limitation concerns the WRD-CMAES instantiation: its current implementation consumes the bare ASR-only objective of Equation 5 and does not yet support the KL regularizer of Equation 8. Extending the Optuna interface to expose a KL-aware sampling target is under active development, and we expect to incorporate this support in a future revision of the codebase.

9 Ethical Considerations 

This work is inherently dual-use: methods for identifying and modifying refusal directions can help analyze and improve model safety, but they can also be used to weaken safeguards in aligned models. In particular, interventions that suppress refusal may increase the risk of harmful compliance in domains such as cyber abuse, privacy violations, disinformation, or other unsafe requests. We therefore view WRDO primarily as a tool for controlled mechanistic analysis and safety evaluation. Our experiments are conducted offline on open-weight models and use automated guard judges, HarmBench-style evaluation, and utility benchmarks to study both safety and capability effects. The goal is to better understand refusal representations, not to encourage unsafe deployment or misuse. The broader implication of our findings is that refusal behavior may be concentrated in low-dimensional internal features and can therefore be brittle under targeted interventions. This suggests that refusal alone should not be treated as a complete safety mechanism; robust deployment requires layered defenses, including safer training, adversarial evaluation, monitoring, external classifiers, and system-level policy enforcement. At the same time, the defense experiment shows a constructive use of the same machinery: WRDO-like methods may help diagnose and partially restore refusal behavior in modified or abliterated checkpoints. Overall, we hope this work supports more robust alignment methods and encourages careful, transparent evaluation of both the risks and defensive uses of refusal-direction interventions.

F Training Hyperparameters 

This section documents the training-time hyperparameters of all six methods used in the main experiments. All values are reproduced in Table 7; the paragraphs below give a short narrative for each method. For the prior baselines (Basic RD, Heretic, RDO) we use the hyperparameter values from their original implementations without any additional tuning. 

Basic RD. Basic RD performs no learned optimization. The only tunable knobs are the dataset filters used to construct the harmful/harmless splits and to select the seed difference-of-means direction: harmful prompts that the unintervened model already refuses are discarded if their refusal-induction score is below induce_refusal_threshold ,candidate directions whose KL to the clean policy on the benign batch exceeds kl_threshold are dropped, and the bottom prune_layer_percentage of layers is excluded from the layer-search. All three thresholds are taken verbatim from the reference implementation of Arditi et al. [5]. 

Heretic. Heretic runs an Optuna-based Bayesian search over the layerwise schedule of Equation 3. The search budget is n_trials with n_startup_trials random-initialization trials, and uses a KL constraint with scale 

kl_divergence_scale and target kl_divergence_target . Trial selection on the Pareto front prefers the minimum-KL trial whose refusal count is at most refusal_threshold_count . The search ranges over max_weight ∈ [0 .8, 1.5] ,

max_weight_position relative to depth in [0 .6, 1.0] , min_weight ∈ [0 .0, 1.0] , min_weight_distance relative to depth in [0 .05 , 0.6] , the direction-index fraction in [0 .4, 0.9] , and the schedule scope ∈ { global , per-layer }. We use the released Heretic toolkit out of the box, with the defaults of Weidmann [6] for every hyperparameter listed above.

H Cross-Judge Transfer 

The learned WRDO instantiations consume a specific guard judge as their training signal, which raises the possibility that the resulting coefficient vector w⋆ exploits idiosyncrasies of that judge rather than capturing a transferable refusal-suppression direction. We probe this potential judge overfitting through a cross-judge transfer analysis: w⋆

is optimized against each guard in turn and then re-evaluated against the other two, with the magnitude of the off-diagonal drop quantifying the extent to which performance is judge-specific.

Discussion. The cross-judge analysis shows that learned WRDO interventions do not always transfer equally across guard judges. In several cases, optimizing against one judge yields high ASR under that same judge but noticeably weaker performance under another, indicating that part of the learned direction may depend on judge-specific decision boundaries rather than on a fully transferable refusal-suppression signal. The most pronounced asymmetry is observed for WildGuard: interventions that perform well under WildGuard tend to transfer relatively well to the other judges, while interventions optimized for other judges can fail to preserve high WildGuard ASR. This suggests that WildGuard provides the most reliable and demanding optimization signal among the considered guards. By contrast, Llama-Guard-3 often reports lower ASR than the other judges. We interpret this cautiously: rather than necessarily indicating weaker attacks, this may reflect lower sensitivity of Llama-Guard-3 to harmful behavior in the evaluated generations, leading to more false negatives or inconsistent harmfulness detection. As a result, lower Llama-Guard-3 scores should not be treated as definitive evidence that the intervention is less effective.

K Sensitivity to the KL Coefficient β

The KL coefficient β of Equation 8 is the single hyperparameter that sets where WRD-GRPO-IS sits on the attack-versus-utility trade-off, so the value reported throughout the paper is not an arbitrary default. We select it by sweeping β ∈ { 1, 10 , 10 2, 10 3, 10 4} on Qwen3-8B-Base at α = 1 .0, with everything else held at the values of Table 7, and reading ASR and utility off the same run. Tables 10 and 13 report both sides of the sweep. The selection is made once, on this model, and the resulting value is reused unchanged for every other model and judge in the paper; we did not tune β per model. The two sides of the sweep move in opposite directions, but not at the same rate. ASR falls gradually as β grows, staying almost flat over the middle of the range before dropping off at the largest value. Utility does not track it: it stays poor while the penalty is weak, with MMLU and GSM8K far below the clean model and perplexity an order of magnitude above it, and then recovers abruptly in a single step to the unintervened level.
