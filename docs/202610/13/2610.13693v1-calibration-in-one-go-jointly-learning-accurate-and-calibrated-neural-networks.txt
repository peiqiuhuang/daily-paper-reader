ABSTRACT 

Neural networks (NNs), while highly flexible and versatile, are often poorly cal-ibrated. Post-hoc calibration methods adjust the outputs of an already trained model using a separate calibration dataset and additional calibration parameters. This process adds overhead that requires separate optimization of the calibrator and subsequent model selection. In this paper, we show that standard (post-hoc) calibrators can be trained end-to-end with the backbone via a bilevel Stackelberg formulation. This formulation eliminates the need for a separate calibration set or a dedicated calibration loss by jointly optimizing accuracy and calibration using cross-entropy, introducing only one additional hyperparameter: the calibrator learn-ing rate. Although our framework supports calibrators ranging from temperature and matrix scaling to NN layers, our results show that using the final classifica-tion layer itself as the calibrator yields accurate, well-calibrated models with no additional parameters. Our approach outperforms SOTA calibrators with minimal added complexity, while also avoiding the multiplicative overhead of separate calibration components in settings such as multi-task learning. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Machine learning, and neural networks in particular, have become an important part of modern society, with their predictions increasingly informing downstream decision making. In many classification settings, however, the predicted class alone is insufficient: downstream systems may instead rely on the predicted probability of each outcome as a measure of uncertainty. Such probabilities play an important role in high-stakes applications, including healthcare (Ricci Lara et al., 2023), fair decision-making (Gupta et al., 2026), and machine learning-based risk assessment (Brito et al., 2023), among others (Wang, 2023; He et al., 2026). Beyond supporting decision making, predictive probabilities can also provide insight into what a model is unsure about, thus offering an additional layer of model understanding and human-AI collaboration (Tomsett et al., 2020). However, for the predicted class probabilities to be useful in these settings, they must accurately reflect the model’s predictive uncertainty. In practice, machine learning models, and neural networks in particular, are often miscalibrated (Guo et al., 2017). Classifier calibration aims to address this issue by aligning a classifier’s predicted probabilities with the observed likelihood of the corresponding outcomes (Platt, 2000; Guo et al., 2017; Murphy, 2023). For instance, among predictions assigned 90% probability to class C, class C should be correct 90% of the time. For example, as illustrated by the reliability diagram in Fig. 1a, a perfectly calibrated classifier lies along the diagonal, whereas deviations from the diagonal indicate calibration error (Guo et al., 2017). Various approaches have been proposed to calibrate models, including train-time methods and post-hoc methods (Wang, 2023). Train-time approaches often incorporate additional calibration-related terms into the training objective, whereas post-hoc approaches train a separate model on the output of a trained model (Guo et al., 2017), as illustrated in Fig. 1b.

Although effective, these approaches introduce additional complexity into the model development pipeline. Train-time methods require the model designer to choose and tune additional terms or use a calibration-specific objective, while post-hoc methods require training an additional model and, typically, a separate held-out calibration set. On top of this, both of these families of methods come with their own complexity. For example, post-hoc calibrators can themselves overfit, motivating their regularization (Berta et al., 2025; 2026a;b) or using simpler calibrators such as Temperature Scaling (Guo et al., 2017). This pipeline complexity is particularly pronounced in settings like Multi-Task Learning (MTL) (Caruana, 1997; Javaloy & Valera, 2022), where a shared model serves multiple tasks, potentially requiring a separate calibrator for each. Moreover, the richer annotation requirements of MTL make reserving additional data exclusively for calibration particularly costly. Despite these challenges, calibration in MTL remains comparatively underexplored. 

Motivation. In this paper, we aim to obtain neural networks that are simultaneously accurate and well calibrated in a single training procedure , while retaining the efficiency of standard training and avoiding a separate post-hoc calibration stage or additional calibration data. To this end, we formulate calibration as a bilevel optimization (BLO) problem (Dempe, 2002; Beck et al., 2027), where standard model training is the follower and the calibrator acts as the leader, as shown in Fig. 1c. We efficiently solve the resulting Stackelberg problem with a single forward and backward pass per iteration, jointly updating model and calibrator parameters within the same training step using separate learning rates. This introduces one additional hyperparameter beyond standard training: the calibrator learning rate. The resulting framework integrates calibration directly into end-to-end training while retaining the efficiency of standard training: (i) it requires neither a calibration-specific loss nor a separate calibration dataset; (ii) it imposes no additional architectural constraints and accommodates calibrators ranging from temperature and matrix scaling to neural layers, adding at most the trainable parameters of the corresponding post-hoc calibrator; and (iii) it introduces only one additional hyperparameter, namely the calibrator learning rate. Empirically, our approach achieves accuracy and calibration competitive with substantially more complex state-of-the-art methods while limiting the additional calibration overhead to a single hyperparameter. Remarkably, additional calibration parameters are unnecessary: using the final classification layer itself as the calibrator yields accurate, well-calibrated models , as demonstrated by the example in Fig. 1a. We further show that this single-stage, low-overhead formulation extends naturally to more complex settings such as MTL, where conventional calibration is cumbersome. 

2 RELATED WORKS 

Post-hoc calibration. Post-hoc calibration modifies the output probabilities of a trained classifica-tion model by learning a transformation of its outputs on a held-out calibration set. Given an input 

x, a classifier f is typically trained with cross-entropy (CE), after which a calibrator c is fitted to a held-out calibration set, resulting in the calibrated model c ◦ f (x) = c(f (x)) . Parametric post-hoc calibrators include Platt Scaling (Platt, 2000), designed for binary classification, which applies a learned sigmoid transformation to the logits z, i.e., σ(az + b). Multi-class extensions include Vector 2Preprint. Under review. Scaling and Matrix Scaling (MS) (Guo et al., 2017), which learn more flexible transformations of the logits. Temperature Scaling (TS) (Guo et al., 2017) uses a single learned scalar T to rescale the logits, σ(z/T ). Despite their simplicity, these models can still overfit the calibration set, motivating recent work on regularizing calibrators (Berta et al., 2025; 2026a;b). Nonparametric approaches include histogram binning (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2001) and isotonic regression (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002), which directly estimate calibration mappings from empirical frequencies. However, their flexibility can make them prone to overfitting, particularly with limited calibration data (Menon et al., 2012). Moreover, multiclass extensions typically rely on one-vs-rest schemes (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002; Murphy, 2023), substantially increasing pipeline complexity. Overviews of post-hoc calibration methods can be found in (Guo et al., 2017; Murphy, 2023; Berta et al., 2026a). 

Progressive training approaches. More recently, multi-stage neural network training procedures have been proposed to improve calibration. In particular, Two-Stage Training (TST) (Jordahn & Olmos, 2024; Khodaverdian et al., 2025) first trains the network and then retrains only some of the final fully connected layers while freezing earlier representations. This can be extended to impose a probabilistic prior on the final hidden layer, turning the model in a last-layer Bayesian Neural Network, that further enhances calibration (V-TST) (Jordahn & Olmos, 2024). Other works connect poor calibration to over-compression (Tao et al., 2024; Wang & Zhang, 2024), especially in later layers through the information bottleneck principle, with Wang & Zhang (2024) proposing progressively freezing earlier layers during training. The aforementioned methods can be cast as bilevel (or multi-level) optimization problems, which we convert into train-time calibration approaches. 

Train-time calibration. Train-time calibration methods modify the learning objective or training procedure to encourage calibrated outputs. Examples include adding Lp regularization (Joo & Chung, 2020), incorporating sliced Wasserstein distance penalties (Joo & Chung, 2020), or including differ-entiable bin membership terms in the loss (Karandikar et al., 2021). Other approaches include label smoothing (Szegedy et al., 2016; Müller et al., 2019) or replacing CE with focal loss (Mukhoti et al., 2020), both of which can be interpreted as CE with an additional maximum-entropy regularizer (Meis-ter et al., 2020; Mukhoti et al., 2020). Finally, several works propose specialized loss functions designed to produce calibrated models (Bohdal et al., 2021; Popordanoska et al., 2022). Our approach provides a complementary train-time mechanism: rather than requiring a particular calibration loss, it modifies the optimization structure and can therefore be used with standard training objectives, such as CE, or combined with existing calibration-aware losses, as seen in our experiments. 

Bilevel Optimization and Calibration. Our work leverages BLO to turn post-hoc calibration into a train-time procedure. Related work has also explored calibration through the lens of BLO. In (Bohdal et al., 2021), meta-learning alternates between CE updates for model training and optimization of the hyperparameters of calibration regularizers. Similarly, BLO has been used to learn validation sample weights to improve calibration (Sanguin et al., 2025). This approach is computationally demanding, requiring multiple optimization steps to approximate the bilevel problem (Franceschi et al., 2018), with a follower minimizing CE on Dtrain and a leader optimizing validation-sample weights. These approaches are orthogonal to ours, as they either enhance existing calibration methods via BLO or formulate a different bilevel objective. Our approach instead addresses calibration within standard backpropagation-based training, avoiding expensive optimization and held-out calibration data. 

3 TOWARDS CALIBRATION IN ONE GO

(Post-hoc) Calibration as a Bilevel Optimization Problem. Bilevel optimization problems are hierarchical optimization problems involving two decision makers, a leader and a follower. The leader makes a decision while anticipating the follower’s best response to that decision, thus inducing a hierarchical dependency between the two levels (Dempe, 2002; Beck et al., 2027). Learning a neural network that is both accurate and well calibrated can be formulated as a BLO problem, in which predictive accuracy is optimized at the follower level and calibration at the leader level:

That is, calibration is performed only after model training, so the calibration objective does not influence which predictive-model solution is learned, thus skipping their interaction. From a BLO perspective, post-hoc calibration can therefore be interpreted as a decoupled approximation of the BLO problem in Eq. (1), in which the follower is solved first and its solution is subsequently treated as fixed when optimizing the leader. However, since decoupling is optimal only when the follower problem admits a unique solu-tion (Dempe, 2002; Beck et al., 2027), it is generally not justified for neural network training, where multiple equivalent local optima typically exist (Breiman, 2001; Goodfellow et al., 2016; Rudin et al., 2024). This motivates our approach to jointly optimize model training and calibration: rather than fixing an arbitrary follower solution before calibration, we allow the calibrator to influence which neural network parameters are learned . Our goal is therefore to solve the bilevel problem above in a way that jointly learns accurate and well-calibrated neural networks while retaining the efficiency of standard neural network training. 

Calibration in One Go (COG). The bilevel formulation of Eq. (1) naturally admits a Stackelberg-game interpretation (Von Stackelberg, 2010): the calibrator acts as the leader, updating the calibration parameters θcal , while anticipating the response of the classifier (the follower), which in turn adapts the neural network parameters θtrain to minimize the training loss. This joint problem can be addressed efficiently using gradient-based bilevel optimization methods (Liu et al., 2021). Specifically, we employ a first-order, Stackelberg-inspired scheme in which the leader and follower parameters are 

simultaneously updated iteratively using gradients of their respective objectives, with distinct learning rates for the two levels, in the spirit of Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA) (Fiez et al., 2020; Javaloy & Valera, 2022). Further details on BLO and GDA are provided in App A. For computational efficiency, we further make two simplifying choices. First, we use the same loss function for both levels, e.g., cross-entropy loss, such that Lcal := Ltrain . Second, we approximate both expectations in Eq. (1) using the same minibatch B t from the training data, i.e., Dcal = Dtrain .Consequently, both objectives can be evaluated on the same forward pass and their gradients computed with a single backpropagation pass , as in standard neural network training. Thus, the resulting procedure differs from standard training only in that the neural network and calibration parameters are updated with distinct learning rates as:

From Post-hoc Calibration to COG. Implementing standard post-hoc calibration methods within COG is straightforward and independent of the neural network’s architecture. For example, Tempera-ture Scaling can be implemented by introducing a single additional parameter (COG-TS), Matrix Scaling by an additional weight matrix and an additional bias vector (COG-MS), and TST by in-troducing multiple calibration layers (COG-TST). COG can also be applied without introducing additional trainable parameters (COG-FC). Recent work has shown that the internal representations of a neural network can be as effective for prediction as its final classification layer (Wang & Zhang, 2024); thus, the final classification layer can be treated as the calibrator within COG, allowing the method to be applied to arbitrary neural network architectures. Finally, COG is not only restricted to post-hoc CE-based calibration. For example, by replacing the objectives at both levels with the Label Smoothing objective, we obtain COG+LS. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We structure our empirical evaluation in three stages. First, we compare standard post-hoc calibration methods with their COG counterparts to isolate the effect of replacing post-hoc calibration with joint training. Second, we benchmark COG against calibrators from different methodological families under increasingly challenging conditions, including in-distribution data, out-of-distribution data, and covariate shift. Finally, we evaluate COG in the more complex setting of multi-task learning, where calibration must be achieved jointly across multiple prediction tasks. 

Experimental Setup and Metrics We focus on multiclass image classification and train ResNet101 models (He et al., 2016) from scratch on SVHN (Netzer et al., 2011), CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), following the setup of (Jordahn & Olmos, 2024). Full experimental details are provided in App. B, extended results in App. C, and experiments with additional CNN backbones in App. D. We report accuracy together with two scoring rules, negative log-likelihood (NLL) and the Brier score (Glenn et al., 1950), following recent recommendations for calibra-tion evaluation (Minderer et al., 2021; Silva Filho et al., 2023; Berta et al., 2025; Khodaverdian et al., 2025; Chidambaram & Ge, 2025). App. C additionally reports ECE (Naeini et al., 2015), RMSCE (Hendrycks et al., 2018) and MCE (Naeini et al., 2015) across multiple binning choices. 4.1 COMPARING POST -HOC METHODS AND THEIR COG C OUNTERPARTS

We compare representative methods from four families of calibrators: train-time calibration via Label Smoothing, post-hoc calibration via Temperature Scaling, a Bayesian calibration method via V-TST (with 10 Monte Carlo samples) (Jordahn & Olmos, 2024; Khodaverdian et al., 2025), and our (also train-time) calibration approach COG-FC and its Label Smoothing variant COG+LS. We select COG-FC as the representative COG variant because it achieves strong performance while introducing no additional calibration parameters; COG+LS additionally replaces the standard cross-entropy loss with the calibration-regularized objective used by Label Smoothing. Moreover, since no single metric fully captures the trade-off between predictive accuracy and calibration quality, several works recommend considering the Pareto front between these objectives, allowing their relative importance to be determined by the application (Minderer et al., 2021; Silva Filho et al., 2023; Chidambaram & Ge, 2025; Sharifdeen et al., 2026). For a compact summary, we compute, for each dataset, the unweighted average rank of each method across Accuracy, NLL, and Brier score (Javaloy et al., 2025). We denote the method with the best average rank as Top-ranked . App. C.2 and C.3, report results for all considered calibration methods and calibration errors.

Out of distribution (OOD) & covariate shift. Lastly, we assess whether ID calibration transfers to OOD detection and covariate shifts, following (Ovadia et al., 2019; Jordahn & Olmos, 2024). For covariate shifts, we use CIFAR-10-C, CIFAR-100-C (Hendrycks & Dietterich, 2019), and rotated SVHN (Jordahn & Olmos, 2024). OOD samples come from different datasets and lack valid labels in the original label space, whereas covariate shifts preserve the original labels under input corruption. For OOD detection, we use maximum softmax probability as the confidence score and treat ID samples as positive and OOD samples as negative. We report AUROC (Bradley, 1997) and FPR@95TPR, where higher AUROC and lower FPR95 indicate better OOD detection. As shown in Tab. 3, COG-FC performs particularly well for OOD detection, with COG+LS also achieving strong results. Across the three datasets, a COG variant achieves the best performance in each case. Under covariate shift (Tab. 4), we observe trends consistent with prior work (Ovadia et al., 2019): regularization-based approaches such as Label Smoothing are often more robust, and COG+LS generally improves over COG-FC. At the same time, performance varies across datasets and shift types, highlighting that robustness to distribution shift is distinct from in-distribution calibration (Ovadia et al., 2019). 4.3 USE CASE : M ULTI -T ASK LEARNING 

Prerequisite Questions. Calibration in Multi-Task Learning remains relatively under-explored, raising two fundamental questions: how should calibration be applied in MTL, and how should calibration be quantified across heterogeneous tasks? For the former, should each task be calibrated independently, requiring separate K post-hoc calibrators or regularizers, or should a single approach be applied across tasks? This is particularly challenging when tasks differ in type, e.g., binary classification, multiclass classification, and regression, which may require different calibration methods. Even tasks of the same type may have different calibration requirements, further increasing the complexity of the calibration pipeline. In contrast, COG can be applied directly to MTL models using the same training objective as the network, avoiding separate calibration procedures for each task. The second question concerns how to quantify calibration across tasks. Given the aforementioned issues, rather than introducing MTL-specific calibration metrics, we evaluate using the previous metrics. 

Experimental Setup. For this experiment, we use CelebA (Liu et al., 2015), where the model predicts 40 facial attributes, i.e., 40 binary tasks. For non-COG methods each task is calibrated separately, resulting in 40 separate calibrators or regularization terms, as seen in Fig. 4. 5 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we simplify neural network calibration by reframing post-hoc calibration as a bilevel optimization problem and solving it through Stackelberg-inspired joint optimization. Our framework, COG, converts post-hoc calibrators into train-time, end-to-end alternatives that require no held-out calibration data and introduce only minimal additional complexity: a single additional learning rate. Across a broad range of experiments, architectures, and calibration approaches, COG achieves strong and consistent performance, matching or outperforming existing methods in most settings. In particular, COG-FC achieves state-of-the-art calibration performance while introducing no additional trainable parameters . We further find that COG is stable to optimize and performs well for OOD detection, demonstrating its flexibility across architectures, post-hoc methods, and regularization-based approaches. We further study calibration in MTL settings, where task heterogeneity requires conventional calibration methods to be selected and applied separately for each task. In contrast, COG works out of the box for MTL, providing a unified calibration approach with strong overall and task-specific performance without additional trainable parameters or separate calibration procedures. Overall, we recommend COG-FC as a simple, stable, and lightweight default for calibration, given its strong performance across diverse settings and lack of additional trainable parameters. 

Future Work and Limitations. Several directions remain for future work. First, our evaluation could be extended to alternative definitions of calibration and emerging calibration metrics, partic-ularly those addressing limitations of standard metrics such as ECE. Second, we do not explicitly explore optimistic and pessimistic bilevel optimization (Dempe, 2002; Beck et al., 2027). In optimistic BLO, the leader assumes that, among multiple optimal solutions of the follower, the follower selects the one that is most favorable to the leader. In contrast, pessimistic BLO considers the worst-case selection among the follower’s optimal solutions, accounting for the least favorable outcome for the leader’s objective (Dempe, 2002; Beck et al., 2027). Pessimistic bilevel optimization, which is closely related to robust optimization (Beck et al., 2022; 2023), could be particularly relevant for calibration under adversarial settings or covariate shifts, while optimistic bilevel optimization may yield even better solutions for the calibrator. Further theoretical analysis of calibration from a bilevel optimization perspective could also provide additional insight. For MTL, future work could consider more challenging and heterogeneous settings, including tasks with different output spaces or objectives, alongside state-of-the-art MTL training methods and designing calibration metrics specifically for multi-task problems. Finally, although our experiments focus on computer vision, COG is not inherently tied to a particular architecture or modality and could be extended to other differentiable models and domains. B.1 TRAINING DETAILS 

For the backbone considered in our experiments, ResNet-101 (He et al., 2016), we trained the models for 300 epochs in the single-task setting and 100 epochs in the multitask setting. All experiments used the Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2014) with a batch size of 256. Unless otherwise stated, we used five random seeds for every experiment reported in the main paper. All experiments used PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019) version 2.11. For the COG experiments, we used a step learning-rate scheduler (as recommended in (Fiez et al., 2020; Kwon et al., 2023)) that reduced the learning rate by a factor of 10 every 10 epochs for both tasks. For CelebA, the learning rate was reduced by a factor of 10 every 3 epochs. For both single and multi task settings we trained with cross-entropy loss. Specifically, for the MTL setting, we used equal weights across all tasks. All post-hoc calibrators and evaluation metrics are implemented using the Probmetrics library (version 1.2.0) (Berta et al., 2025), except for Temperature Scaling, for which we use the original implementation (Guo et al., 2017). All post-hoc calibrators were fitted on the validation set. During training, for all models considered in the experiments, we selected the best model checkpoint based on the validation Brier score, as this criterion leads to better-calibrated models (Berta et al., 2025). For label smoothing, in all cases, we used a smoothing value of 0.1.

ViT Experiments. For the ViT ablation we use the ViT architecture seen in (Jordahn & Olmos, 2024). We train for a maximum of 400 epochs, with a early stopping patience of 50 validation checks. Mean Square Calibration Error (RMSCE) (Hendrycks et al., 2018) and maximum calibration error (MCE) (Naeini et al., 2015). These calibration metrics are reported as percentages. In cases where NLL is not reported in the main text, we additionally include it here to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Standard calibration metrics such as ECE have several well-documented limitations, particularly in the multiclass setting (Minderer et al., 2021; Chidambaram & Ge, 2025). The limitations discussed below represent only a subset of those identified in the literature. First, ECE is a biased estimator, whose magnitude of the positive bias increases with model accuracy (Minderer et al., 2021). Fur-thermore, its reported value can vary substantially with the choice of the number of bins, making comparisons between models sensitive on the binning scheme (Minderer et al., 2021; Silva Filho et al., 2023; Tao et al., 2024). Moreover, the relationship between ECE and Accuracy appears to hold primarily for highly performant models (Tao et al., 2024) and may depend on the architecture considered (Sharifdeen et al., 2026). Comparisons between in-hoc and post-hoc calibration methods can also be problematic when relying solely on standard calibration metrics (Ashukha et al., 2020; Tao et al., 2024). Lastly, ECE evaluates calibration only with respect to the top-1 predicted class, and recently it has been shown that even a trivial predictor that always outputs the mean confidence can outperform post-hoc calibration methods according to standard calibration metrics (Chidambaram & Ge, 2025).
