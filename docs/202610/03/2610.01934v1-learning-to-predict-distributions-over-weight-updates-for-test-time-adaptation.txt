## ABSTRACT 

Hypernetworks have recently shown success in dynamically adapting the param-eters of Large Language Models (LLMs) at runtime based on signals such as task descriptions or additional demonstrations. Here we ask: how much adaptation signal can be obtained using only the input query to an LLM? To answer this, we study query-conditioned hypernetworks for LoRA estimation. Further, we in-troduce distributional hypernetworks, able to produce not only point estimates of parameter adapters, but also a distribution over possible LoRAs. For this we pro-pose a simple end-to-end loss using a differentiable Monte Carlo approximation and explore multiple distribution parameterizations including regression and con-vex combination variants. Results show that even using the mean of the learned distribution can outperform deterministic hypernetworks. Crucially, the learned distribution enables a different form of test-time scaling: instead of spending ad-ditional compute only by sampling more token sequences from a fixed model, we sample weight updates, yielding multiple adapted models for the same query. Per-formance improves as more weight samples are considered and remains stronger than corresponding token-sampling adaptation baselines. Finally, we find that generated updates can transfer across queries, suggesting that the hypernetwork learns reusable structure in how the model should adapt. Together, these results show that query-conditioned distributions over weight updates can support both adaptation and test-time scaling. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Updating parameters in a Large Language Model (LLM) requires gradient-based optimization over multiple rounds with sufficient data. This is a brittle process that can lead to catastrophic forgetting, even when using parameter efficient adaptation such as LoRA [1]. Gradient-descent is therefore particularly unsuitable for altering parameters of models at test-time, where there is little or no supervision data available. Hypernetworks offer a promising alternative way to do this. A hyper-network is a neural network that generates the weights of another network [2]. Hypernetworks are trained offline using ordinary backpropagation, but at inference time, they directly predict weight updates for a target network. The learned parameters of the hypernetwork can therefore be viewed as slow weights that learn how to produce fast weights for each new input [3, 4]. Unlike learned optimizers [5], the hypernetwork does not require gradients at inference time. It turns parameter adaptation from a repeated optimization process into a direct prediction. Recent work has shown success using hypernetworks to generate LoRA adapters from natural-language task descriptions [6], or generate adapters from multiple demonstrations of related exam-ples [7]. These methods allow gradient-free adaptation, but still require such auxiliary information to be available. Here, we propose to predict a LoRA directly from the language model input. The key intuition is that different queries may reveal regularities in how the model should adapt, allow-ing the hypernetwork to learn an implicit mapping from input structure to useful parameter updates rather than relying on an explicit task description. This leads to a broader question: how much adaptation signal is contained in a single query? Is it sufficient to determine a useful weight update? And if a single update is underdetermined, can the query instead constrain a useful distribution over

To address this, we propose a simple differentiable Monte Carlo approximation of the marginal log-likelihood using two types of updates: Gaussian LoRA regression and convex combinations of LoRA exemplars, depicted in Figure 1. We explore multiple parameterization and optimization variants, such as weight correlations and loss aggregation, and compare our approach with a point-estimate hypernetwork. Our experiments show a single query does indeed contain substantial adaptation signal. It suffices to generate useful LoRA updates without task descriptions or demonstrations, and can further support a distribution over plausible updates. The mean of the learned distribution outperforms deterministic hypernetworks by 4.6–11.7% accuracy across 9 benchmarks. More importantly, this distribution becomes a new axis for test-time scaling: sampling different adapted models is competitive with, and often stronger than, sampling additional outputs from fixed weights. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We study how much adaptation signal can be extracted from a single query, showing that it is sufficient to generate useful query-conditioned LoRA updates without test-time opti-mization or task-level auxiliary information. • We introduce distributional hypernetworks that predict a distribution over query-specific weight updates based on a simple differentiable Monte Carlo approximation. • We introduce test-time scaling in weight space, where additional inference compute is used to sample alternative model updates rather than additional token sequences. • We study the structure of the generated updates, including when query-specific generation becomes useful and whether generated adaptations generalize to other in-domain queries. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Hypernetworks. Hypernetworks learn to generate the parameters of another network and provide a way to amortize adaptation without optimizing the target model at inference [2]. In language mod-els, HyperTuning [7] and HINT [8] generate parameter-efficient adaptations from demonstrations or instructions, while Text-to-LoRA (T2L; [6]) generates LoRA weights from a task description. More recent methods generate adaptations from longer contexts or related examples [9–11]. Hy-perDecoders move closer to instance-level adaptation by generating a separate adaptation from each input [12]. These methods differ in what information is used to condition the hypernetwork, but predict a single adaptation. We show that a single query can provide enough information to generate useful query-conditioned LoRA updates and a distribution over possible LoRA updates. This con-trasts with test-time training methods, which adapt the model through gradient-based optimization at inference time [13–18]. 

Distributions over Weights. Learning distributions over neural network parameters has been studied in Bayesian neural networks and generative weight models. Bayesian Hypernetworks [19], 2Learning to Predict Distributions over Weight Updates for Test-Time Adaptation implicit weight uncertainty [20], and HyperGAN [21] use learned generators to represent distribu-tions over model parameters. These methods show that useful variation can be represented directly in weight space, but the resulting distributions are typically used for uncertainty estimation, ensem-bling, or representing a global model distribution. More recent work studies distributions specifically in the low-rank adaptation space. ICM-LoRA [22] and DiffLoRA [23] learn conditional generative models from collections of previously optimized adapters, while Bayesian LoRA methods model uncertainty around task-level adapta-tions [24–26]. LoRA ensembles similarly obtain multiple task-level adaptations through indepen-dent training [27]. Our distribution is different in both how it is produced and how it is used. It is conditioned on each query and trained directly through the frozen LM’s response loss, rather than by reproducing previously optimized adapters or approximating a task-level posterior. Sam-ples from the distribution are treated as alternative adaptations for the current query, allowing the hypernetwork to represent several useful updates rather than committing to a single one. Further discussion of related work is deferred to Appendix A.

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models, training and evaluation data. We evaluate our method on five language models, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Gemma3-1B-Instruct, Llama3.2-1B-Instruct, and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct. We use a diverse set of benchmarks, covering standard LLM tasks GSM8K, MATH500, HumanEval, ARC-Challenge, MMLU-STEM, MedQA and GPQA-Diamond. Hypernetwork and baselines are trained using another collection of tasks NuminaMath CoT, MATH train, MathInstruct, MedMCQA (using only its training split), Magicoder OSS Instruct 75K, and CAMEL AI. These datasets cover a broad set of problems from mathematics, coding, science, and medicine. The data results in approximately 1.3 million training examples containing around 530 million tokens. We will report macro accuracy, as the unweighted mean of accuracy across the seven evaluation benchmarks. 

Distributional Hypernetwork architecture. We start by constructing a deterministic version of the hypernetwork from Section 3.1, by modifying the T2L model [6]. This model receives as input a layer embedding, and a module embedding and a task embedding produced with a text encoder. We replace the last embedding by a learned pooled representation of the query, implemented with an additional attention mechanism over the last layer of the language model after it encodes the query. This network is trained with the loss in Equation 5. We construct a sampler for the distributional version of this network as explained in Section 3. This uses the same backbone but separately predicts mean and standard deviation. It then applies the Gaussian or convex Hull transformations to obtain samples from the LoRA distribution. The regression hypernetwork predicts LoRA factors directly, while the mixing hypernetwork predicts logits over a bank of reference LoRAs, which are softmaxed to obtain the mixture weights. The reference LoRAs are first trained for three epochs on the same training data, after which the hypernetwork is trained for one additional epoch. The mixing setting, therefore, receives more training exposure than the regression setting. Training follows Equation 2. We explore training variants including Gaussian covariance learning as in Equation 10, modeled with a low-rank matrix product for tractability, as well as average, best-of-K and worst-of-K losses. We observe empirically that diagonal covariance and best-of-K losses provide the best results (Figure 2). Finding 1: A Single Query Contains Useful Adaptation Signal 

A single query contains enough adaptation signal to generate effective LoRA updates without task descriptions or demonstrations. The resulting updates match or outperform standard low-rank adaptation baselines. Another relevant trend is that the distributional hypernetwork outperforms its deterministic coun-terpart in all but the largest model sizes. We attribute this improvement in part to regularization introduced by sampling during training. A deterministic hypernetwork is optimized only at the up-date it predicts, while a distributional hypernetwork receives gradients through perturbed updates sampled around its prediction. The resulting objective therefore depends not only on the loss at one point, but also on nearby points in weight space. For zero mean Gaussian perturbations, the expected loss can be viewed locally as the loss at the mean together with a curvature dependent term, favoring solutions whose nearby perturbations also have low loss. In this sense, distributional training encourages the predicted update to lie in a more robust region of the adaptation space rather than optimizing a single point. Finding 2: Distributional Training Improves the Predicted Update 

Training the hypernetwork as a distribution produces a stronger single update than directly train-ing a point estimate. Even without weight sampling at test time, the mean of the learned distri-bution consistently outperforms the deterministic hypernet. 5.3 PREDICTING WEIGHT DISTRIBUTION FROM QUERY 

The previous section showed that a single query contains enough adaptation signal to predict a useful update. Here we ask whether the query can also enable learning a distribution over good LoRA updates. A distributional hypernetwork provides more than a stronger single update. It also allows us to sample a set of alternative adaptations at test time, thus giving an additional way to spend test-time compute. Most test-time scaling methods keep the model weights fixed and spend additional compute on sampling more output sequences [40–42]. With the distributional hypernet, we can instead sample multiple LoRA updates, producing a different adapted model for each generation. We compare these two ways of using additional compute through majority voting. For FullFT, LoRA, MoL, and deterministic hypernetworks, all K generations are sampled from the same adapted weights. These methods therefore scale only through token sampling. For distributional hypernet-works, each generation uses a separately sampled weight update from the distribution. Increasing 

K explores different adapted models in addition to output sequences. We use temperature 0.8 for token sampling, while generations from sampled weight updates are decoded greedily ( T = 0 ). Figure 4 shows the resulting scaling behavior across five language models using macro accuracy over seven benchmarks. Weight sampling remains competitive with token sampling as the test-time budget increases, and is often stronger than the corresponding deterministic hypernet. This is espe-cially clear for Qwen3 1.7B and Gemma 3 1B, where sampling from the learned weight distribution provides substantial gains over repeatedly decoding from a single predicted update. Even when the advantage is smaller, as for Qwen3 4B, weight sampling remains competitive with strong adaptation baselines. These results suggest that useful test-time diversity can come not only from sampling different outputs, but also from sampling different versions of the adapted model itself. Finding 3: Test-Time Compute Can Be Spent in Weight Space 

Sampling weight updates provides a new way for test-time scaling. Across model families, weight sampling remains competitive with token sampling and often performs better, showing that useful diversity can come from changing the adapter rather than only its generated tokens. 5.4 REUSING GENERATED UPDATES ACROSS QUERIES 

So far, we have generated a separate update for every test query. We next ask whether the updates produced for different queries contain structure that can be accumulated and reused. For each bench-mark, we split the examples into two disjoint halves. We use only the prompts from the first half to generate hypernetwork outputs and combine them into a single shared LoRA. This adapter is then kept fixed and used for every query in the second half. Importantly, the hypernetwork is never called on the held-out queries and no labels from the first half are used. Although the hypernetwork observes the first-half queries as conditioning inputs, neither it nor the baselines receives labels or undergoes optimization on these examples. The experiment therefore measures whether the updates generated from one set of queries can transfer to unseen queries from the same benchmark. For the regression hypernet, we obtain LoRA factors for each query in the first half. The determin-istic hypernetwork contributes its prediction, while for the distributional hypernetwork we use the predicted mean without sampling. We average the A factors across queries and separately average the B factors, producing one rank-r LoRA reused on the held-out half. For the mixing hypernet, each query produces mixing weights over the reference LoRA bank. We average these coefficients across the first half and use them to construct one shared adapter for the second half. Figure 5 shows that the generated updates retain useful information across queries. Although the shared adapter is constructed entirely from a disjoint set of prompts, it remains effective on the held-out half, and the distributionally trained hypernetworks generally produce stronger reusable adaptations than their deterministic counterparts. This suggests that the hypernetwork is learning more than isolated per-query corrections: its outputs capture regularities that can be accumulated across examples and reused without calling the hypernetwork on every new query. Thus, the same mechanism can support both query-specific adaptation and a persistent shared adapter built from previously observed inputs. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We study how much adaptation signal can be extracted from a single query. Our results show that this is sufficient to generate useful query-conditioned LoRA updates and distributions over plausible updates. Distributional hypernetworks further improve the predicted update and enable test-time scaling through weight sampling. Sampling different adapted models is competitive with, and often stronger than, sampling more outputs from fixed weights. Finally, the generated updates exhibit reusable structure across queries, supporting both per-query and shared task-level adaptation. 9Learning to Predict Distributions over Weight Updates for Test-Time Adaptation 

## 7 LIMITATIONS 

Our experiments focus on 1–4B parameter models and LoRA-based adaptation, and it remains to be seen how the behavior scales to substantially larger models. The hypernetwork must also be kept in memory and adds one forward pass per query, but inference remains considerably lighter than optimization-based adaptation since it requires no backward pass, optimizer state, or iterative updates. Given the consistent gains from generated and sampled updates, scaling this approach to larger models and more efficient hypernetworks is a promising direction for future work.

## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Test-Time Training. Test-time training adapts a model to the current input by updating its param-eters during inference. Early methods construct a self-supervised objective from the test input and take one or more gradient steps to adapt to distribution shift [13, 14]. More recent work applies the same idea to language models, including sample-specific optimization, test-time learning, and adaptation to long contexts [15–18]. These methods differ in the objective and the parameters being updated, but all perform optimization after seeing the test input. Our goal is complementary. We train the adaptation mechanism beforehand so that a useful update can be produced directly from the query without gradient-based optimization at test time. 

Gradient-Free Adaptation. Hypernetworks provide a natural way to amortize parameter adapta-tion by learning a network that generates the parameters of another network [2]. This idea is closely related to fast weights, where a set of learned parameters controls another set of parameters that can change more rapidly [3, 4]. In language models, HyperTuning and HINT generate parameter-efficient adaptations from demonstrations or instructions [7, 8], while Text-to-LoRA generates a LoRA directly from a natural-language task description [6]. More recent work such as SHINE and Doc-to-LoRA maps longer contexts into LoRA weights in a single pass [9, 10], and example-based hypernetworks generate adaptations from examples from related domains [11]. HyperDecoders move closer to instance-level adaptation by generating a separate decoder adaptation from each in-put [12]. These approaches show that useful parameter updates can be generated without test-time backpropagation. Our setting differs in that the semantic information comes only from the current query and the hypernetwork predicts a distribution over possible LoRA updates rather than a single adaptation. 

Distributions over Weights. Learning distributions over neural network parameters has a long history in Bayesian neural networks and generative weight models. Bayes by Backpropagation learns distributions over weights through variational inference [32], while Bayesian Hypernetworks, implicit weight uncertainty, and HyperGAN use learned generators to represent richer distributions over neural network parameters [19–21]. In these approaches, samples typically represent uncer-tainty or different models drawn from a global learned weight distribution. Several recent methods study distributions specifically in the low-rank adaptation space. ICM-LoRA learns a conditional generative model from a collection of LoRAs that were optimized be-forehand [22]. DiffLoRA similarly learns to generate LoRA weights from previously constructed personalization adapters using a diffusion model [23]. These methods provide important evidence that LoRA weights can be modeled with a conditional generative distribution, but the distribution is learned by reproducing previously optimized adapters. Bayesian LoRA methods take a differ-ent view. Laplace-LoRA constructs a distribution around a fitted LoRA after adaptation, while BLoB learns a variational distribution over LoRA parameters during fine-tuning [24, 25]. Amor-tized Bayesian meta-learning similarly represents uncertainty over task-specific low-rank adapta-tions [26]. LoRA ensembles obtain weight diversity by independently optimizing several adapters for the same task [27]. Our distribution is different in both how it is produced and how it is used. It is conditioned on each query and trained directly through the frozen language model’s response loss rather than by recon-structing a set of adapters or approximating a task-level posterior. Samples from the distribution are treated as alternative adaptations for the current query rather than only as uncertainty estimates. This also means that the predicted distribution may change from one query to the next, even when the queries come from the same benchmark or task. 

Test-Time Scaling. Most test-time scaling methods keep the model parameters fixed and spend additional compute by generating and selecting among more output sequences [40–42]. Other work shows that useful variation can also exist in weight space. AdapterSoup and Model Soups aver-age independently trained parameters [43, 44], while LoRAHub, LoRARetriever, and LoRA-Flow retrieve or combine adapters for a new input or task [39, 45, 46]. Model Swarms searches among and modifies model experts during inference, and evolutionary weight perturbations provide another way to search for diverse solutions in parameter space [47, 48]. These results motivate weight space itself as a useful source of test-time diversity. 14 Learning to Predict Distributions over Weight Updates for Test-Time Adaptation Our approach learns this search distribution during training. At test time, the hypernetwork directly produces a distribution from which multiple adapted models can be sampled. We study this in two settings. The regression hypernetwork predicts a distribution directly over LoRA weights, while the mixing hypernetwork predicts a distribution over combinations of reference LoRAs. The latter is related to adapter retrieval and composition, but instead of returning one composition, it defines several possible adaptations that can be explored with additional test-time compute. 

## B HYPERNETWORK DETAILS 

B.1 HYPERNETWORK ARCHITECTURE 

Figure 6 shows the architecture used for the hypernetwork. The hypernetwork is conditioned on three inputs: the token-level representation of the input query, a learned layer embedding, and a learned module embedding. The query is first passed through the frozen language model to ob-tain a sequence of hidden states. The hidden states are projected to the internal dimension of the hypernetwork before being used as the context representation. We use this pair representation as the query to a cross-attention layer, while the sequence of query token representations acts as the keys and values. Thus, each (layer , module ) pair can attend to a different part of the input when constructing its LoRA parameters. The cross-attention output is combined with the pair representation through a residual connection and then processed by eight residual MLP blocks. A final head projector consisting of LayerNorm, a linear layer, and a SiLU activation produces the representation used by the output heads. The output layer contains separate heads for the two LoRA factors A and B. For a distributional version of the hypernetwork with diagonal covariance, the same features are used to predict both the mean and element-wise standard deviation of each factor, μA(x), σ A(x), μ B(x), and σB(x). A sampled LoRA is obtained through the reparameterization A = μA(x) + σA(x) ⊙ ϵA, ϵA ∼ N (0 , I ), (16) B = μB(x) + σB(x) ⊙ ϵB, ϵB ∼ N (0 , I ), (17) where the noise for A and B is sampled independently. The deterministic hypernetwork uses the same architecture but predicts only the corresponding A and B point estimates. B.2 TRAINING DETAILS 

For the distributional hypernetwork, we sample K candidate LoRA updates for each query and evaluate the language modeling loss for each corresponding adapted model. Under the best-of-

K objective, the candidate with the lowest loss is selected, and the backward pass is performed through that sampled update. Figure 7 illustrates this gradient flow using the colored arrows. The gradient passes from the language modeling loss through the adapted language model, through the generated LoRA update, and finally into the hypernetwork that produced it. Although gradients are propagated through the language model to obtain the learning signal, its pretrained weights remain frozen throughout training. Only the hypernetwork parameters are updated. Because the sampled LoRA is produced through the reparameterized distribution, the same gradient also trains the parameters controlling both the center and spread of the predicted update distribution. ## E WHEN DOES GENERATING AN UPDATE BECOME USEFUL ?

Generating a different update for every query may not always be necessary. If different inputs require similar adaptations, a single shared LoRA may be sufficient. We therefore vary the diversity of the training distribution by training on K domains, with K ranging from 4 to 512, and compare the deterministic hypernetwork with a single LoRA and MoL. Figure 9 shows that the benefit of generation increases with task diversity. On in-domain data, the hypernetwork consistently outperforms the shared LoRA. On unseen domains, the shared LoRA remains competitive at low diversity, but becomes less effective as K increases and a single up-date becomes less representative of what different queries require. MoL provides a stronger query-conditioned baseline, but remains limited to selecting from a fixed set of updates, while the hyper-network can generate a new update for each query. 

Finding 4: Task Diversity Determines When Updates Should Be Generated 

A shared update works well when tasks are similar, but becomes less representative as task diver-sity grows. At high diversity, generating input-conditioned updates scales better than relying on a fixed update or a fixed bank of updates. 

## F LEARNING A DISTRIBUTION OVER WEIGHT UPDATES 

F.1 CHOOSING THE COVARIANCE 

A distributional hypernetwork also requires a choice of how to represent the covariance over its gen-erated updates. We compare diagonal, block diagonal, and full covariance parameterizations. Since directly predicting the latter two would be expensive, we represent them using low-rank factoriza-tions. Figure 2(left) shows that a more expressive covariance provides little benefit. The three parame-terizations perform similarly at different sampling budgets, while the diagonal covariance performs slightly better as the number of samples increases. At K = 8 , for example, the diagonal covari-ance reaches approximately 0.75 mean accuracy, compared with 0.74 for block diagonal and 0.68 for full covariance. We therefore use a diagonal covariance throughout the remaining experiments, since it is both simpler and cheaper to parameterize. The results shown are mean accuracy across 8 benchmarks. We therefore compare objectives that place different amounts of pressure on the sampled distribu-tion. The mean objective optimizes all sampled updates, while the median and top fraction objec-tives require progressively smaller portions of the distribution to perform well. Best-of-K places the weakest constraint on the full distribution, since only the best sampled update receives the optimiza-tion signal. The choice of K also controls the balance between guiding the center of the distribution and pre-serving its spread. With a small finite K, the best sample remains closely coupled to the mean, providing a useful signal for where the mean should move. As K increases, the objective becomes more tolerant of a wider distribution, since a good sample can be found farther from the mean. Top fraction objectives provide an intermediate tradeoff, applying more pressure on the mean and favor-ing a smaller variance than best-of-K, while still avoiding the stronger contraction encouraged by the mean objective. We measure the resulting coverage using oracle performance as the number of weight samples in-creases. As shown in Figure 2(right), the difference becomes clear as the sampling budget grows. Thus, objectives that require more of the distribution to perform well leave substantially less room for search, while best-of-K retains the largest searchable region. Mode collapse is a distinct failure mode in generative modeling, where a learned distribution fails to represent multiple valid modes [49]. Our concern here is different: the predicted variance may shrink toward zero. Mode collapse concerns whether a model covers several distinct valid target modes. Distribution collapse instead refers to the predicted variance shrinking toward zero, so that repeated samples produce the same update. Our goal here is not to guarantee coverage of every possible mode, but to retain variation in weight space for sampling to remain useful at test-time. 

Finding 5: Best-of-K Loss Preserves a Searchable Weight Distribution 

Objectives that require many sampled updates to perform well encourage the learned distribution to contract. Best-of-K places less pressure on the full distribution and retains substantially more coverage as the weight sampling budget increases.
