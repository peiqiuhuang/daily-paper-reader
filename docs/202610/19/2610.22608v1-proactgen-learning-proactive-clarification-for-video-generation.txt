ABSTRACT 

Recent advances in video generation let users create high-quality videos from natu-ral language descriptions, yet obtaining results that match user intent remains time-consuming: individual generations are computationally expensive, and underspec-ified prompts force repeated cycles of generation, inspection, and revision. Ex-isting video prompt optimization methods improve generation through automatic rewriting, but cannot supply the ideas and preferences that were not in the ini-tial description. We propose ProactGen, a video generation framework that learns proactive clarification, eliciting user intent through questions grounded in gener-ated video drafts. Specifically, we train a vision-language model as a questioning policy with reinforcement learning: observing the initial prompt and a video draft, it learns to ask the questions most valuable to downstream generation, rewarded by the alignment between the final generated video and a target reference. We further find that effective questioning does not require drafts of final-output quality, and exploit this property by generating drafts with fewer denoising steps, keeping the added cost of clarification low. Experiments show that ProactGen improves target alignment over direct generation by at least 1.8× the gain of the strongest base-lines. Moreover, a single round of ProactGen already exceeds the alignment that the strongest baseline reaches after four refinement rounds, in 29% of its time. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Rapid progress in video generation has enabled ordinary users to create high-quality videos from a single natural language description (Wan et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). Turn-ing that description into a video that matches what the user had in mind, however, remains time-consuming, for two compounding reasons. First, video generation is expensive: every full genera-tion runs tens of denoising steps over a spatiotemporal latent space and costs substantial computation and waiting time. Second, users rarely manage to specify everything they want in the initial prompt. Details such as the subject’s appearance, the manner of an action, the lighting, or the camera mo-tion are often recognized as missing or misinterpreted only after the user sees the result (Hutchinson et al., 2022; Hahn et al., 2024). Together, these difficulties force users through repeated cycles of generation, inspection, and revision, each paid for with a costly full generation. Existing work addresses this problem mainly by rewriting the prompt. Video prompt optimization methods (Gao et al., 2025a; Cheng et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a) use language mod-els to expand or rewrite the initial prompt into a form the generator handles well, and some further refine the rewrite with feedback from generated videos (Gao et al., 2025b; Long et al., 2026). These methods improve how the available information is expressed, but cannot supply the ideas and prefer-ences that were never in the initial description: the generator has no way of knowing what color the user pictured for the subject or whether the camera should push in or orbit, and a rewrite can only substitute a guess. These results show that interaction can recover intent that rewriting cannot, and that which question to ask can be learned from what happens af-terwards. In these settings, however, questions are selected from the text of the request or a belief state parsed from it, and learning, where present, is supervised by a text response or by the user’s choice among candidates. How to choose questions from what a video generator actually produces for a given prompt, and to learn that choice from the final video, remains open; to our knowledge, proactive clarification has not been studied in video generation. We propose ProactGen, a video generation framework that learns proactive clarification: its questions are grounded in a generated draft, and what to ask is learned from the final generation outcome. Given the initial prompt, the generator first produces a video draft; a questioning policy observes both the prompt and the draft and asks a small number of scoped questions; the user’s answers are merged into a refined prompt, from which the generator produces the final video. The policy is trained with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), rewarding the alignment between the final video and a target reference, so it learns to read the defaults and uncertainties the draft exposes and to ask for the information that matters most to downstream generation. Questioning needs a draft, and a draft is itself a video generation; if clarification had to wait for a full-quality generation, its benefit to the user’s total time would be largely offset. We observe that effective questioning does not require a draft of final-output quality: questions depend on how the draft renders the subject, layout, action, and camera, and diffusion models settle these aspects early in denoising, with later steps mainly refining texture and detail (Balaji et al., 2022). ProactGen therefore generates drafts with a greatly reduced number of denoising steps and spends the full budget only on the final video. What the draft gives up is the late-stage detail that questioning does not depend on, so clarification adds little beyond a low-cost draft to direct generation while the cues that questioning relies on are largely preserved. We evaluate ProactGen with a simulated user whose intent is given by a hidden reference video. On 500 samples from InstanceCap (Fan et al., 2025), ProactGen achieves higher target alignment than direct generation, representative video prompt optimization methods, and a proactive clarification method adapted to video on all three metrics, and its gain over direct generation is at least 1.8× that of the strongest baselines. Ablations show that reinforcement learning brings a clear gain and that the benefit of observing the draft emerges only after training. Measured in time, one round of ProactGen, about 16 s, already exceeds the alignment reached by four rounds of automatic rewriting or 15 turns of proactive clarification, and further rounds add further gains. 2Our contributions are as follows: • To our knowledge, ProactGen is the first to bring proactive clarification to video generation; we build a reproducible simulated-user protocol and compare with prompt optimization and proactive clarification baselines. • We propose a reinforcement learning framework in which the questioning policy learns, from the final generation outcome, what to ask about a generated draft, targeting the defaults the generator fills in on the sample at hand. • We find that effective questioning does not need final-quality drafts and use few-step drafts for clarification, which at equal time buys more alignment than rewriting. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Prompt optimization. One line of work improves generation by automatically rewriting the user’s prompt. In image generation, Promptist (Hao et al., 2023) trains a rewriter with reinforcement learn-ing using aesthetic and relevance scores of the generated images as reward, and RePrompt (Wu et al., 2026) adds chain-of-thought reasoning supervised by multiple reward models. In video generation, RAPO (Gao et al., 2025a) retrieves modifiers from the training corpus and trains a branch selector, VPO (Cheng et al., 2025) and Prompt-A-Video (Ji et al., 2025) train rewriters from video-level pref-erences or reward-model signals, and SCMAPR (Yang et al., 2026a) runs a multi-agent pipeline of scenario routing, rewriting, and text-level semantic verification. Recent methods further refine the prompt at inference with feedback from generated videos: RAPO++ (Gao et al., 2025b) inspects the output with a vision-language model and corrects the prompt, and VISTA (Long et al., 2026) self-improves through a loop of multi-agent critique. Whether or not they look at generated output, the input of these methods is limited to what the user already wrote, and for preferences absent from the prompt they can only fall back on the model’s prior. ProactGen is complementary: its learned action acquires the missing information from the user. 

Interactive clarification. A second line of work lets the system interact with the user to obtain intent. In language modeling, clarification questions have been trained with the quality of the down-stream answer as the signal (Andukuri et al., 2024; Dou & Liu, 2026; Zhang et al., 2025), or ambiguity has been detected from disagreement among generated code solutions (Mu et al., 2023), with text as the downstream product. In image generation, Proactive T2I (Hahn et al., 2024) has a language model parse entities, attributes, and relations from the prompt into a belief graph, selects uncer-tain and important elements to ask the user about, and proposes an automatic evaluation protocol with a simulated user driven by a reference image; its questioning policies are heuristic or prompted. PASTA (Nabati et al., 2024) learns with offline reinforcement learning which set of candidate prompts to present to the user, APPO (Li et al., 2026) lets the user choose among rendered candidates and optimizes the prompt accordingly, and DiffChat (Wang et al., 2024) and DialogGen (Huang et al., 2025) revise prompts from user editing instructions or hold multi-turn dialogue. In the video domain, IC-Seg (Yang et al., 2026b) uses hierarchical GRPO to learn when and how to clarify in referring segmentation. In ProactGen, questions are grounded in a video draft the generator produced for the sample at hand, and question selection is learned with the alignment of the final generated video to the target as the reward. 

3 METHOD 

ProactGen lets the system proactively obtain, within a single generation pipeline, the information missing from the user’s initial prompt, so that the final video comes closer to what the user intends. To train and evaluate this ability reproducibly, we follow the simulated-user protocol of proactive T2I (Hahn et al., 2024): a reference video plays the role of v⋆, and a simulated user with access to it answers the questions. The reference is visible only to the simulated user and the reward computation; neither the questioning policy nor the generator can access it, and both see only p0 and the draft the system generates. The evaluation measure is Align (ˆ v, v ⋆), the visual alignment between final video and reference (§3.3). 3.2 DRAFT-GROUNDED CLARIFICATION PIPELINE Figure 2 shows the interaction pipeline of ProactGen. Given p0, it proceeds in four steps. 

Draft generation. The generator produces a video draft d = Gdraft (p0). The draft shows the ques-tioning policy what the generator does with this prompt: which details it fills in by default and where its output departs from the description. It uses a greatly reduced denoising budget (§3.4). 

Questioning. The questioning policy πθ is a vision-language model that takes p0 and several frames uniformly sampled from the draft and outputs a small set of questions q ∼ πθ (· | p0, d ). Each question targets a single piece of information, such as the appearance of an object, the manner of an action, or the camera motion; the policy may neither bundle unrelated items into one question nor request a full description of the video. These constraints keep questions answerable by a real user and prevent reward hacking by asking for the entire description at once. 

Answering. The simulated user answers each question briefly from the reference video, under con-straints: it addresses only what is asked and adds no facts beyond it, and leaves unanswered any question that falls outside the agreed scope (marked invalid) or cannot be verified from the reference (marked unknown). Answer length is bounded, which together with the constraints on questioning keeps a single exchange from yielding a full description. 

Refinement and final generation. A frozen language model merges p0 with all valid question-answer pairs into a refined prompt p1, keeping only content supported by p0 or the answers; the generator then produces the final video ˆv = G(p1) at full budget. Only πθ is learnable in this pipeline; the generator, the simulated user, and the refinement model are frozen. This attributes the learning signal to what was asked and lets ProactGen pair with an off-the-shelf video generator without modifying it. The pipeline can be iterated. When the user wants further correction, the policy observes a new draft generated from the refined prompt p1, or the final video of the previous round, asks again with p1,4and the answers are merged into p1 to give p2. Every round follows the same four steps, so the policy needs no separate training for multiple rounds. The method itself does not prescribe whether later rounds observe a draft or a full video; in §4.4 we evaluate multi-round interaction where every round observes a low-cost draft and only the last runs full generation. 3.3 LEARNING TO ASK FROM GENERATION OUTCOMES We want the questioning policy to select the questions most valuable to downstream generation, a value that can only be observed after the answers are merged and the final video generated. We there-fore treat the pipeline as the environment, questioning as the action, and the final video’s alignment as the reward, and train πθ with reinforcement learning.

Optimization. We use GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). For each training sample (p0, d, v ⋆), we draw K

question sets {q(1) , . . . , q (K)} from the current policy; each independently goes through answering, refinement, and final generation to obtain its reward r(i). Rewards are standardized within the group into advantages A(i) = ( r(i) − ¯r)/ σr , and the policy is updated with a clipped importance-ratio objective under a KL penalty toward the base model. Candidates in a group share the same initial noise for final generation, which reduces the effect of noise randomness on reward differences, and the within-group advantage removes differences in the absolute level of alignment across samples. 

Training data. Training samples are built from real videos with detailed captions: the video serves as the reference v⋆, and p0 is obtained by masking several information slots in the caption and rewriting the rest. Such a p0 keeps the subject and the gist but lacks several details, mirroring the incomplete-ness of real user prompts, and guarantees that every training sample contains missing information that can be asked for. At evaluation no masking is applied, and all additional information from the simulated user comes from the reference video and its caption. 3.4 LOW-COST DRAFTS FOR CLARIFICATION The pipeline above needs a draft before asking, and a draft is itself a video generation; if it used the same budget as the final generation, clarification would double the total generation cost, working against the goal of getting the user a satisfactory video sooner. We observe that what questioning needs from a draft differs from what the user needs from the final video: diffusion models largely form the subject, layout, action, and camera early in denoising and mainly refine texture and detail later (Balaji et al., 2022), and it is the former that questioning relies on. A draft from far fewer steps is therefore visibly coarse, yet already exposes how the generator interprets p0.ProactGen therefore generates drafts with few denoising steps and keeps the full budget for the final generation. The few-step draft uses a timestep schedule different from that of full generation, allo-cating more of its steps to the high-noise phase where content and layout are decided. The policy is trained on low-cost drafts from the start, so it learns to extract useful information from a coarse draft. Since the final generation budget is unchanged, the final generation itself is unaffected by the number of draft steps. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

Our experiments answer four questions. (1) Does ProactGen bring the final video closer to the user’s target than direct generation, video prompt optimization, and a proactive clarification method adapted to video (§4.2)? (2) Does reinforcement learning produce better question selection, and what role does observing the draft play (§4.3)? (3) At equal time, does ProactGen buy more alignment than other methods, and what do low-cost drafts contribute (§4.4)? (4) What does the trained policy actually ask (§4.5)? 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Task and data. We evaluate text-to-video generation under the simulated-user protocol of §3.1, with two evaluation sets. The first is 500 videos from InstanceCap (Fan et al., 2025), with p0 the original caption without masking. The second is built on MiraData (Ju et al., 2024): since the official MiraBench evaluation set is not released, we sample 500 MiraData videos disjoint from our training set, use the short captions that come with the dataset as p0 without rewriting, and evaluate with the text–video alignment metrics of MiraBench (below); we denote this set MiraBench-500. The training set comprises about 15K MiraData videos with detailed captions, disjoint from both evaluation sets; the training p0 is obtained by masking 2 to 5 information slots (appearance, scene, lighting, action, camera, and so on) in the caption and rewriting the rest with a language model. 

Models and implementation. The generator is Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (Wan et al., 2025), producing 49-frame videos at 832 ×480; final generation uses 20 denoising steps, and drafts use 8 during both training and testing. The questioning policy is initialized from Qwen3-VL-8B (Bai et al., 2025) and fully fine-tuned; it observes 8 frames of the draft and asks 1 to 5 questions per sample. The simulated user and the refinement model are both frozen Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026); the simulated user observes 8 frames of the reference video together with its caption and answers in at most 10 English words. The training reward is the mean DreamSim over 8 aligned frames (§3.3), with an invalid-question penalty coefficient of 0.01 capped at 0.05. GRPO uses a group size of 8 with shared initial noise within a group, a KL coefficient of 0.04, and a learning rate of 10 −6, and trains for one epoch of 1954 steps. Each reward requires answering, refinement, and a full video generation, which dominates the cost of training. To reduce it, we run rollouts one step off-policy following AReaL (Fu et al., 2026), so that generating and scoring one batch overlaps with the policy update on the previous batch, and serve the generator with LightX2V (Contributors, 2025); together these reduce the wall-clock time per update considerably. All compared conditions share the same initial noise for final generation on each sample. 

Metrics. Our primary metric is the DreamSim-DTW appearance similarity of Animation2Code (Ji et al., 2026): we uniformly sample 49 frames from the final video and the reference, align the two sequences with dynamic time warping (DTW), and average DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023) similarity along the aligned path (higher is better). We additionally report two independent video similarity 6measures not used in training: the latent distance under the CogVideoX (Yang et al., 2025) 3D-VAE (lower is better) and the DnS (Kordopatis-Zilos et al., 2022) video retrieval similarity (higher is better). On MiraBench-500 we use the text–video alignment metrics of MiraBench (Ju et al., 2024): ViCLIP similarity between the final video and each structured caption of the reference (main object, background, style, camera) and its dense caption, the last denoted Overall. All numbers are means over the same 500 samples of each set; when a method fails on a sample (for example, a parsing error in multi-turn dialogue), the sample takes its Direct result. 

Baselines. Direct generates from p0 with neither questions nor rewriting. SCMAPR (Yang et al., 2026a) is a representative video prompt optimization method that routes p0 by scenario, rewrites, and verifies semantics in a multi-agent pipeline; we run it with its original DeepSeek-V3.2 backend. It has no access to the user. RAPO++-SSPO (Gao et al., 2025b) is a representative method that iteratively rewrites the prompt per sample with feedback from generated videos; we adapt its official implementation to our generator and language model, rewrite once per round using the evaluation of the generated video as feedback, and report budgets of 1 and 4 rounds. It likewise has no access to the user. Proactive T2I (Hahn et al., 2024) is a proactive clarification method for image generation that we adapt to video, keeping its belief-graph construction, uncertainty- and importance-based question selection, and turn-by-turn prompt updating, with the same simulated user answering from the reference video. Since its original Gemini 1.5 Pro backend is no longer available, we replace it with the Qwen3.8-27B used in our pipeline, and report budgets of 5 and 15 question turns. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS Table 1 reports the main results on 500 samples. ProactGen outperforms all baselines on all three metrics: its DreamSim-DTW gain over Direct is 0.020 , more than twice the gain of Proactive T2I with 15 turns ( 0.009 ), using a single round of at most 5 questions, and it is 0.009 and 0.010 above RAPO++-SSPO with 4 rounds and Proactive T2I with 15 turns, respectively. In this task the missing information is the user’s specific preferences, which rewriting can only guess from the model’s prior whether or not it observes generated output; Proactive T2I improves with more turns, confirming that acquiring information from the user helps. The gap between ProactGen and Proactive T2I has two sources: ProactGen asks about what the generator actually produces for the sample, as exposed by the draft, whereas Proactive T2I derives its questions from a text-based belief state; and Proact-Gen’s question selection is learned with the final outcome as the signal. We examine the two factors separately in §4.3. On MiraBench-500, whose metrics measure alignment with the captions of the reference rather than with the reference video itself, the picture is the same: ProactGen attains the highest Overall, background, style, and camera alignment. 4.3 ABLATION: REINFORCEMENT LEARNING AND DRAFT OBSERVATION To separate the contributions of reinforcement learning and draft observation, we compare four con-ditions: the untrained base policy and the trained policy, each asking either with the draft or from text only, with the rest of the pipeline unchanged; the text-only trained policy is trained independently from the base without observing a draft (Table 2).

In the first, the prompt gives the person, the car, and “close-ups of the headlights,” but not whom the person faces or how the camera moves; the draft defaults to having him face the car with the camera parked on the front end, two choices that set the composition of the whole clip. In the second, the prompt says “speaks into microphones” and “another man partially visible,” but not who holds the microphones, what the other man looks like, or how tight the framing is; the draft resolves these into the subject holding a single microphone himself, the second man absent, and a tight framing. We also observe failures: the simulated user returns “unknown” for some questions, or the answer is correct but the generator does not follow the refined prompt; the latter shows that part of the remaining gap lies in the generator’s instruction following, not in the questions. 

5 CONCLUSION 

We presented ProactGen, a video generation framework that learns proactive clarification, eliciting user intent through questions grounded in a generated draft. A questioning policy observes the initial prompt and a generated draft, selects the questions most valuable to downstream generation, and is trained with reinforcement learning using the alignment between the final video and the target as the reward. We further found that effective questioning does not require final-quality drafts, and used low-cost drafts from few denoising steps to support clarification. With a simulated user holding a hidden reference video, ProactGen improves target alignment over direct generation by at least 

1.8× the gain of the strongest video prompt optimization and proactive clarification baselines; one round of interaction costs about as much as one round of automatic rewriting yet already exceeds the alignment the baselines reach after several rounds; and the time saved by low-cost drafts accumulates as further rounds add further gains, even though the policy was trained only in the single-round setting. Extending ProactGen to real users, and embedding multiple rounds of clarification within a single denoising process to further reduce computation, remain promising directions for future work. 9AI USE STATEMENT We used generative AI tools in the following ways. A.1 TRAINING DATA CONSTRUCTION Training samples come from MiraData (Ju et al., 2024) video clips and their structured captions, 15,625 unique samples in total. Each full caption is parsed into information slots: static slots cover appearance, scene, lighting, and style; dynamic slots cover action, interaction, temporal relation, motion, and camera. The subject is always kept. For each sample, a hash of the sample ID and a fixed salt deterministically selects 2 to 5 slots to mask, at least one static and one dynamic; masking an action slot also masks the interaction and temporal-relation slots that depend on it. The remaining facts are rewritten by a language model (Qwen3.8-27B, temperature 0) into one natural text-to-video prompt as p0, and the rewrite must not leak the masked facts. A.2 MIRABENCH-500 The official MiraBench (Ju et al., 2024) evaluation set (150 prompts) is not released. From the downloaded MiraData clip pool we exclude all training clips and clips whose time span overlaps a training clip, then sample 500 clips at random and freeze the set, using the official short caption as p0 and the corresponding clip as the reference. The simulated user answers from 8 frames of the reference and the official five-field structured caption. Metrics are the five MiraBench ViCLIP text–video alignments (subject, background, style, camera, dense caption) with the official ViCLIP weights and 32-token truncation; Overall is the alignment with the dense caption. A.3 PROMPTS AND PROTOCOLS 

Questioning policy. The system prompt asks the policy to pose 1 to 5 English questions of at most 40 words each, given the initial prompt and 8 frames of the draft; each question is limited to one bounded visual fact, and the policy must not answer the questions, bundle independent facts, list alternatives, or request a complete description. The full system prompt is in Appendix B. 

Simulated user. The simulated user receives 8 frames of the reference video and the full target caption, and returns one of three states per question: VALID (with an answer of at most 10 words), UNKNOWN (insufficient evidence), or INVALID (outside the agreed scope). Directly visible facts follow the reference video; temporal, motion, camera, and naming information that sparse frames cannot confirm is supplemented from the caption; on conflict the video wins and the conflict is recorded. One repair call is allowed after a parse failure. The full system prompt is in Appendix B. 

Prompt refinement. The refinement model receives p0 and the numbered VALID question-answer pairs and outputs a natural-language prompt in the style expected by the generator. It must preserve the facts already in p0, respect confirmations, corrections, and negations in the answers, introduce no entity supported by neither p0 nor the answers, and treat unanswered questions as missing informa-tion. If no VALID answer exists, p0 is used unchanged. The full system prompt is in Appendix B. 

Generator settings. Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, 49 frames at 24 fps and 832 ×480, UniPC scheduler. Final generation uses 20 steps, timestep shift 12, guidance scale 3.0, and a fixed Chinese negative prompt. Drafts use 8 steps and timestep shift 3, all else equal. Initial noise is generated on CPU in FP32 from a fixed seed and shared across all conditions of a sample and across the candidates of a GRPO group. A.4 TRAINING DETAILS The policy is Qwen3-VL-8B, fully fine-tuned with the vision encoder and projector frozen. GRPO uses group size 8, 8 prompts per step for 64 candidates, gradient accumulation 8, per-device batch size 1, on 8 GPUs. Learning rate 10 −6, warmup ratio 0.03, weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping 1.0, KL coefficient β = 0 .04 , sampling temperature 1.0 and top-p 0.9, with within-group reward normalization. Training runs 1 epoch of 1954 steps in bf16. We use the final checkpoint (step 1954) fixed before training, with no early stopping or checkpoint selection. The reward is the mean 8-frame aligned DreamSim (ensemble variant) minus the invalid-question penalty (0.01 per question, capped at 0.05); the final video is scored on raw RGB frames, and the reference is center-cropped and resized to 832 ×480. 13 A.5 EVALUATION METRIC The evaluation metric DreamSim-DTW follows the appearance similarity of Animation2Code (Ji et al., 2026) (DreamSim distance as the DTW cost, score = mean similarity along the optimal path) and shares the DreamSim model weights and the 832 ×480 spatial preprocessing with the training reward, differing in frame sampling and aggregation. A.7 BASELINE ADAPTATION 

Proactive T2I. We keep the core logic of the original method: a language model parses entities, attributes, and relations from the prompt into a belief graph, selects questions by uncertainty and importance, and updates the belief and the prompt with each round of answers. Gemini 1.5 Pro used by the original is no longer served; we replace it with the Qwen3.8-27B used elsewhere in this paper. As a public reference that the replacement does not weaken the baseline, the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index gives Qwen3.8-27B about 20 in non-reasoning mode and Gemini 1.5 Pro (September 2024) an estimated 8 (Artificial Analysis, 2026); the index is not a direct measure 14 for this task and only confirms that the swap is not an obvious downgrade in general capability. The simulated user answers from 8 frames of the reference video with the same simulated-user model as ProactGen. The original does not cap answers at 10 words, so it may obtain more information per turn than ProactGen. The 5-turn result is the prompt saved at the end of turn 5 of the 15-turn run. Of 500 samples, 20 fail at 5 turns and 36 at 15 turns in the parsing or dialogue stage; the raw failures are kept, and aggregate metrics use the same-sample Direct video and scores. This baseline tests what proactive questioning achieves without generation grounding and without learning. 

SCMAPR. We use the official implementation with its original backend DeepSeek-V3.2 (Liu et al., 2025) and BGE-M3 embeddings, running the full routing, strategy generation, rewriting, verification, and conditional revision pipeline on the 500 p0 with at most 5 revision rounds, 7702 API calls in total. It has no access to the user and represents how far rewriting alone can go. 

RAPO++-SSPO. We adapt the official implementation to our generator and language model and run 4 refinement rounds, each using a semantic score and a physical-consistency verdict on the generated video as feedback; among the candidates formed by the initial prompt and each round’s rewrite, one is selected by a weighted ranking over the semantic score, the physics verdict, and video similarity. The main text reports budgets of 1 round (2 candidates) and 4 rounds (5 candidates). It has no access to the user and tests how far automatic rewriting with generation feedback can go. 

Methods not compared. We do not compare with RAPO (Gao et al., 2025a), whose retrieval corpus and branch selector are bound to the training data of a specific generator and cannot be fairly repro-duced on ours. For the same reason, Stage 1 of RAPO++ (Gao et al., 2025b) (retrieval-based rewrit-ing aligned with the generator’s training data) and Stage 3 (fine-tuning the rewriter on prompt pairs collected at test time) are tied to a specific generator; we compare only with its generator-agnostic test-time stage, SSPO. Questions from the trained policy are more often answerable from the reference and draw more informative an-swers. 

Training curve. During training, the 8-frame DreamSim mean is evaluated every 200 steps on a fixed set of 96 development samples: 0.668 at step 200, 0.675 at step 1000, 0.679 at step 1400 (the highest), and 0.674 at step 1954. We use the final checkpoint fixed before training and do not select by the development set. 

Draft steps. A policy trained and run with 20-step full-budget drafts reaches 0.681 after one round versus 0.678 with 8-step drafts (§4.4); the two are independently trained policies and the difference is not significant under the paired interval. 

Failure cases. We observe three kinds of failure. (1) The simulated user returns UNKNOWN: the question is legal but neither the reference video nor the caption determines the answer, for example the color of an off-screen object; such questions do not enter the refined prompt. (2) Merging errors: the refinement model occasionally attributes an answer to the wrong subject, or introduces an entity mentioned only in the question when the answer is a negation; the system prompt forbids this, but a few samples still show it. (3) The generator does not follow the refined prompt: the answer is correct and written into the prompt, yet the final video does not reflect it, for example a requested camera motion or a second person is missing; these failures indicate that part of the remaining gap lies in the generator’s instruction following. All cases keep their sample IDs and raw question–answer records and will be released with the code.
