Lower-bound contribution.
A long chain accumulates descent over many coordinates, but placing its
entire value inside a narrow lower perturbation jeopardizes global regularity. A naive quadratic lift also lets
distant lower-variable queries amplify the hidden gradient. We separate extensive descent from a bounded
frontier, embedding only the frontier through a sine. This keeps the full hidden gradient globally small and
permits simultaneous control of all prescribed derivatives. A deferred-prefix random-rotation argument
covers unrestricted algorithms. The lower problem is scalar, and exact upper gradients already suffice for
every lower bound.

Adjoints, numerical differences, and sample regularity.
FdeHBO already combines finite-difference
Hessian/Jacobian-vector approximations with a projected adjoint (Yang et al., 2023). Its paired differences and
momentum estimators reuse randomness, and its assumptions include samplewise smoothness and stochastic
Hessian bounds. Our architecture is related, but our estimates use independently sampled population gradients
without those assumptions. The new estimates concern uniform weight norms and fully counted allocations.
Polynomial differentiation is classical (Fornberg, 1988), and the projected-adjoint architecture is already
present in FdeHBO.
Lower bounds and other interfaces.
The 𝜖−6 lower bound of Kwon et al. (2024) permits gradients that are
unbiased only near 𝑦∗(𝑥), while also providing an approximate lower minimizer. Our lower construction instead
maintains global unbiasedness at arbitrary queries through a globally bounded full-gradient perturbation.
Condition-number bounds instead sharpen another axis (Ji, 2026; Chen et al., 2026a); the latter’s revised
SFO result has stochastic term Ω(𝜅9/2
𝑦𝜖−4), where 𝜅𝑦is the lower-level condition number, for constrained,
zero-respecting algorithms. Our characterization fixes these structural parameters and varies accuracy and
lower-variable regularity. SPABA uses variance reduction and a different derivative/sampling interface
(Chu et al., 2024). The recent Stoc-SGHA method obtains stochastic exponent six, and exponent four with
additional lower-level stochastic smoothness; its expectation guarantees also assume bounded iterates (Gu et al.,

(Carmon et al., 2020; Arjevani et al., 2023); our contribution is their globally regular bilevel realization and
order-uniform scaling. Appendix I records the detailed interfaces and version-specific comparisons.

7
Discussion and Conclusion
The matching law separates fixed-order exponents from uniform smoothness gains. For 𝛽> 0, the effective
order saturates around log(1/𝜖)/𝛽, leaving the unavoidable factor log2𝛽(1/𝜖). For 𝛽= 0, finitely many
derivatives at order 𝑂(log(1/𝜖)) suffice for Θ(𝜖−4). Qualitative analyticity does not imply either quantitative
normalization: the baseline hard family is entire in 𝑦but has accuracy-dependent higher-derivative growth.
The theorem fixes 𝑀, Λ, 𝛽and all baseline budgets while allowing the hardness dimension to grow. It
characterizes lower-variable derivative envelopes; joint analyticity, optimal condition-number dependence,
and general infinite derivative sequences remain natural extensions. Other observation models and stronger
output guarantees also lead to distinct complexity questions.
The numerical study complements this characterization in two ways: the budget-dependent construction
exhibits the information barrier, and the fixed collection illustrates cost growth under explicitly specified
practical schedules. The latter comparison distinguishes relative growth from absolute sample cost. Testing
order-dependent gains on a larger fixed collection is a useful direction for further experiments.
At the degenerate boundary 𝐿1 = 𝜇, coupling is forced to vanish and the class with possibly noisy upper
gradients has rate Θ(𝜖−4); Appendix H proves this separately. Within the nondegenerate fresh-gradient
model, the uniform theorem identifies both the finite-order exponent and the exact infinite-order overhead,
with globally valid lower-bound responses and fully counted upper-bound samples.
Reproducibility statement
The theoretical appendices supply the interpolation identity, sample allocations, scalar hard functions, global
regularity estimates, and adaptive transcript coupling. Appendix J specifies the numerical instances, output

I.2
Local unbiasedness is a different observation contract
Definition 1.1 of Kwon et al. (2024) introduces a 𝑦∗-aware oracle: a call includes an approximate lower
solution, and gradient unbiasedness is required only within a prescribed neighborhood of the true lower
solution. Their lower bound uses a finite accuracy-dependent radius. Outside that neighborhood an oracle can
supply uninformative responses not equal in expectation to the true gradient. Their upper method also works
for the ordinary globally unbiased oracle (the infinite-radius case), establishing the existing 𝑂(𝜖−6) baseline
upper bound. Their expected-complexity convention and oracle accounting differ from our fixed-budget
formulation. The lower bounds therefore apply to distinct observation contracts.
In our lower instance, the mean of the full vector 𝐺𝑔is ∇𝑔at every real (𝑥, 𝑦), and its conditional
variance is uniformly bounded. There is no accuracy-dependent region outside which the mean guarantee is
suspended. The bounded sine signal, not a permitted oracle bias, prevents far-away 𝑦queries from bypassing
the information lower bound.
I.3
Adjoint-based algorithms and sample reuse
FdeHBO (Yang et al., 2023) is an important methodological antecedent to the projected adjoint in (17). Its
equation (9) differences two gradients with the same sample at 𝑦± 𝛿𝑣, and equations (10)–(12) use shared-
sample momentum differences. Its Assumption 3 imposes samplewise versions of its smoothness conditions
and second-moment bounds on stochastic Hessian/Jacobian blocks. Thus “Hessian-free computation” there
does not mean that only population smoothness and one-point fresh samples are assumed. Our claims concern
the latter contract. The contributions of the upper proof are the uniform Chebyshev weight estimate, variance
accounting under independent node samples, and the absence of multiplicative order and contraction factors
in the final bound. The polynomial differentiation component builds on classical numerical differentiation
(Fornberg, 1988).
