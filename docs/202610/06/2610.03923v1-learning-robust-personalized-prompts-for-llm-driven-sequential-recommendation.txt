XIANG CHEN, Zhejiang University, China With the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs), LLM-driven sequential recommendation has emerged as a promising paradigm that formulates next-item prediction as autoregressive generation conditioned on natural-language prompts. Despite their potential, existing methods exhibit high sensitivity to prompt formulations: even semantically equivalent prompts with minor wording variations can lead to substantial performance fluctuations, undermining robustness and incurring considerable manual engineering costs. While continuous prompt learning offers a principled alternative by replacing discrete templates with learnable vectors, directly applying it to recommendation faces two interacting challenges: (1) lack of personalization , where existing continuous prompt methods learn a single task-level instruction shared across all users, yet recommendation inherently requires user-specific reasoning guidance; and (2) semantic drift ,where continuous prompt vectors may be pushed by gradient updates into regions that the LLM cannot interpret meaningfully, degrading generation quality, a risk further amplified when user-specific signals are injected, as personalization pushes each user’s prompt in a different direction away from the initialization. To address these challenges, we propose LRPRec, a learnable prompting framework tailored for LLM-driven sequential recommendation. LRPRec parameterizes instruction prompts as continuous vectors initialized from discrete templates and introduces two complementary mechanisms: a personalized prompt injection 

mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding and additively injects it into the shared prompts, enabling user-level adaptation while maintaining parameter efficiency; and a semantic drift constraint that regularizes the shared instruction prompts to remain within a trust region around their initialization anchors, preventing optimization from departing the LLM’s effective semantic space. By applying the drift constraint to the shared component while personalization operates additively, our design decouples stability from expressiveness so that neither objective compromises the other. Extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets demonstrate consistent improvements over strong baselines while eliminating the need for manual tuning of background and task inference templates. 1 Introduction 

Sequential recommendation (SR) [1–4] aims to model users’ evolving interests by leveraging their chronological interaction histories, and has become a fundamental component in modern recom-mender systems [5–7]. Early research in this area mainly adopts ID-based techniques [8, 9], which focus on capturing item transition patterns or co-occurrence signals from user behavior sequences. Despite their effectiveness, such approaches are inherently limited when facing data sparsity and cold-start scenarios, where item identifiers alone provide insufficient semantic information [10]. To alleviate these limitations, content-aware SR methods have been proposed to incorporate auxiliary item information, including textual descriptions, visual content, and other multimodal features [11–13]. By enriching item representations with semantic cues beyond interaction logs, these meth-ods attempt to enhance recommendation quality under sparse data conditions. Recently, the rapid development of Large Language Models (LLMs) [16] has opened new opportu-nities for sequential recommendation, owing to their strong capabilities in semantic understanding, reasoning, and generation [17, 18]. Existing LLM-based recommendation approaches can generally be divided into two paradigms [15, 17]: LLM-powered methods, which utilize LLMs as content encoders to generate item representations consumed by conventional recommenders [19], and 

LLM-driven methods, which directly formulate recommendation as prompt-conditioned autoregres-sive generation [20–22]. While LLM-powered approaches benefit from expressive embeddings, they essentially treat LLMs as feature extractors without exploiting their generative capabilities, and often suffer from semantic misalignment between LLM-derived representations and downstream embedding spaces [23, 24]. In contrast, LLM-driven methods leverage the intrinsic reasoning and language understanding of LLMs [25] to produce personalized results end-to-end, representing a more promising direction. Representative methods include TALLRec [26], which reframes recom-mendation as instruction-tuned generation, and LLaRA [22], which combines textual prompts with collaborative embeddings projected from conventional recommenders. However, as illustrated in Figure 1, a key bottleneck of LLM-driven sequential recommendation lies in the heavy dependence on discrete natural-language prompts . The entire recommendation process is conditioned on a prompt that typically concatenates a background instruction , a task description , the interaction sequence , and a candidate pool . We observe that existing methods are highly sensitive to the prompt formulation: even prompts that are semantically equivalent can produce substantially different rankings when small variations in wording, phrasing, or syntax Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-Driven Sequential Recommendation 3

are introduced ( e.g., reordering clauses, replacing synonyms, or changing punctuation). Such prompt sensitivity undermines robustness and reproducibility, and forces practitioners to spend considerable effort on manual prompt engineering before deployment. This issue is particularly problematic in SR, where the same system must reliably serve diverse users, one would expect consistent behavior as long as the essential information (interaction history and candidates) is provided, yet performance fluctuates noticeably across seemingly interchangeable templates. Several prompt learning methods have been proposed to reduce the reliance on handcrafted discrete prompts. POD [27] distills discrete prompts into continuous vectors to bridge user/item IDs and natural language. LASER [28] inserts trainable virtual tokens at both the prefix and suffix of a frozen LLM to inject collaborative signals. PPR [29] generates personalized prefix prompts conditioned on user profiles. RPP [30] employs multi-agent reinforcement learning for instance-wise prompt assembly. PromptAgent [31] uses Monte Carlo Tree Search for automatic prompt optimization. Among these, methods that operate in the discrete prompt space ( e.g., RPP, PromptAgent) still search over natural-language templates, meaning their outputs remain subject to the same wording sensitivity they aim to resolve. In contrast, continuous prompt learning methods (e.g., POD, LASER, PPR) eliminate template dependence by replacing discrete prompts with learnable vectors, representing a more principled direction. However, directly applying continuous prompt tuning to recommendation still exhibits two notable limitations: (1) Lack of user-level adaptive instruction prompts : existing continuous prompt methods optimize task-level prompt representations shared across all users without providing user-specific adaptive instruction signals. While LASER injects collaborative signals via virtual tokens and PPR [29] introduces user-conditioned prefix generation, their personalization relies on shallow user profile features rather than leveraging the LLM’s deep semantic understanding of behavioral patterns, limiting their ability to capture nuanced preference dynamics; and (2) No control over semantic drift : continuous prompt vectors can be pushed by gradients into regions that the LLM cannot interpret meaningfully, yet none of these methods provide explicit mechanisms to keep learned prompts within the LLM’s effective semantic space. As a result, existing continuous prompt learning approaches fall short of achieving both stable and personalized prompt optimization for LLM-driven SR. To address these issues, we propose Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-driven sequential Rec ommendation (LRPRec). Our key insight is that while replacing discrete templates with learnable continuous prompts eliminates prompt sensitivity, naively applying standard prompt tuning to recommendation is insufficient due to two fundamental challenges unique to this setting. Critically, these two challenges are not independent, they interact in a way that makes addressing them jointly non-trivial: injecting user-specific signals into prompts inherently increases the risk of semantic drift, as personalization pushes each user’s prompt in a different direction away from the initialization; conversely, overly strict drift constraints can suppress the expressiveness needed for effective personalization. Our framework is designed to navigate this tension. 

First, recommendation is inherently a personalization task. Standard prompt tuning learns a single task-level instruction shared across all instances, which is reasonable for generic NLP tasks where the objective is uniform ( e.g., “translate this sentence”). However, in sequential recom-mendation, different users exhibit vastly different preference patterns. For example, a user with concentrated interests requires different reasoning guidance than one with diverse tastes. A fixed, instance-invariant prompt cannot provide optimal inference context for all users simultaneously. To address this, we introduce a personalized prompt injection mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding and directly adds it to the shared instruction prompts, enabling user-level adaptation while maintaining parameter efficiency. 4 Xiaolin Zheng et al. 

Second, continuous prompts risk drifting away from the LLM’s semantic space during optimization. Unlike discrete tokens that are inherently within the LLM’s vocabulary [32], contin-uous vectors can be pushed by gradients into regions that the LLM cannot interpret meaningfully. Our experiments confirm this: random initialization leads to severe degradation, demonstrating that prompts must reside in semantically valid regions. While template-based initialization pro-vides a good starting point, it offers no guarantee against drift during training, especially when personalized signals are injected, which amplifies the optimization dynamics. To mitigate this, we propose a semantic drift constraint that regularizes the shared instruction prompts to remain within a controlled neighborhood of their initialization anchors, ensuring that optimization improves upon the template without departing from the LLM’s effective semantic space. By applying the drift constraint specifically to the shared prompt component while allowing the personalized injection to operate additively, our design decouples the stability and personalization objectives, enabling both to be optimized without mutual interference. This yields a robust and effective prompting framework that can be directly deployed by simply providing interaction histories and candidate pools. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We identify and empirically characterize the prompt sensitivity problem in LLM-driven sequential recommendation, showing that semantically equivalent prompts can cause substantial perfor-mance variance. We further analyze why standard continuous prompt tuning is insufficient for this setting, revealing two interacting challenges, lack of personalization and semantic drift that must be addressed jointly rather than in isolation. 

• We propose a personalized prompt injection mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding and additively injects it into the shared learnable prompts, enabling user-level adaptation for the recommendation task while maintaining parameter efficiency. 

• We introduce a semantic drift constraint that regularizes the shared learnable prompts to re-main within a trust region around their initialization anchors. By decoupling this constraint from the personalized component, our design prevents semantic drift without suppressing the expressiveness of user-specific adaptation. 

• Extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets demonstrate that LRPRec consistently improves recommendation performance and robustness over strong baselines, while eliminating the need for manual tuning of background and task inference templates. 

2 Related Work 

Sequential Recommendation. Sequential recommendation has become a prominent research topic due to its effectiveness in modeling the dynamic evolution of user interests over time [1, 33, 34]. Early studies typically relied on the Markov Chain assumption to capture sequential dependencies among user interactions [8]. With the advancement of deep learning, a variety of neural architectures, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) [35], Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [36], and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) [37], have been introduced to model complex user–item interaction patterns. To alleviate inherent issues such as data sparsity and cold-start scenarios, content-aware sequential recommendation methods [12] incorporate auxiliary side information, including knowledge graphs [38] and textual descriptions [2, 39], leading to substantial performance improvements. More recently, multimodal learning paradigms have further advanced representation learning in SR [20, 40, 41], enabling the capture of richer sequential behaviors across heterogeneous modalities [13, 42]. Nevertheless, despite these improvements, conventional models remain limited in their ability to perform sophisticated semantic reasoning when compared with 

LLM-based approaches [15, 43]. Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-Driven Sequential Recommendation 5

LLMs for Sequential Recommendation. Owing to their strong representation learning and text generation capabilities [44], Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently emerged as a promising direction for boosting sequential recommendation performance [43, 45]. Existing LLM-based recom-mendation approaches can generally be categorized into two paradigms [15, 17, 45]: LLM-powered 

and LLM-driven frameworks. The LLM-powered paradigm [19, 46, 47] integrates LLMs into tradi-tional recommender systems to enhance contextual understanding or semantic representations [14]. For instance, NoteLLM [19] utilizes LLMs to generate compact note-level embeddings for recommendation refinement, while SAID [48] leverages LLMs to construct semantically consis-tent item representations. However, such methods fail to fully exploit the generative strengths of LLMs and often incur notable information loss when transforming LLM outputs into fixed embeddings for downstream tasks [15, 45]. In contrast, the LLM-driven paradigm [17, 22, 43, 49] directly formulates recommendation tasks as prompt-based generation problems, allowing LLMs to produce recommendation results end-to-end. Representative methods include TALLRec [26], which reframes sequential recommendation as a binary prediction task conditioned on user histories, and LLaRA [22], which combines textual prompts with collaborative embeddings projected from recommenders. 

Prompt Learning. Recent studies have explored prompt learning [50, 51] as parameter-efficient paradigms to adapt pre-trained models for recommendation [52, 53], especially under data sparsity and cold-start settings. A foundational line of work reformulates recommendation as language processing with personalized prompt templates [54, 55]. Early continuous prompt tuning methods introduce learnable soft prompts to guide pre-trained encoders without full fine-tuning [56, 57]. POD [27] distills discrete prompts into continuous vectors to bridge user/item IDs and natural language, while LASER [28] proposes Bi-Tuning that inserts trainable virtual tokens at both the prefix and suffix of a frozen LLM to inject collaborative signals and enable controllable generation. Multi-stage approaches such as SCRec [58] separately learn semantic and collaborative prompts before joint prediction [59]. Beyond data-driven prompts, knowledge-enhanced methods such as KPT [60] transform structured knowledge graphs into prompt representations to improve generalization [61]. To address the inherent lack of collaborative filtering capability in LLMs, A-LLMRec [62] injects pre-trained CF embeddings as prompt inputs, and Recommendation Critic [63] trains a separate collaborative model whose outputs serve as soft prompts for the LLM. More recently, prompt learning has shifted toward user-level personalization: PPR [29] generates personalized prefix prompts from user profiles, PeaPOD [64] distills individual preferences into soft prompts, CollaPPA [65] combines collaborative fine-tuning with personalized preference alignment prompts, and LLM-Rec [66] designs multiple prompting strategies for input text enrichment. Automatic prompt optimization methods, including RecPrompt [67] with self-tuning prompt engineering for news recommendation, RPP [30] with multi-agent reinforcement learning for instance-wise prompt assembly, Chat-Rec [68] with multi-turn dialogue prompts, and PromptAgent [31] with Monte Carlo Tree Search, further enable the discovery of expert-level prompts beyond manual design [69]. Unlike existing continuous prompt learning methods that learn task-level prompts without user-specific adaptive instruction signals and lack mechanisms to prevent semantic drift ( e.g., POD, LASER), LRPRec uniquely combines personalized prompt injection with a semantic drift constraint. A key design consideration is that personalization and stability are inherently in tension: injecting user-specific signals increases drift risk, while strict drift control can suppress personalization. LRPRec resolves this by applying the drift constraint to the shared prompt component while allowing personalization to operate additively, enabling both objectives to be achieved without mutual interference. 6 Xiaolin Zheng et al.

3.2 An Overview of LRPRec 

LRPRec is a prompt-learning framework tailored for the LLM-driven sequential recommendation paradigm. It targets a key practical limitation of prior LLM-driven methods: recommendation performance can fluctuate noticeably under semantically equivalent natural-language prompts, which makes deployment brittle and incurs substantial manual prompt engineering overhead. Given a user interaction sequence S𝑢 and a candidate pool C𝑢 , LRPRec first constructs the instance-specific evidence via lightweight serialization (Eq. (4)). Instead of using discrete background and task templates, LRPRec parameterizes the two instruction components as continuous learnable prompt vectors, i.e., Pbg and Ptask (Eq. (5)), which are shared across all users and instances. To adapt the shared prompts to individual users’ preference patterns, we introduce a personalized prompt injection mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding and directly adds it to the instruction prompts (Section 3.5). Furthermore, to prevent the learnable prompts from drifting away from the LLM’s semantic space during optimization, we incorporate a semantic drift constraint as a regularization term (Section 3.6). The LLM input is formed by concatenating the personalized instruction prompts with the evidence (Eq. (10)), and the LLM performs standard autoregressive generation to output the next-item prediction. To make training efficient and practical, we first adapt the backbone LLM to the recommendation domain via parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and then freeze the adapted LLM weights entirely, updating only the learnable prompt vectors and the projection parameters (Section 3.7). After training, the learned prompts and projection parameters are fixed and reused for all test instances, so inference only requires the raw interaction sequence and candidate set as inputs (Section 3.8), eliminating the need for manual tuning of background and task inference templates. Overall, LRPRec provides a principled replacement for discrete prompt templates in LLM-driven recommenders: it learns a robust instruction context via continuous prompting, injects user-specific Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-Driven Sequential Recommendation 7

3.5 Personalized Prompt Injection 

The learnable prompts Pbg and Ptask are shared across all users, providing a unified instruction context. However, this instance-invariant design overlooks a fundamental characteristic of rec-ommendation: personalization . In generic NLP tasks such as sentiment classification or machine translation, the task instruction is inherently uniform ( e.g., “classify the sentiment of this review” applies identically to all inputs). In contrast, sequential recommendation requires the LLM to infer user-specific preferences from highly diverse behavioral patterns. Users with concentrated interests (e.g., exclusively watching horror movies) benefit from reasoning that emphasizes genre consistency, while users with broad tastes benefit from reasoning that captures recent interest transitions. A single fixed instruction cannot optimally guide the LLM’s inference for all these diverse scenarios. To reconcile parameter efficiency with personalization, we propose personalized prompt injection :the shared prompts serve as a repository of global recommendation knowledge, while a user-specific preference embedding is directly added to adapt them to each user’s behavioral characteristics. 

User Preference Summarization. Rather than relying on shallow statistics ( e.g., mean-pooling token embeddings) that cannot capture high-level behavioral patterns, we leverage the frozen LLM itself to produce a semantically rich user preference representation. 3.6 Semantic Drift Constraint 

In Section 3.4, we adopt template-based initialization to provide a semantically meaningful starting point for the learnable prompts. The necessity of this choice is empirically confirmed by the failure of random initialization (Section 4.3), which demonstrates that prompts must reside within the LLM’s effective semantic space to produce meaningful outputs. However, template-based initialization only guarantees a valid starting point and provides no safeguard against the prompts gradually drifting into semantically invalid regions during gradient-based optimization. As training progresses, the continuous prompt vectors may be pushed away from the manifold of meaningful token embeddings, causing the LLM to receive contexts it was never trained to interpret, ultimately degrading generation quality. Discussion: Why Constrain Only Shared Prompts. One may note that the final input to the LLM is the personalized prompt ˜ p𝑖 = p𝑖 + e𝑢 (Eqs. (7)); since it originates from the LLM’s internal semantic space, it is inherently interpretable to the model and does not face the same out-of-distribution risk as freely optimized continuous vectors. different failure mode: it prevents the shared learnable parameters Pbg , Ptask from being pushed by accumulated gradients into regions that no longer correspond to meaningful language concepts. In contrast, e𝑢 is not a learnable prompt vector but a data-dependent signal computed fresh for each user instance; it adapts the shared prompts to individual preferences, which is precisely the intended personalization behavior. Constraining e𝑢 would suppress personalization and defeat the purpose of the injection mechanism.

Decoupled Two-Stage Training. A prerequisite for effective prompt optimization is that the backbone LLM already possesses basic recommendation capabilities. First, it allows the prompt optimization to focus exclusively on learning optimal instruction representations, since the backbone already understands the recommendation task. 4 Experiments and Analysis 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate on three datasets: 

• MovieLens [71] is a widely adopted benchmark for movie recommendation, which provides explicit user ratings together with corresponding movie titles. 

• Steam [72] contains user-generated reviews of video games from the Steam Store, along with the associated game titles. 

• LastFM [73], sourced from the Last.fm music service, records user–artist listening interactions as well as artist names. Since fine-tuning large language models typically incurs substantially higher computational costs compared to training conventional recommendation models, we preprocess and sample datasets to keep the scale tractable. Specifically, we retain the MovieLens dataset at its original scale, and for the Steam dataset, we uniformly sample one-third of the users and one-third of the games, preserving the interactions among them to construct a dataset of moderate size. Across all datasets, interaction sequences are ordered by timestamp and split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1. This temporal splitting strategy ensures that future interactions are excluded from the training phase, effectively preventing information leakage [74]. After preprocessing, MovieLens contains 1,478 items, 1,063 user sequences, and 112,384 interactions; Steam contains 3,987 items, 13,426 user sequences, and 241,653 interactions; LastFM contains 5,142 items, 1,387 user sequences, and 83,917 interactions. 

Baseline Algorithms. We compare LRPRec with the following representative baselines spanning four categories. 

• Traditional Sequential Recommenders. We include several representative sequential rec-ommendation models that differ in their underlying architectures. GRU4Rec [75] models user Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-Driven Sequential Recommendation 13 

behavior sequences using gated recurrent units, capturing sequential dependencies through recurrent state transitions. Caser [36] leverages convolutional neural networks to extract local and global patterns from interaction sequences by applying horizontal and vertical convolu-tional filters. SASRec [72] adopts a self-attention mechanism to model long-range dependencies among items, and has become a strong and widely used attention-based baseline for sequential recommendation. HGN [76] introduces a hierarchical gating network that captures both user-level and item-level sequential patterns through feature gating and instance gating mechanisms. 

BERT4Rec [77] employs a bidirectional transformer with masked item prediction to model users’ interaction preferences, capturing left-to-right and right-to-left sequential dependencies. 

• Proprietary LLM APIs. We evaluate several state-of-the-art proprietary large language models accessed via API in a zero-shot setting. (1) GPT-5.4 [78] is a contemporary proprietary LLM from OpenAI, demonstrating strong reasoning and instruction-following capabilities across diverse tasks. (2) Claude-Opus-4.6 is Anthropic’s most capable model, excelling at complex reasoning, nuanced instruction following, and long-context understanding. (3) Gemini-3.1-Flash [79] is Google’s efficient multimodal model optimized for low-latency inference while maintaining strong generation quality. (4) DeepSeek-V4-Flash [80] is a highly efficient open-weight model from DeepSeek-AI with million-token context support and competitive reasoning performance. 

• LLM-driven Sequential Recommenders. We further compare our approach with LLM-based recommenders that are fine-tuned or adapted for recommendation tasks. (1) Llama2-7B [81] is a widely used open-source large language model released by Meta, which has demonstrated strong capabilities in instruction following and text generation. (2) Llama3.1-8B-Instruct [82] is an instruction-tuned variant of the Llama 3.1 family, offering improved instruction following and reasoning capabilities over its predecessor. (3) MoRec [83] augments conventional recommenda-tion models by incorporating item-side modality information, such as textual features, through a dedicated text encoder. Following the official implementation, we instantiate MoRec with BERT as the text encoder and SASRec as the recommendation backbone. (4) TALLRec [26] applies instruction tuning to large language models using recommendation-oriented corpora, enabling LLMs to perform recommendation tasks via natural language prompts. Since TALLRec formulates recommendation as a binary decision problem ( i.e., predicting YES or NO for a target item), we adapt it to our setting by reformulating the task as selecting the next item from a given candidate set. (5) LLaRA [22] is a Large Language-Recommendation Assistant framework for sequential recommendation that integrates a conventional sequential recommender with a large language model to perform next-item prediction. Following the official SASRec-based implementation, we instantiate LLaRA with SASRec as the sequential recommendation backbone, and represent each item using a hybrid prompt that concatenates item text tokens with a behavioral token projected from the SASRec item embedding via an adapter. (6) MLLM-MSR [20] harnesses multimodal large language models for multimodal sequential recommendation by encoding visual and textual item features jointly. 

• Prompt Learning Enhanced Methods. We compare with prompt-based approaches that optimize soft or discrete prompts for LLM-based recommendation. (1) POD [27] distills the recommendation knowledge of large LLMs into compact continuous prompt representations for efficient inference. (2) PPR [29] generates personalized soft prefix prompts conditioned on user profiles to adapt a frozen LLM for sequential recommendation. (3) LASER [28] introduces a parameter-efficient bi-tuning strategy that inserts learnable virtual tokens at both the input and output ends of an LLM to inject collaborative signals. (4) PromptAgent [31] formulates prompt optimization as a strategic planning problem and employs Monte Carlo tree search to automatically discover expert-level prompts. (5) RPP [30] leverages reinforcement learning to 14 Xiaolin Zheng et al. 

personalize discrete prompt components on a per-user basis, enabling instance-wise prompt selection for recommendation. 

Evaluation Metrics. Following LLaRA [22] and MLLM-MSR [20], we adopt a candidate-based eval-uation protocol with a candidate set size of 20 (1 ground-truth item + 19 uniformly sampled negatives that the user has not previously interacted with). Because (M)LLM-based methods embed item de-scriptions ( e.g., titles) directly into the prompt, an excessively large candidate pool would exceed the context window or cause out-of-memory issues during training and inference. For fair comparison, LRPRec and all baselines that incorporate a candidate pool in the prompt use the same pool size of 20. Each method is required to identify the correct next item from this pool, and we assess performance using the HitRatio@1 (HR@1) metric, which is computed only over valid predictions ( i.e., outputs that correspond to items in the candidate pool): HR@1 = # correct predictions /# valid outputs. When properly prompted, LLM-based recommendation models are able to directly output a single predicted item. In contrast, for conventional recommendation approaches, we treat the item with the highest predicted score as the final recommendation. Notably, as LRPRec adopts a generative prediction framework, it may occasionally produce invalid outputs, such as meaningless tokens or items that do not belong to the predefined candidate pool. To account for this issue, we further report an auxiliary metric, termed ValidRatio, which measures the fraction of predictions that correspond to valid candidate items across all sequences, thereby reflecting the models’ ability to follow task instructions. Inspired by [4], some models may achieve a high HitRatio@1 , yet fail to generate valid or meaningful recommendations. To mitigate this issue, we introduce a new evaluation metric, Valid HitRatio@1 (VHR@1), defined as 𝑉 𝐻𝑅 @1 = 𝑉 𝑎𝑙𝑖𝑑𝑅𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑜 × 𝐻𝑖𝑡𝑅𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑜 @1. This composite metric penalizes models that achieve high accuracy only among valid outputs while producing a large fraction of invalid predictions, thereby preventing inflated performance estimates from models that “gamble” on a subset of valid generations. By jointly accounting for both validity and accuracy in a single score, VHR@1 provides a more faithful reflection of real-world deployment quality than reporting the two metrics independently.

4.3 Ablation Study 

To validate the contribution of each component in LRPRec, we design six ablation variants grouped into three categories: (1) core module ablation, (2) initialization and prompt strategy, and (3) personalization design alternatives. Results are reported in Table 2. 

4.3.1 w/o Personalization . This variant removes the personalized prompt injection mechanism entirely. The shared prompts Pbg and Ptask are used directly without adding the user preference embedding e𝑢 , meaning all users receive identical instruction prompts. As shown in Table 2, removing personalization leads to consistent and notable performance drops across all three datasets. Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-Driven Sequential Recommendation 17 

This confirms that a single fixed instruction context cannot optimally guide the LLM’s inference for users with diverse behavioral patterns. The personalized injection mechanism enables the shared prompts to produce user-adaptive reasoning guidance, which is essential for recommendation quality. Notably, the ValidRatio remains high in this variant, indicating that the learnable prompts still maintain valid generation behavior. The performance loss is purely in recommendation accuracy, underscoring that personalization contributes to precision rather than validity .

4.3.2 w/o Drift Constraint . This variant removes the semantic drift constraint by setting 𝛼 = 0in Eq. (14), allowing the learnable prompts to optimize freely without any regularization toward their initialization anchors. As shown in Table 2, we observe two effects: (1) ValidRatio drops noticeably, indicating that unconstrained optimization pushes prompts into regions where the LLM produces more invalid outputs; (2) despite maintaining reasonable HitRatio@1, the overall VHR@1 degrades due to the compounding effect of lower validity. These results validate our hypothesis that gradient-based optimization can gradually drift the prompt vectors away from the LLM’s effective semantic space. The hinge-style constraint provides a necessary spatial safeguard: it allows the prompts to improve upon the discrete template within a trust region while preventing them from entering semantically invalid regions that the LLM was never trained to interpret. 

4.3.3 w/ Random Initialization . This variant replaces the template-based initialization with random initialization, i.e., both Pbg and Ptask are initialized from a random distribution. As shown in Table 2, this leads to severe degradation: VHR@1 drops to below 6% on all datasets, while ValidRatio also substantially decreases. This demonstrates that the LLM’s generation behavior is extremely sensitive to the semantic validity of its input context. Random vectors lie far outside the manifold of meaningful token embeddings, causing the LLM to produce incoherent outputs regardless of subsequent optimization. This result provides strong empirical evidence for our design choice of template-based initialization and further motivates the semantic drift constraint: if the starting point matters this much, preventing drift away from it during training is equally critical. 

4.3.4 w/ Discrete Prompt . This variant discards learnable prompting entirely and instead uses the best-performing handcrafted discrete prompt template identified through manual prompt engineering on each dataset. As shown in Table 2, while discrete prompts achieve reasonable per-formance, they consistently underperform LRPRec across all datasets. Moreover, discrete prompts exhibit high variance across different template formulations (as visualized in Figure 3): semantically equivalent templates with minor wording differences can lead to notable performance fluctuations. In contrast, LRPRec learns a single robust instruction context that automatically adapts to the backbone LLM and data distribution, eliminating the need for manual trial-and-error and providing stable performance.

4.4 Generalization Analysis 

To evaluate whether LRPRec can alleviate the brittleness caused by discrete prompt formulations, we conduct a prompt-level generalization study. Specifically, we prepare four handcrafted prompt templates, denoted as Prompt_A –Prompt_D , which are semantically equivalent but differ in wording, syntactic structure, and length. All templates contain the same essential components (a background role description, the interaction sequence, the candidate pool, and an explicit selection instruction) but present them with different phrasing and organization. We design these templates to vary along three dimensions: verbosity (concise vs. detailed), formality (conversational vs. technical), Learning Robust Personalized Prompts for LLM-Driven Sequential Recommendation 19 

and structural organization (flat narrative vs. explicitly labeled sections), thereby covering a repre-sentative range of prompt styles that practitioners might employ in practice. The four prompts used in this study are as follows: 

Prompt_A: You are a recommendation system. Here are the interactions of the user: <Interaction>. Here is the candidate pool: <Candidate>. Please choose exactly one item from the candidate pool as the next item the user will interact with. Only output the chosen item. 

Prompt_B: I will give you a user’s interaction history and a set of candidate items. User’s interaction history: <Interaction>. Candidate items: <Candidate>. Based on the user’s history, select the most likely next item from the candidate items. Return only the item name. 

Prompt_C: The user has previously interacted with the following items: <Interaction>. Now you are given a list of candidate items: <Candidate>. Task: predict which candidate item the user will interact with next. Answer with one item from the candidate list. 

Prompt_D: You are an expert sequential recommender. Input: [Interaction Sequence] <Interaction> [Candidate Pool] <Candidate>. Instruction: Pick the single best next-item prediction from the candidate pool. Your output must be exactly one candidate item, without explanation or additional text. 

For each dataset, we use each of the four templates as the initialization source for LRPRec’s learnable prompts. Concretely, the token embeddings of a given template are extracted from the LLM’s embedding layer and used to initialize the learnable vectors Pbg and Ptask (Eq. (5)). Since the four templates differ in length, the resulting prompt lengths 𝑀 and 𝑁 naturally vary across initialization templates. This yields four independently trained LRPRec instances per dataset, each starting from a different semantic anchor. The goal is to examine whether the final learned prompts converge to similar performance regardless of the initialization template, thereby validating that LRPRec is robust to the choice of handcrafted template used for initialization.

Beyond evaluating models with their matched training templates, we further investigate a more challenging scenario: what happens when a model trained with one discrete template is evaluated using a different template at inference time? LRPRec eliminates this fragility entirely by replacing template-dependent inference with learned, template-agnostic continuous instructions.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we proposed LRPRec, a learnable prompting framework for LLM-driven sequential recommendation that addresses the brittleness caused by discrete natural-language prompt for-mulations. Our work is motivated by the observation that existing LLM-driven recommenders are highly sensitive to prompt wording, and that naively applying standard prompt tuning to recommendation faces two fundamental challenges: lack of personalization and semantic drift risk. To overcome these challenges, LRPRec decomposes the prompt into instance-invariant instruction components and instance-dependent evidence, replaces the brittle discrete instruction templates with continuous learnable vectors initialized from handcrafted templates, and introduces two complementary mechanisms. First, we propose a personalized prompt injection mechanism that encodes user behavioral signals into a preference embedding via the frozen LLM and directly adds it to the shared instruction prompts, enabling user-level adaptation while maintaining parameter efficiency. Second, we introduce a semantic drift constraint that regularizes the shared learned 24 Xiaolin Zheng et al. 

prompts to remain within a trust region around their initialization anchors, preventing gradient-based optimization from pushing prompts into regions that the LLM cannot interpret meaningfully. By applying the drift constraint to the shared component while allowing personalization to operate additively, these two mechanisms are decoupled so that stability and expressiveness can be optimized without mutual interference. Extensive experiments on three datasets demonstrate that LRPRec consistently outperforms strong baselines from multiple categories, including ID-based sequential recommenders, multimodal methods, LLM-driven methods, and prompt learning enhanced methods, while maintaining perfect output validity across all datasets. Ablation studies confirm the necessity of each component, and generalization analysis shows that LRPRec remains robust regardless of which handcrafted template is used for initialization, effectively eliminating the need for manual tuning of background and task inference templates. The semantic drift visualization further provides direct empirical evidence for the drift phenomenon and validates the effectiveness of our trust-region constraint. Future work could explore extending learnable prompting to more realistic settings with dynamic candidate generation, larger candidate pools, cross-domain transfer, and online feedback to further improve adaptivity and scalability.
