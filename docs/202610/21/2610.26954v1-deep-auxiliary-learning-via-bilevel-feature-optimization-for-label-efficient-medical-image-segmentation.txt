# Deep Auxiliary Learning via Bilevel Feature Optimization for Label-Efficient Medical Image Segmentation 

Fuping Wu, Xi Jia, Le Zhang, Jiahua Li, Rima Mustafa, Thomas E. Nichols, Bartłomiej W. Papie˙ z

Abstract — Auxiliary learning (AL), which can improve the performance of a primary task by leveraging related auxiliary tasks, is commonly regarded as a special case of multi-task learning. Existing AL approaches typically require manually annotated auxiliary labels, increasing an-notation costs, particularly in medical imaging where ex-pert labeling is expensive. In this work, we investigate whether segmentation performance can be improved us-ing auxiliary tasks whose labels are automatically derived from existing segmentation annotations, requiring no ad-ditional manual labeling. To address this, we propose a novel auxiliary learning framework, formulated as a bilevel optimization problem. Our method enables effective knowl-edge transfer between tasks via a feature communication module, enhancing the primary segmentation task. We evaluate the method on three medical image segmenta-tion benchmarks under limited-label settings. The results demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art methods, achieving superior label efficiency. The code is publicly available at https://github.com/FupingWu90/ ALBO .

Index Terms — Segmentation, Auxiliary Learning, Label Efficiency, Bilevel Optimization 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# DEEP learning has achieved remarkable success in med-ical image segmentation. However, its performance is highly dependent on large-scale annotated datasets, the cre-ation of which is time-consuming and requires domain ex-pertise [1], [2]. This limitation has prompted research into label-efficient learning strategies. Auxiliary learning (AL), a specialized form of multi-task learning (MTL), has shown strong potential to improve the performance of a primary task under limited supervision by exploiting related auxiliary tasks, and therefore is well-suited to weakly supervised settings such as semi-supervised learning [3]. Unlike MTL, which treats all tasks equally, AL prior-itizes a designated main task through auxiliary supervision.

Despite this encouraging progress, especially on natural image benchmarks [4], two key challenges remain unresolved for segmentation. The first is the design of label-efficient auxiliary tasks. Existing methods typically rely on either (i) additional an-notated auxiliary tasks such as depth estimation [5], or (ii) self-supervised objectives that benefit from large corpora of unlabeled images [6], [7]. Although effective, these strate-gies often require additional annotation, auxiliary modalities, or extensive unlabeled datasets, which may be difficult to obtain in medical imaging because of annotation cost and privacy constraints [8]. In this work, we address a funda-mental question: Through AL, can models improve the label efficiency of the target (main) task using auxiliary tasks, whose labels are derived solely from the existing data? And if so, how? Previous studies have employed handcrafted or algorithmically generated labels, such as object boundaries, to regularize segmentation outputs [9]–[11]. However, these approaches are evaluated only when abundant labeled images are available, and their robustness under limited-label settings is not discussed. Notably, our setting assumes only the existing annotations of the main task: the auxiliary labels are generated from these annotations, and the training set is not expanded with additional unlabeled images. This is the key differ-ence from semi-supervised learning (SSL), which improves label efficiency by exploiting a pool of unlabeled data; the two strategies are therefore complementary, as discussed in Sec. IV-F.3. The second challenge is to optimize the AL models such that the auxiliary task consistently benefits, rather than in-terferes with, the primary segmentation task. Simultaneously learning multiple tasks can result in negative transfer , where the performance of the main task deteriorates [12]. Existing approaches primarily attempt to mitigate this either by dy-namically adjusting task importance during training [13], or modifying gradients to reduce conflict between tasks [14], [15]. However, the underlying causes of negative transfer remain underexplored, particularly in medical imaging set-tings where tasks are often highly imbalanced in difficulty and representation [16]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, without an appropriate optimization technique, the features learned for boundary detection by methods such as Joint and Independent can be inconsistent with this auxiliary task. The intuition is that models can benefit from learning auxiliary tasks that capture complemen-tary structural cues (e.g., boundaries or foreground masks) to improve feature representations for the main segmentation task. Building on this idea, we propose ALBO: auxiliary 

learning via bilevel optimization. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:  

> •

We propose ALBO, an auxiliary learning framework for medical image segmentation that exploits auxiliary tasks whose labels are automatically derived from existing segmentation annotations. A bidirectional communication module enables feature exchange between the primary and auxiliary branches.  

> •

We formulate AL as a bilevel optimization problem and employ a finite-difference approximation to make the optimization tractable during training while enhancing representation learning.  

> •

The proposed method is evaluated on two 2D and one 3D benchmark datasets and three types of backbones (convolutional U-Net, ViT-based DINOv3, and Mamba architecture), demonstrating superior label efficiency. 

II. R ELATED WORKS 

A. Auxiliary learning (AL) 

AL enhances the performance of a main task by leveraging knowledge from related auxiliary tasks [17]. Prior AL research has primarily focused on two fronts: auxiliary task selection and optimization strategies. Several works have investigated how to identify effective auxiliary tasks by modeling task relationships, in which a common strategy is to group tasks based on their positive transfer potential [18], [19]. Another line of research focuses on improving the optimization of AL models. In GCS [20], gradient cosine similarity is employed to assess task-relatedness and to determine whether an auxiliary loss is beneficial for the main task. In [21], gradient similarity is used to guide the adaptive selection of auxiliary tasks in reinforcement learning. In [16], task weights are automatically adjusted by minimizing validation error. In AuxiLearn [22], nonlinear combinations of losses are learned dynamically to improve optimization. In AuxiNash [4], the AL problem is formulated as a generalized bargaining game to resolve task interactions. Unlike prior work, we propose a framework applicable to multiple auxiliary tasks, rather than methods tailored to a specific task. For example, Fan et al. [23] design a task-specific scheme using auxiliary image registration for one-shot segmentation, which is less flexible in terms of auxiliary task selection. Sobotka et al. [24] require paired multimodal data as auxiliary labels, which falls outside our annotation-free setting. Huang et al. [25] do not study auxiliary learning; instead, they introduce an auxiliary model for expert correction. Registration-based methods have also been explored for label-efficient segmentation, including atlas-based unsuper-vised segmentation via contrastive registration that warps a single reference mask (CLMorph [26]) and few-shot segmen-tation via self-supervised reconstruction (EFS-MedSeg [27]); unlike these, ALBO neither relies on registration nor on unlabeled data, deriving its auxiliary labels for free from the existing main-task labels. The optimization strategies above steer the auxiliary influ-ence only through the loss or gradient of a shared encoder, by gating the auxiliary gradient with its similarity to the main gradient [20], [21], reweighting tasks by validation error [16], learning a nonlinear loss combination [22], or bargaining over task weights [4]. They therefore neither model an explicit bidirectional exchange of features between the main and auxiliary branches, nor structurally guarantee that the main task is prioritized during optimization. ALBO addresses both limitations: the bidirectional communication module lets the main and auxiliary branches exchange features in both directions (Sec. III-A), and the bilevel formulation casts the main task as the upper level so the auxiliary task is optimized only insofar as it serves the main one (Sec. III-B), using annotation-free auxiliary labels rather than task-specific extra data [23], [24].

Soft parameter-sharing methods design separate encoders for each task while enforcing constraints on their associated parameters. For example, MTAN [35] employs an attention mechanism among task-specific networks to facilitate the sharing of a general feature pool. Hard parameter-sharing methods, in contrast, primarily focus on finding a local update vector to reduce task conflict. Liu et al. introduce an approach to compute an update vector with equal projections on each task gradient [36]. PCGrad [37] projects each task’s gradient onto the normal plane of other task gradients, combining them to form the final update vector. GradDrop [38] randomly drops task gradients based on the extent of their conflicts. CAGrad [14] minimizes the average loss function and is theoretically proven to converge to the optimal point. Additionally, mixture of experts (MoE) architectures has also been widely adopted in MTL with an example of modeling the global task rela-tionships by adding a generic convolution path to the original MoE structure [39]. Beyond architectural design, a large body of MTL work targets the optimization of shared parameters, which can be grouped into loss-balancing schemes that reweight per-task losses, such as random loss weighting (RLW) [40] and AMTL [41], and gradient-manipulation schemes that resolve conflicting task gradients, such as PCGrad [37], Grad-Drop [38], CAGrad [14], and Nash-MTL [42]. However, these methods are designed for the symmetric MTL objective of improving all tasks jointly; they treat every task equally and do not privilege a designated main task, so a freely derived auxiliary signal can dilute rather than strengthen the primary segmentation. In contrast, ALBO adopts an asymmetric bilevel formulation that places the main segmentation task at the upper level and updates the auxiliary branch only to benefit it, which is better matched to the label-efficient auxiliary-learning setting studied here. III. M ETHODS 

Because the auxiliary labels here are derived for free from the main-task data, our aim is not to solve the two tasks symmetrically but to make the auxiliary task serve the main one; we therefore take AL as a bilevel problem in which the main task occupies the upper level. Given an image x, its corresponding main task label ym, and an auxiliary label ya

automatically generated from (x, ym), AL aims to learn a prediction model f for the main task with the help of ya.Throughout the paper, we refer to ya as the auxiliary label 

and to the task of predicting it as the auxiliary task . Our model is composed of three functional modules: a shared encoder g(·; θ), a primary (main-task) head hm(·; ψm), and an auxiliary head ha(·; ψa), so that the main- and auxiliary-task predictors are f = hm ◦ g and fa = ha ◦ g, respectively. Because the encoder is built from the bidirectional communi-cation module (BCM) in Sec. III-A, it carries two interacting branches, and its parameters split into a main branch θm

and an auxiliary branch θa, i.e., θ = (θm, θa); the two heads carry parameters ψm and ψa. Grouped by the role they play in optimization, the main (upper-level) variables are 

{θm, θa, ψm} and the auxiliary (lower-level) variables are 

{θa, ψa}; the shared branch θa is the variable through which the two levels interact. As shown in Fig. 2(a), in this work, our main task is to segment anatomical structures. We employ the U-Net [43] as the encoder. To better exploit auxiliary supervision, we propose a novel auxiliary learning framework via bilevel 

optimization, ALBO, which aligns with our motivation from both network architecture and optimization perspectives, as detailed in the following sections.

IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Datasets and pre-processing 

We evaluated the proposed method with three segmentation tasks: (1) Optic cup/disc (OC/OD) segmentation using the REFUGE 2018 dataset [46]. REFUGE consists of 400 color fundus photography images for training, 400 for validation, and 400 for testing. All images were cropped to a size of 

512 × 512 . For REFUGE, each model was trained three times 

with different random initializations to ensure reliability. (2) Cardiac segmentation using the ACDC dataset [47], which contains 200 short-axis cardiac MR-cine images with the labels of the right ventricle (RV), myocardium (Myo), and left ventricle (LV). All slices were cropped to a size of 128 × 128 .For ACDC, each model was evaluated with five-fold cross-validation . For each fold, we used 40 cases for validation, and the remaining 160 cases for the training data selection. (3) The Hippocampus dataset from Medical Segmentation Decathlon Challenge [48] consists of 260 3D scans with annotations of the hippocampus head (HH) and body (HB) available. All volumes were resized to 48 × 64 × 48 with zero-padding. For the Hippocampus, we also employed five-fold cross-validation . For each fold, we used 52 scans for validation, and the remaining 208 scans for the training set selection. For evaluation, the average Dice and the average symmetric surface distance (ASSD) were reported. 

B. Implementation details 

We implemented nine representative methods for compar-ison: (1) Independent: this is the conventional independent learning for the main segmentation tasks without involving any auxiliary tasks; (2) Four AL methods, including Joint, GCS [20], AuxiLearn [22], and AuxiNash [4]. (3) Four MTL methods, including CAGrad [14], Nash [42], RLW [40], and AMTL [41]. Concretely, Independent trains only the main task, minimizing Lm(θm, θa, ψm) over {θm, θa, ψm} with no auxiliary loss, whereas Joint minimizes the equally-weighted sum Lm + La over all parameters {θm, θa, ψm, ψa} by conventional single-level gradient descent, i.e., without the bilevel look-ahead of ALBO. For comparison, all models were evaluated using the same U-Net-shaped network (as shown in Fig. 2(a)), and run on a single 40GB NVIDIA A100 GPU. We employed 3D models for the Hippocampus and 2D models for the others. For simplicity, we denote the U-Net with 128 and 1024 bottom channels as U-Net-S and U-Net-L, respectively. For all datasets, we trained ALBO using the SGD optimizer with the initial learning rate of α = β = 0 .1 and decay of 0.9 every 200 iterations. All models were trained for a maximum of 15 , 000 iterations with a batch size of 20.

D. Effect of auxiliary-task selection and combination 

We study how the choice and number of auxiliary tasks affect segmentation performance by evaluating four auxiliary tasks, namely boundary, distance, saliency, and Canny edge (Fig. 3), both individually and in combination. Table II reports results with 10 labeled samples from REFUGE and ACDC. First, the boundary task is the strongest and most robust single auxiliary task: it attained the best score on all six ACDC metrics and the best OC Dice on REFUGE, and no other single task was competitive across both datasets. The remaining single tasks were weaker and less consistent. The Canny edge was competitive on ACDC but markedly worse on REFUGE, while the distance and saliency tasks were the weakest overall. Second, combining auxiliary tasks was not systematically beneficial. On REFUGE, using all four tasks gave the best OD Dice and the lowest ASSD, marginally ahead of the boundary task; on ACDC, however, every combination was inferior to the single boundary task, and the four-task set fell well below it. These results indicate that the bilevel feature communication mechanism extracts most of the informative structure from a single, well-matched auxiliary task. Adding more tasks provides, at best, a marginal and dataset-dependent gain, and AUTHOR et al. : TITLE 7

may harm accuracy. Thus, increasing the number of auxiliary tasks is not intrinsically beneficial. This justifies our choice of a single, mask-derived boundary auxiliary task: it is the most robust option across both modalities, and avoids the task-balancing burden and negative-transfer risk of multi-auxiliary training. 

E. Label efficiency study 

We evaluated the label efficiency of ALBO by training the model with different numbers of samples and using boundary detection as the auxiliary task.  

> 1) Comparison on REFUGE.:

For OC and OD segmenta-tion, we randomly selected 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 retinal images for training. Since all compared methods achieved significantly better performance ( p < 0.01 ) with the BCM than with the CCM when training with the whole 400 labeled images, we used BCM-embedded U-Net-S for all methods to provide a fairer comparison of label efficiency. Table III presents the average performance on OC and OD of all methods, trained with either 400 or 10 labeled images. With sufficient training data, the performance of all methods was close. When the labeled data were reduced to 10 samples, ALBO demonstrated the best robustness, whereas all other AL and MTL methods performed worse than Independent. We additionally report the 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95) and, using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, mark in Table III the metrics that differ significantly from ALBO. With 400 labeled images, although the Dice scores of all methods were similar, ALBO attained the best HD95 on both OC and OD, indicating more accurate boundary delineation. With only 10 labeled images, ALBO yielded the best Dice and ASSD on both structures, and its Dice and ASSD improvements over the competing methods were statistically significant ( p < 0.01 ). Fig. 4 (a) illustrates the trend of Dice scores averaged on OC and OD obtained by all methods as the number of labeled training samples decreased. Overall, ALBO is more robust to changes in training data size. When the number of labeled training samples is N ≤ 80 , ALBO significantly outperforms the competing methods in average Dice ( p < 0.05 ). A similar trend can be observed for ASSD. The top two rows of Fig. 5 illustrate predictions from each method on a representative test sample, trained with 400 and 10 retinal images, respectively. These results show that ALBO achieves the best performance when the labeled training data are limited.  

> 2) Comparison on ACDC.:

For cardiac segmentation, be-sides using the full 160 scans in each fold, we also randomly selected 10, 20, 40, and 80 scans for training. In contrast to REFUGE, the models tended to underfit on ACDC, and CCM provided more favorable results than BCM. Accordingly, we used U-Net-L for all compared methods to ensure a fair comparison. Table IV presents the average performance on RV, Myo, and LV of all methods, trained with either 160 or 10 labeled scans. ALBO consistently obtained the best results across all metrics, particularly when the labeled training data was limited to 10. Table IV also reports the HD95 and the significance of each method relative to ALBO (using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test). ALBO obtained the best Dice and ASSD on every structure and the best or second-best HD95; with only 10 labeled scans it significantly outperformed all competing methods on all three metrics for every structure ( p < 0.01 ). Similarly, Fig. 4 (b) illustrates the trend of Dice of all methods averaged on all structures with varying numbers of labeled training data. As the number of labeled scans decreases, the performance gap between ALBO and the competing methods widens, demonstrating the superior label efficiency of ALBO. Specifically, ALBO significantly outperforms others in average Dice across all training sample sizes ( p < 0.05 ). A similar trend is observed for ASSD. The middle two rows of Fig. 5 illustrate representative predictions from each method, trained with 160 and 10 scans, respectively. ALBO produces more accurate predictions for the sample, especially when labeled data were limited.  

> 3) Comparison on Hippocampus.:

For hippocampus seg-mentation, we used the full set of 208 scans and randomly selected 20 scans for training, and employed U-Net-L for all compared methods. Table V presents the average results on the HH and HB. Most methods outperformed the In-dependent when only 20 labeled scans were available, and ALBO remained robust in this low-label setting. Table V additionally reports the HD95 and the per-metric significance relative to ALBO (using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Because this benchmark was close to saturation, all methods performed comparably and many pairwise differences were not statistically significant; ALBO nevertheless remained among the most competitive methods, achieving the best HD95 and the tied-best ASSD on the HH with 208 scans and the best HB Dice with 20 scans, while a few of the strongest auxiliary-learning methods such as AuxiNash and Nash were only marginally better on individual metrics. F. Discussion 

> 1) Can ALBO learn task-consistent feature representations?:

In Fig. 1, we visualize encoder feature maps from the main and auxiliary branches for a representative retinal image using Independent, Joint and ALBO. Compared with Independent and Joint, ALBO produces feature representations that are more aligned with the corresponding tasks: the main-branch features are more focused on the segmentation target, while the auxiliary-branch features more clearly capture boundary-related structure. This suggests that ALBO encourages more task-consistent feature learning through bidirectional commu-nication and bilevel optimization.

Relationship between ALBO and SSL. Both ALBO and SSL target the limited-annotation regime, but they rely on different resources. ALBO assumes only the existing annotations of the main task and generates its auxiliary labels from them without expanding the training set, whereas SSL improves label efficiency by additionally exploiting a pool of unlabeled images. This leads to three practical observations. (i) Comple-mentarity. Because the two strategies draw on different sources of information, they are complementary rather than competing. As shown in Table VII, combining ALBO with PL improved the average Dice of PL from 76 .59% to 80 .23% (boundary detection) and 81 .69% (distance-map prediction), whereas PL + Joint yielded only 75 .12% and 77 .98% , respectively. (ii) Exploiting unlabeled data. ALBO itself does not require unlabeled data, but it can exploit them when they are available: as formulated in Eq. (7), any SSL regularizer that can be computed on unlabeled images can be added to the main and auxiliary levels, while the bilevel structure that prioritizes the main task remains unchanged. (iii) Practical scenarios. 

ALBO is preferable when a sizeable and representative pool of unlabeled images is unavailable or cannot be used, for example, in small single-center cohorts or under data-sharing and privacy restrictions that limit the collection of additional images. Using only the 10 labeled ACDC scans, ALBO reached an average Dice of 79 .48% (Table IV), which is higher than four of the six SSL methods in Table VII that additionally used 150 unlabeled scans. When abundant unlabeled data from the same distribution are available, SSL is the natural choice, and ALBO can then serve as a stronger supervised component within it, as in PL + ALBO.

G. Computational and resource analysis 

We analyze the cost of the bilevel training scheme. Table IX reports the training and inference cost of ALBO and of a single-level baseline ( Independent ) on REFUGE with N = 10 

labeled images, measured on one NVIDIA RTX 6000 (24 GB) with batch size 20 .The additional computational cost arises from the bilevel optimization, which introduces one virtual inner update and a finite-difference approximation of the Hessian–vector prod-uct [45], requiring approximately two additional forward– backward passes per iteration. Consequently, the per-iteration training time increases by approximately 5.6×, and the peak training memory increases by 46%. This overhead is incurred only during training. At inference, ALBO performs a single forward pass through the same segmentation network as the baseline, resulting in identical model size, memory footprint, and real-time inference latency ( < 15 ms per image). Fur-thermore, ALBO converges in fewer iterations (2880 versus 5040) while achieving a lower training loss (Fig. 7), reducing the wall-clock time to the best model from the expected 5.6×

to 3.2×. Overall, training completes in approximately three hours on a single GPU with a peak memory footprint below 10 GB.

(iii) Performance on rare structures. Like other methods trained with very limited supervision, ALBO cannot span the full anatomical variability from ten labeled scans; it therefore has a ceiling on rare, atypical, or very small, low-contrast targets, such as the optic cup at N =10 .

APPENDIX II EFFECT OF THE BCM AND CCM M ODULES ACROSS 

NETWORK SCALES 

To support the discussion of the bidirectional communica-tion module (Sec. III-A) and the bilevel optimization (Sec. III-B), this appendix reports the full comparison between the BCM and CCM modules under two network scales, U-Net-S (128 bottom channels) and U-Net-L ( 1024 bottom channels), on the ACDC and REFUGE datasets. For ACDC, we used five-fold cross-validation with 160 labeled scans per fold. For REFUGE, each model was trained three times with different random initializations using 400 labeled images. Tables X–XI summarize the results. On ACDC, the models are prone to underfitting (Table X). With U-Net-L, CCM and BCM give comparable results for all methods. With the smaller U-Net-S, CCM is preferable to BCM for the nine compared methods; nevertheless, ALBO with BCM still attains the best average Dice ( 90 .22% ). On REFUGE, embedding the BCM consistently helps at the small scale: with U-Net-S, all nine compared methods improve significantly over CCM (paired t-test, p ≪ 0.01 ;Table XI, panels (a)–(b)), opposite to the trend on ACDC. When the network is enlarged to U-Net-L, all nine methods degrade markedly due to overfitting, under both CCM and BCM, whereas ALBO remains the most robust (panels (c)– (d)). These observations indicate that the benefit of the BCM is not automatic: it depends on the dataset and the network scale, and the bilevel optimization is what enables the BCM to be exploited robustly across settings.
