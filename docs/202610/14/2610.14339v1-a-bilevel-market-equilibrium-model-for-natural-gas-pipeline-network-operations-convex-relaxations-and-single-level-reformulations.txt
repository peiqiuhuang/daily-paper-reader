# A Bilevel Market Equilibrium Model for Natural Gas Pipeline Network Operations: Convex Relaxations and Single-level Reformulations 

Novel Kumar Dey 1, Afrooz Jalilzadeh 1, Erfan Yazdandoost Hamedani 1, Saif R. Kazi 2 and Anatoly Zlotnik 2

Abstract — We propose an integrated market equilibrium model for determining the spot market prices and flow rates in a natural gas pipeline network. The traditional approach solves the market equilibrium model separately from the natural gas network flow allocation problem in a sequential manner. Our proposed framework synthesizes marketing and operations by using a bilevel optimization formulation where the market equilibrium model in the upper level is connected and simultaneously solved with the natural gas network flow model in the lower level. We introduce convex relaxations for the nonconvex Weymouth pipe pressure drop equation and a surrogate for the compressor operating cost. Furthermore, we compare multiple single level reformulations of the bilevel problem based on Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions, Wolfe duality, and Lagrange duality conditions. Numerical studies on a small 6-node network demonstrates the efficacy and accuracy of our model and the comparison between different duality and optimality based reformulations. 

I. INTRODUCTION The urgent need to increase electricity supplies worldwide is driving rapid construction of natural gas-fired generators to service base load in the power grid as well as to compensate for variability and intermittency in concurrently expanding renewable electricity sources [1]. The resulting interdepen-dence between energy flows in gas pipelines and the power grid, as well as markets for wholesale electricity and natural gas has motivated methods to coordinate operations [2], [3] as well as financial markets for wholesale power and gas in day-ahead and in real-time markets [4]. Gas-electric system interdependence results in complex market dynamics [1], in which actions in one market influence outcomes in both. A major difference between how the power grid and gas pipelines function is the degree to which physical and financial decision-making are integrated. For the power grid, system operators dispatch generators based on the outcomes of optimal power flow (OPF) optimization problems [5]. The OPF problem determines the power production setpoints for participating generators that meet demand, satisfy transmission constraints, and minimize cost. The dual variables are interpreted as locational marginal prices of power [6]. In contrast, gas pipelines are built to provide a given transportation capacity on each segment, and gas transportation service is sold to shippers [7], [8]. This first stage typically uses capacity models, and does not consider physical variables such as pressure or flow. Pipeline operators then determine compressor settings and the flow allocation on the pipeline that satisfy consumer nominations. Researchers have examined problem formulations to syn-thesize financial and operating processes in gas pipeline net-works using optimization [9], [10], [11]. A proposed solution is the optimal gas flow (OGF) problem, which has been ex-amined in steady-state [12], [13] and dynamic contexts [14], [15]. While it can be used to produce physical and financial solutions, the OGF is a single-level nonlinear program (NLP) formulation that additively includes competing terms that maximize revenue and minimize compressor operating costs in the objective function. While the OGF can be interpreted as a representation of an efficient market for congestion pricing on a pipeline network, it does not represent the actual two-stage function of wholesale gas markets and operations. In addition, the nonlinearity and nonconvexity of gas flow equations warrants careful interpretation of the dual solution in the OGF and other optimization problems for natural gas networks. To model the hierarchical interplay between economic trading and physical transport in liberalized gas markets, recent studies established multi-level equilibrium frameworks where upper-level market participants optimize competitive choices while lower-level transmission system operators (TSOs) enforce physical network constraints [16], [17]. Because embedded lower-level physics that are governed by non-convex equations like the Weymouth pressure-drop relationship preclude standard Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) or strong-duality reformulations, concurrent studies intro-duced tailored convex relaxations (such as second-order cone bounds) coupled with disjunctive cuts and branch-and-bound techniques [18], [19]. This integration of physical transport dynamics with convex relaxation schemes yields computationally tractable single-level reformulations that rig-orously account for TSO control, market power, and network bottlenecks in large-scale natural gas grids. In this study, we develop a bi-level optimization formu-lation that represents the wholesale market and operating arXiv:2610.14339v1 [math.OC] 9 Oct 2026 solution stages of decision making related to flow scheduling in gas pipeline systems. The upper or outer level is acapacity model that maximizes economic value for pipeline users based on supply and demand bids, and accounts for regulated tariffs on each pipeline segment. The lower or inner level solves an optimal gas flow problem that minimizes the cost of gas compressor operation to deliver the cleared gas nominations subject to the physical and engineering constraints used in the OGF. We then apply and compare various convex relaxations and single-level reformulations, including Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) based mathematical program with complementarity constraints (MPCC) [20], Wolfe-duality (WDP) [21], Mond-Weir (MDP) [22], and Lagrange-dual [23] reformulations. Our key contribution is a theorem on projection of feasible sets and lifting of feasible points for these reformulations, and we use a market equi-librium test case for a small synthetic network to compare the latter using several performance metrics. The remainder of the paper is as follows. In Section II, we describe the bilevel market equilibrium problem expansion of the OGF. In Section III, we describe convexification of the equality constraints that represent steady-state gas flow through a pipe. In Section IV, we describe single-level reformulations of the bilevel problem, and then state and prove our main feasibility equivalence theorem. We describe the test case and numerical experiments in Section V, and then conclude in Section VI. II. B ILEVEL GAS NETWORK MARKET MODEL

Bilevel optimization problems are structurally non-convex and NP-hard [20], making it very difficult to find their optimal solutions. Typical methods reformulate the bilevel problem into a single level optimization problem using either the optimality or duality conditions on the lower level problem. It is known that optimal solutions of the single level reformulation need not be optimal for the original bilevel problem [20]. However, it has been shown that if the lower level problem is convex and the global solution of the single level reformulation lies in the interior of it’s feasible region, then the solution is a global optimal point to the bilevel problem [26]. The original bilevel gas network market optimization prob-lem formulated in Eq. (14) is both nonlinear and nonconvex. We thus propose convex relaxations to the lower level problem such that the leader-follower formalism from [26] is applicable in this case. The main non-convexities in the lower level problem arise from the objective function, the bilinear compressor equalities, and the nonlinear Weymouth equation. In the following subsections, we propose convex reformulations for the objective function which substitutes the compressor ratios, and relax the Weymouth equalities using a set of convex hull inequalities for fixed directional flow.

L(x, ·, u, v) and complementarity gives L(x, y, u, v) = Q(y), the same multipliers yield d(x, u, v) = Q(y) and hence satisfy the LDR inequality (33) as well. The literature shows that MPCC, WDP, MDP, eMDP, and LDR characterize the feasible set of Eq. (25) when strong duality holds with dual attainment for every x [21], [22], [23], [28]. Thus, none can generate a spurious follower response, and the assumption is needed only to guarantee that every follower-optimal response can be represented in the corresponding reformulation. Second, when the objective comparison in a dual-based formulation is enforced only within a tolerance ε,as in numerical computations, we show that ε simultaneously bounds the follower suboptimality of y, the duality gap at x,and the multiplier suboptimality. We developed a bilevel gas-market model coupling market equilibrium model with network operation using a leader-follower reformulation approach. For the convexified fol-lower, we characterized the common certifiable response set of five single-level formulations and bounded follower suboptimality through dual-certificate tolerances. On the six-node instance, relaxed MPCC, WDP, and MDP, and ex-act MPCC, matched the best-known Gurobi objectives and service with residuals below 10 −6. Exact WDP matched these values but exceeded the stationarity threshold. These numerical observations do not extend the convex follower guarantees to the exact nonconvex model.
