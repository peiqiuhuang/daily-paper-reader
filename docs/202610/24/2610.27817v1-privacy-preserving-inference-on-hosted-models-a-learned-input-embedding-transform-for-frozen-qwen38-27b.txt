# Privacy-preserving inference on hosted models: a learned input-embedding transform for frozen Qwen3.8-27B

Abstract 

Every prompt sent to a hosted language model is readable by whoever runs it. We study whether the prompt can remain on the client while the host receives transformed vectors that stand in for it. A language model converts text to vectors exactly once, with a public lookup table; we perform that step locally and pass the vectors through a small trained network (55.6M parameters) before they leave the perimeter. The host runs a frozen 27B model (Qwen3.8-27B) on the transformed vectors and never receives text or token ids. We train the transform against the frozen model as a fixed judge, combining a forward-KL utility term with an auxiliary term that pushes each vector away from its original direction. On 1,490 MMLU questions held out from training, the transform costs 1 .5 points of accuracy (80 .7% vs. 82 .3%), breaking 68 of the 1 ,226 questions the unmodified model answers correctly (5 .5%), while 30 .1% of content tokens no longer decode to their original token under a nearest-neighbour lookup. We report sweeps over the inference-time strength scalar and the training-time hiding weight, and two continuation budgets. Neither sweep improves the trade: hiding collapses to 1 .1% at strength 0.8 while 48 answers are still broken, and every hiding weight above 0 .3 roughly doubles the breaks. Continued training is the one exception, improving both axes at 2 ,000 additional examples on two seeds before reversing at 6 ,000. Nearest-neighbour hiding is only a diagnostic; it is not a privacy guarantee. An adversary that can run a transform of this family on inputs of its own choosing recovers the prompt outright. Estimating the displacement subspace at rank 64 and projecting it out returns all 2 ,794 content tokens of a held-out 96-prompt set from the checkpoint reported above. The hiding rate on that same set reads 59 .7%. Recovery is equally complete in all 42 cross-checkpoint pairs tested, so keeping the transform’s weights on-prem does not help either. That an unkeyed transform of this family falls to an adaptive attacker is already known [4]. We additionally report the transfer between checkpoints and the utility cost measured alongside it. Accordingly, we do not claim privacy. The transform preserves much of the model’s utility under the measurement protocol given here, and fails against a simple adaptive attack. 

# 1 Introduction 

A hosted model needs the whole input to do its work. Whatever is sent, the host holds a copy for at least the duration of the request. For sensitive inputs such as customer records, medical notes or internal documents, this means the provider sees the data during inference. Each of the usual mitigations gives something up. Redaction changes the question the model is asked, so it can change the answer that comes back. Self-hosting re-moves the host from the picture, but the cost of serving a 27B model then falls on the user. A trusted execu-tion environment seals the input inside the host’s hard-ware [17], which helps as far as the host’s configuration can be trusted. This paper measures a different route. A transformer consumes text only through its first step: tokenisation followed by a lookup in a public embedding table. Ev-ery later layer is arithmetic on vectors. The lookup can therefore be moved to the client, and the host given vec-tors instead of tokens. The table is public, so the move alone hides nothing: anyone can invert a raw embed-ding by a table lookup. We therefore measure whether a 1arXiv:2610.27817v1 [cs.AI] 20 Oct 2026 learned transform can displace those vectors far enough to frustrate recovery while the frozen model still com-putes the right answer. Both properties have to hold at once, and either one alone is easy to get. Neither extreme is useful: raw em-beddings are trivially invertible, whereas noise strong enough to hide the input destroys the model’s utility. The difficulty is in obtaining the two together, which is what the rest of this paper measures. We do not obtain them: the utility side is affordable and we characterise its price, and the privacy side fails outright to an ad-versary that can run the transform on inputs of its own choosing (§5.7).

# 2 Threat model and scope 

What is in scope. The prompt, as it crosses the client boundary. The host receives a floating-point ten-sor and generation settings. It gets no text and no token ids, and it never runs the tokeniser. §5.7 tests whether transmitting only transformed vectors is suffi-cient to conceal the prompt. 

Out of scope. The answer. The host generates it and can read it. Any fact the answer discloses is disclosed to the host, so a request whose output restates a sen-sitive field cannot hide that field from the host by this mechanism. Prompt length is visible, as is the number of generated tokens and the timing of the request. 

Adversary. We distinguish two. A passive adversary observes the transmitted tensor and knows the public embedding table. An active adversary additionally sub-mits inputs of its own choosing and observes the corre-sponding tensors, learning the transform’s response on chosen plaintexts. The hiding rate of §4.2 characterises the weakest passive attack, nearest-neighbour decoding. What the active adversary achieves is reported in §5.7. 

Custody of the transform. The transform runs on the client, so a deployment may hold θ on-prem and never publish it. We treat that as an operational con-trol rather than part of the boundary, and assume the adversary knows θ. The attack of §5.7 does not rely on knowing θ. The reason is structural: the rows of ∆ lie in the row space of W2, at most r = 1024 of d = 5120 dimensions, and with ε = 0 the remaining dimensions carry E unchanged. That subspace is what the attack of §5.7 estimates, and it is a property of the architecture and its training recipe rather than of one checkpoint— which is why the attack transfers between checkpoints (§5.7) and why an adversary holding any member of the family is as dangerous as one holding θ itself. We expect, but have not shown, that an adversary confined to observing ordinary protected traffic can reach the same subspace without submitting anything: the positions that still decode correctly—the major-ity, or utility would fail—supply matched pairs ( Zi, E i)whose differences span it. The one observer-side attack we implemented, on a 7B pilot, had to guess E and recovered nothing. Closing that gap would remove the last operational restriction on the adversary, and we flag it as untested rather than unavailable. 

Not a cryptographic claim. The transform has no key and admits no ε. It is empirical obfusca-tion measured against specific decoders, and nothing stronger [18].

The hiding rate is strongly length-dependent (Table 3): 41 .5% on prompts of at most 96 tokens against 13 .0% beyond 384. ## 5.5 The hiding weight 

The hiding weight λc is a training-time choice, so each value requires a new transform. Table 4 reports one run each at λc ∈ { 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0} with every other setting held fixed, including seed, batching and schedule. The sweep therefore supports only the weaker conclusion: no value of λc tested improves hiding at a proportionate cost in breaks.

## 5.7 The chosen-input attack 

The recovery reported here is conditional, and the con-ditions are worth stating before the result. The adver-sary needs two things: a copy of a transform trained by this recipe—not necessarily the victim’s own—and the ability to run it on inputs of its choosing. Both are or-dinary in a client-side deployment, since the transform ships to the client in order to be used, but they are con-ditions, and an adversary with neither is outside what this subsection measures. Given both, the method is direct. For the evaluated attack we use the same inference configuration as the main result: a = 1 and ε = 0. On inputs it chooses, the adversary knows E exactly, so it observes Z −E = ∆. It takes the leading right singular vectors of the displace-ments it has collected, projects intercepted traffic onto their orthogonal complement, and applies the nearest-neighbour lookup of Eq. (7). The displacement cancels and E survives, because Eq. (2) confines ∆ to at most r

of d dimensions and leaves the remaining d−r directions of E untouched. Rank 64 suffices. On the anchor set of §5.3—96 held-out Alpaca prompts—the attack returns 2 ,794 of 2 ,794 content tokens from the checkpoint of Table 1, with no learned weights and no gradient steps. The hid-ing rate of §4.2 reads 59 .7% on that same set, mea-sured on the same tensors. The two numbers together show that nearest-neighbour hiding substantially over-states protection against this attack: the rate reports one decoder’s failure, and a decoder that removes the displacement is not that decoder. The adversary’s copy need not be the victim’s. Esti-mating the subspace from one checkpoint and applying it to traffic protected by a different one recovers every content token in all 42 (attacker, victim) pairs tested. The subspace appears to be a property of the architec-ture and training recipe rather than of a particular run, which is what removes on-prem custody of θ (§2) from the set of controls that would help. The broader vulnerability of an unkeyed transform is already known. [4], whose objective is ours (§7), re-ports 100% attack success against the unkeyed abla-tion of its own method under an adaptive white-box at-tacker, and identifies key-conditioning as the difference between that row and a defensible system. The trans-form measured here is unkeyed, and this result is what that predicts. In our evaluation a rank-64 subspace es-timate is sufficient, and the attack transfers between checkpoints. Eq. (2) explains the low-rank structure it exploits: the displacement lies in a subspace of rank at most r = 1024. 

Scope of the claim. One attack family was run. We did not implement a learned inverter or iterative cor-rection [13] against this checkpoint, and the passive ad-versary of §2—restricted to observing ordinary traffic, with no inputs of its own—remains untested. The re-sult above therefore establishes that this construction fails against one specific and cheap adversary, which is sufficient to withhold a privacy claim. It is not a gen-eral statement that every attack succeeds. The utility measurements of §5 were not collected under a threat model this paper defends. 

# 6 Limitations and future work 

The hiding rate is not a privacy measure. It reports the failure of one decoder: nearest-neighbour lookup in the public table, position by position. A fixed rotation of the embedding space defeats that decoder completely while preserving every pairwise relationship in the data, so a high hiding rate licenses no conclusion. A low hiding rate does show exposure, but a high one does not show protection. §5.7 measures that gap di-rectly: 59 .7% hidden on the anchor set of §5.3, whose every content token the chosen-input adversary returns. We report the rate because it is cheap enough to run on every input and comparable across transforms. We use it only as a decoder-specific diagnostic. 

The fixed displacement subspace is structurally vulnerable. Under the deterministic inference set-ting evaluated here ( ε = 0), §5.7 is decisive against the residual form Z = E + a∆ at any setting of the weights: Eq. (2) confines the displacement to at most r

dimensions, so E passes through unchanged in at least 

d−r = 4096 directions and no amount of training moves it. Sampled noise is a separate case we have not tested, since ε ⊙ σ has diagonal covariance in Rd (§3) rather than lying in the row space of W2. Avoiding this attack appears to require either a displacement spanning Rd or a secret the adversary does not hold. We have evaluated neither. Keying is the route the literature has taken [4], and it relocates the problem rather than removing it: the same ablation that shows the keyed model resisting a mismatched-key attacker shows it yielding completely to one holding the right key, so what is being defended is key management. Raising r towards d is the cheaper of the two to test here and is the first item of future work. Whether the utility cost measured here survives either change is unknown. The inversion literature (§7) 6points the same way, though none of those attacks is implemented here. 

The answer is unprotected. The host generates the reply and can read it. A workload whose answer restates the sensitive field gains nothing for that field. 

Evaluation is multiple-choice. The break rate scores a single letter per question, which bounds the damage to a four-way decision and says nothing about free-form generation quality. A pilot on 96 free-text prompts broke 3 of 70 correct answers, too few to con-clude from; a larger blind-graded run is outstanding. 

Statistical scope. One seed per point in Table 4; two seeds per continuation budget in Table 5. Confidence in-tervals treat questions as independent, which questions within a subject are not, so the stated intervals are op-timistic. The paired tests between arms are exploratory and gate no decision. 

Distribution. The transform saw only Alpaca in-struction prompts of at most 96 tokens. Hiding degrades with length (Table 3), and the evaluation distribution differs from training in domain as well as length. 

Scale. Every number here comes from one small con-figuration: a 55 .6M-parameter transform with a bottle-neck of r = 1024, trained on 800 Alpaca prompts for 20 ,000 examples on a single H200. Three scaling ques-tions remain. A corpus matched to the evaluation dis-tribution would separate the length dependence above from the training set that produced it. A larger compute budget would allow more seeds and training schedules. A wider bottleneck bears on §2 directly, since the rank bound on the displacement is set by r: the question is whether subspace estimation still recovers the input as 

r approaches d, which a higher hiding rate does not an-swer. The one scale result we have settles none of the three, since the continuation arms improved both axes at 2 ,000 additional examples and reversed at 6 ,000 (Ta-ble 5) with the optimiser restart confounded with the extra examples. 

# 7 Related work 

Cryptographic and hardware routes. Confiden-tial computing seals the input inside host hardware, which moves the trust to the hardware vendor and leaves the data itself unchanged [17]. Secure multi-party and homomorphic protocols give provable guarantees at a cost in latency that is still the binding constraint for models of this size; [12] surveys the practical trade-offs across this space. Our method provides no comparable cryptographic or hardware-backed guarantee, and is not an alternative to these routes. 

Inversion attacks. These are the adversarial base-line. Embeddings leak substantial input content to learned inverters [3], iterative correction recovers most short texts exactly [13], and vocabulary-matching against hidden states recovers text in the third-party inference setting specifically [5]. Our nearest-neighbour hiding rate (§4.2) is weaker than all three. The attack of §5.7 assumes more than any of them—query access to a transform of the family—but needs no training in exchange, and recovers everything. 

Client-side perturbation of representations. 

This is the family the present transform belongs to: the client holds the embedding step and sends perturbed vectors onward. [6] obfuscates word representations while keeping them usable; [7] splits the model so the embedding layer runs on the client, adds locally differ-entially private noise, and denoises the output client-side; [8] and [9] sanitise at the text level for closed-box models. These methods differ primarily in the guarantees they provide. [7] carries a local differential privacy budget; [10] derives its protection from ( k, ϵ )-anonymity; [11] states Rényi-DP guarantees for a keyed permutation scheme. Those are claims of a different type from the one available here, which is empirical obfuscation mea-sured against named decoders and nothing more. The transform in this paper belongs to the last group, and §5.7 is the cost of that position. 

What these systems are evaluated against. 

The evaluation norm in this family is a passive de-coder. [7] reports nearest-neighbour token inversion and an attribute-inference attack; [6] reports text-reconstruction attacks; [10] reports nearest-search at-tacks together with a trained inverter, which is astronger decoder than any we run against our own checkpoint; [11] reports token-frequency matching and a learned substitution-deciphering attack; [4] reports nearest-neighbour retrieval and vocabulary matching. An adaptive adversary appears once in this set, in [4]’s key ablation, and a chosen-input subspace estimation appears nowhere. We do not read this as an oversight peculiar to any one paper. [12] makes a related observa-tion, that methods of this class “do not protect model parameters, inference internals, or adaptive multi-query interactions”—which is the adversary of §5.7. 

Relation to OSNIP. [4] is the closest prior work. Its objective is ours: a forward KL between a frozen model’s predictive distributions under clean and per-turbed embeddings, plus a penalty on | cos( h, z )|, with the perturbation applied residually and the norm held fixed—in their case by projecting back onto the sphere of radius ∥h∥, in ours by the penalty λn of Eq. (5), and in both cases by backpropagating through the frozen host. Their hiding term is hinged at a margin ϵ where 7ours is not, and they train on a mixture that is 80% Al-paca and evaluate on MMLU among others. We claim no methodological novelty over that construction. The substantive difference is the key. OSNIP condi-tions the transform on a user-specific secret and adds a diversity term that forces different keys to produce ge-ometrically separated outputs; the transform measured here is unkeyed. [4] directly evaluates the effect of key conditioning in its ablation, which reports 100% attack success against the no-key variant under an adaptive white-box attacker, against 0% for the keyed model un-der a mismatched key. §5.7 is therefore an independent confirmation of that row rather than a new finding, at 27B scale and with the attack named and costed—rank 64, one SVD, no learned weights—and with the struc-tural reason it works (Eq. (2) bounds the displacement to r of d dimensions) made explicit. Relative to [4] we additionally evaluate cross-checkpoint transfer and report utility as paired answer changes. Our recovery needs no access to the victim’s own transform, only to a sibling trained by the same recipe, which holds across all 42 checkpoint pairs tested and shows that on-prem custody of the victim’s θ does not prevent this attack. [4] reports 98 .49–101 .48% re-tained performance; we report the same kind of config-uration as 68 broken answers against 45 repaired ones on paired questions (§5), concentrated in near-ties (Ta-ble 2). Both readings can be true of one system, and §4.2 explains why we think only the second is informa-tive for a user who cares which answers change. We also note that [4]’s own ablation records 100% attack success against the keyed model when the attacker holds the correct key. In that ablation, resistance depends on the attacker not possessing the correct key. 

Mechanics. Our objective is a distillation-style diver-gence between a frozen teacher’s answer distributions under clean and perturbed inputs [14], with reparame-terised sampling [15]. 

# 8 Conclusion 

A contextual transform on input embeddings can be trained against frozen Qwen3.8-27B without touching the host model. On 1,490 MMLU questions held out from training, the host scores 80 .7% through the trans-form against 82 .3% clean, and 5 .5% of the answers it previously got right come back wrong. At the same time 30 .1% of content tokens no longer decode to them-selves under a nearest-neighbour lookup. The damage concentrates in near-ties, and neither the inference-time strength scalar nor the training-time hiding weight buys a better trade: hiding disappears at a ≤ 0.8 while most of the answer damage remains, and every larger hiding weight roughly doubles the breaks. The utility cost is therefore modest and reasonably well characterised, and it is not the binding constraint. An adversary that holds a transform of this family— any member of it, not the victim’s—and can run it on chosen inputs recovers every content token of the evaluated checkpoint at rank 64 (§5.7), in all 42 pairs tested. Against that adversary neither the hiding rate nor on-prem custody of the weights is a control. The ex-periments therefore characterise the utility cost of this transform and give a protocol for measuring it. They do not support a privacy claim: the residual architecture does not protect the prompt from an adversary who can query it. The mechanism is not obviously beyond repair, and the repair the field has adopted is a secret key [4]. In its own ablation the keyed model resists an attacker hold-ing the wrong key completely and yields to one holding the right key completely. Resistance there depends on the attacker not possessing the correct key, not on the geometry of the embedding space. A displacement span-ning Rd rather than a fixed r-dimensional subspace is the other direction, untested here. Whether either pre-serves the utility measured above is unknown. The next experiments should test a wider bottleneck, a corpus matched to the evaluation distribution, and a budget affording several seeds per point. The chosen-input at-tack of §5.7, with thresholds declared in advance, should be run against any checkpoint they produce before it is described as private.
