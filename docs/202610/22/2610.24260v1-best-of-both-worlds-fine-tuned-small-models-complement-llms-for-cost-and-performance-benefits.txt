Abstract 

BERT-based models require thousands of la-beled samples to train. Larger models like GPT–X require only 1–5 examples per class. However, performance, cost and time trade-offs of such models remain under-explored. In this work, we classify survey text data into 16 classes. We focus on experimental approaches and results on performance, cost, and time taken for this problem. We compare these pa-rameters of a trained RoBERTa-based model, and an (off-the-shelf) GPT–3.5 model for text classification. Baseline experiments show that the time taken by GPT–3.5 for generating pre-dictions on 10,000 records is 6 hours, whereas a RoBERTa-based model takes only 12 mins on a T4 GPU. To identify the best prompting ap-proach for GPT–3.5, we explore batch prompt-ing. We experiment with (1) batch-size, and (2) few-shot sampling. We find the best batch size is 20 with human few-shot curation. Experi-ments show the F1 score of a trained RoBERTa-based model (96%) is much higher than a GPT– 3.5 model (83%). In case of GPT–3.5, batch prompting is beneficial in lowering inference costs by 70%, without hindering task perfor-mance. We find the best approach is an ensem-ble of a RoBERTa-based and GPT–3.5 model (F1: 97.5%) incurring only 4.4% of inference cost compared to a standalone GPT–3.5. 

1 Introduction 

Natural language processing has seen impressive advancements in the past few years, especially in text classification. Large language models have achieved performance comparable to supervised baselines such as question answering (Trivedi et al., 2023), named entity recognition (Wang et al., 2025), relation extraction (Wan et al., 2023) and hu-man annotator performance (Gilardi et al., 2023).

Despite the remarkable success, previous studies (Sun et al., 2023; Mathur et al., 2023) show that LLMs may still significantly underperform fine-tuned models in the task of text classification. In this paper, we do a performance, cost, and time tradeoff of GPT–3.5 and a supervised RoBERTa-based model on text classification task. We ex-periment with different batch sizes, few shot sizes and sample selection strategies to identify the best prompting approach in terms of performance, cost, and time suitable for the dataset. The dataset is a survey with an open text response. We apply NLP models to classify these response texts into pre-defined topics/classes for better insights and speedy actions. Our final approach involves using a combination of fine-tuned RoBERTa-based model and GPT–3.5, where GPT–3.5 is used to classify the response texts that have a confidence less than 0.5. This approach boosts F1 score to 97.5% incurring only 4.4% of inference cost compared to when an off-the-shelf LLM (GPT–3.5) alone was used. 

2 Problem Background 

Open text response classification is challenging. It involves reasoning over long text and overlapping diverse topics. For experiments in this work, we consider a subset of survey text responses (around 10,000 samples) for prediction and 30,000 of la-beled texts for generating few shot samples or train-ing. We pick 16 topics with clear definitions that are mutually exclusive.

3.1 Baseline Experiments 

Our baseline experiments consist of three models (Table 1). The first model is a fine-tuned RoBERTa-based model. It is trained on 21,000 samples and validated on 9,000 samples. The second is a zero-shot GPT–3.5 model and the final model is a 3-few-shot GPT–3.5. The RoBERTa-based model outperforms both GPT–3.5 approaches. 

3.2 Batch Prompting 

In the preliminary stage of our experiment, we seek to determine the optimal batch size for our input data. To maintain consistency, we kept the size of few-shot examples constant (3 samples) while varying the batch sizes from 10 to 50 in increments of 10. This approach enables conducting an ex-ploration of different batch sizes and its impact on the overall performance, time and cost. This also served as a step in deciding the optimal batch size for subsequent few shot experiments. 

3.3 Few-Shot Techniques

Longer response texts can have two or more topics associated with them introducing complex-ity for the model. Also, having shorter few-shot examples keeps runtimes and costs within budget. 5.2 Few-Shot Techniques 

In-context learning involves providing a few demonstration examples in the prompt template. Performance of GPT–3.5 can show quite the depen-dence on a few shot examples (Brown et al., 2020). There has been past work in determining ways to select such examples for attaining better perfor-mance. Skill-based k-nearest neighbor approaches have been used to select few shot examples (An et al., 2023). Trained retriever models (Rubin et al., 2022) are also used to extract the best suited exam-ples for tasks. 

6 Conclusion 

We carry out an exploration of performance, cost and time tradeoff of a fine-tuned RoBERTa-based model and a GPT–3.5 model for a text classifica-tion task. We explore batch prompting, a technique where multiple responses can be processed in a single API call thus saving cost and time. Upon experimenting with different batch sizes, we find that a batch size of 20 is optimal in terms of perfor-mance, cost and time for our data. Human-curation of few-shot examples emerges as a cost-effective strategy, with an optimal size of around three ex-amples to achieve a favorable F1-score without incurring unnecessary costs. In Appendix A, we examine errors, exploring error types such as com-pound, domain-based, and granularity-based errors, highlighting the need for labels and few-shot ex-amples to be unambiguous and mutually exclusive. Our final approach of using an ensemble model by first using RoBERTa-based model and then us-ing GPT–3.5 on the records where former is less confident achieves superior performance of 97.5% incurring only 4.4% of inference cost compared to when a pre-trained LLM (GPT–3.5) alone was used. For generating training data, we propose leveraging LLMs along with human labeled data. As future work, we would like to explore batching similar records and other batch selection techniques. Also, exploring current state-of-art LLMs and other open-source LLMs to assess the trade-offs would be a promising direction.

Compound : The response text may contain to-kens of a certain class (“equipment”) but belongs to some other class. For example, “Train on the equip-ment” ought to fall into Development as opposed to Equipment (since it talks about training). 

Domain or Short Text : An off-the-shelf GPT– 3.5 despite having provided few-shot examples lacks the deep domain-level understanding of a trained RoBERTa-based model. Especially in cases where response texts can be shorter (giving lesser context). In the example, “snack” refers to the pro-vision of snacks, which is aligned to benefits, as opposed to no complaint. 

Granularity : At times GPT–3.5 assigns a class, but its reasons towards a rather more generic class. In the example “Info on hours” does imply some level of communication but more specifically is a shift related topic.
