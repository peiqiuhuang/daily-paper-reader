Abstract 

Bilevel data selection is a fundamental task with numerous applications in machine learning and statistics. A common strategy for tackling this problem is to relax the original bilevel combinatorial optimization into a continuous one. However, exist-ing methods, including 0-1 relaxation and probabilistic relaxation, still suffer from several limitations, such as unstable gradient estimation and infeasible rounding. To address these issues, this paper proposes a new probabilistic relaxation for the multi-source bilevel data selection problem. Our key idea is to introduce multiple learnable categorical priors to parameterize a probability distribution supported only on feasible data subsets, thus naturally yielding a lossless rounding scheme. We further show that our proposed relaxation admits an effective two-point gradi-ent estimator, which can simultaneously avoid the inverse-Hessian computation required by existing 0-1 relaxation and the unbounded score term appearing in prior probabilistic relaxation. Furthermore, we develop a reducible representation of this estimator, which substantially reduces the number of lower-level retrainings re-quired for gradient computation. Built upon this reducible two-point estimator, we then design a novel stochastic gradient algorithm, termed ReSGD , for multi-source bilevel data selection problem, and establish a rigorous approximation guarantee for the subset returned by ReSGD under weak submodularity. Finally, extensive experiments are conducted to validate the effectiveness of our proposed ReSGD .

1 Introduction 

Data selection aims to identify a small subset of training samples such that training solely on this subset can achieve performance comparable to that trained on the full dataset. As modern learning systems continue to grow in scale, this task has become increasingly crucial in a wide range of applications, including efficient training [15, 33, 34, 55, 78, 79], in-context learning [63, 64, 113] and continual learning [8, 9, 48, 104, 116]. For instance, in continual learning, an effective way to counter catastrophic forgetting [39, 77] is to maintain a small rehearsal memory that stores a subset of previously seen data and then replay them during training on new tasks [12, 18, 72, 111]. From an optimization viewpoint, many of data selection problems [8, 23, 60, 67] can be formulated as bilevel combinatorial optimization , where the upper level evaluates the selected subset according to the performance of the model induced by the corresponding lower-level training problem. However, the discrete nature of data selection makes the underlying bilevel problem highly challenging. To circumvent this, recent works have sought to bring the original bilevel combinatorial optimization 

> Preprint. arXiv:2610.14942v1 [cs.LG] 10 Oct 2026

into the world of continuous optimization, thereby leveraging the rich toolbox of gradient-based methods [7, 65, 83]. Existing approaches mainly follow two routes. The first is 0-1 relaxation [8, 48], which directly relaxes the binary selective mask into a continuous variable in [0 , 1] and then applies implicit differentiation [73, 89] to solve the resulting bilevel continuous optimization. The second is 

probabilistic relaxation [116], which treats each binary mask as a Bernoulli random variable with learnable parameters and then optimizes these parameters via policy gradient descent [107, 101, 58]. Although these two aforementioned continuous-relaxation reformulations have made data selection tasks more tractable, their practical implementation still faces several fundamental limitations: 

➊ Problematic Gradient Estimations. In 0-1 relaxation, estimating the upper-level gradient typically requires approximating the inverse Hessian of the lower-level objective, which is often compu-tationally prohibitive [8, 48, 114]. In contrast, probabilistic relaxation avoids such second-order computation via policy-gradient estimators [101, 107], but these estimators are unbounded and often suffer from instability and high variance during training [46, 108]; 

➋ Limited to Single-Source Scenarios. Both 0-1 and probabilistic relaxation primarily focus on subset selection over a single homogeneous dataset . However, in many real-world applications, we often encounter multiple heterogeneous data sources rather than a single-source dataset. A representative example arises in continual learning, where one often needs to retain a small data subset separately from each past task for future replay [12, 72, 104]. Such settings cannot be trivially decomposed into multiple independent single-source data selection problems, since this treatment will ignore the cross-source correlations among datasets and lead to redundant selections ;

➌ Heuristic and Infeasible Rounding. In continuous relaxation, rounding or derandomization refers to the conversion of the obtained continuous solution into a discrete solution [1, 11]. However, 

satisfactory rounding schemes are still lacking for both 0-1 and probabilistic relaxation. In 0-1 relaxation, current rounding methods are heuristic , so there is no principled way to quantify how the obtained subset relates to the original continuous solution. In probabilistic relaxation, sampling from the learned Bernoulli distributions may return a subset outside the target constraint [13, 19, 20]; 

➍ Incomplete Convergence Analysis. Existing theoretical results of both 0-1 and probabilistic relax-ation mainly focus on convergence to a continuous stationary point [116, 48, 23, 9]. However, this is insufficient for data selection, since our primary interest lies in the quality of the resulting subset rather than the intermediate continuous solution. To date, how to establish rigorous theoretical guarantees for the obtained subset remains an uncharted territory in bilevel data selection. In view of all this, a natural question arises: Can we develop a provable continuous-relaxation framework, together with guaranteed 

rounding scheme and effective gradient estimator, for multi-source data selection problems? In this paper, we will provide an affirmative answer to this question by presenting a novel probabilistic relaxation for multi-source data selection problems. Our key idea is to introduce multiple learnable categorical priors to parameterize a probability distribution supported only on feasible data subsets. Compared with prior relaxations, this constrained construction immediately yields a lossless rounding scheme for multi-source data selection problem. Moreover, we derive an effective two-point gradient estimator for our proposed probabilistic relaxation, which can simultaneously avoid the costly inverse-Hessian computation in 0-1 relaxation [8, 48] and the unbounded score in prior probabilistic relaxation [116], thereby achieving a significant variance-reduction effect. Based on this two-point estimator, we further develop an SGD algorithm, termed ReSGD , for the multi-source data selection problem, and establish a rigorous approximation guarantee for the subset returned by ReSGD under 

weak submodularity [24, 102]. In summary, we make the following contributions: 

• We present a novel probabilistic relaxation for multi-source data selection problem, which only assigns probability mass to feasible subsets and thus naturally induces a lossless rounding scheme. 

• We prove that our proposed probabilistic relaxation admits an effect two-point gradient estimator, which avoids both the inverse-Hessian computation in 0-1 relaxation and the unbounded score in prior probabilistic relaxation , thereby improving stability and reducing variance. Furthermore, under (γ, β )-weak submodularity [102], we show that the subset returned by ReSGD achieves a

3 A New Probabilistic Relaxation via Learnable Categorical Priors 

This section mainly introduces a new probabilistic relaxation for the multi-source data selection prob-lem (6). As discussed in Section 2.1, a critical drawback of the current probabilistic relaxation [116] is that the induced Bernoulli product distribution will assign non-zero probability to subsets outside 

the target constraint, which renders the subsequent rounding operation intrinsically infeasible . 4.1 Approximation Guarantee under Weak Submodularity 

This subsection aims to theoretically verify the effectiveness of our proposed ReSGD . Specifically, we will exploit an intrinsic combinatorial structure of our concerned multi-source data selection problem, known as weak submodularity [22, 24, 30, 102], to establish an approximation guarantee for ReSGD .Before delving into the details, we first introduce some commonly used notations. Specifically, for any set function f : 2 D → R, we denote the marginal gain of adding B to A by f (B|A ) ≜

f (A ∪ B ) − f (A). Especially when B ≜ {e} is a singleton, we simply write f (e | A ) ≜ f ({e} | A ).In addition, we say that a set function f is monotone if and only if f (A) ≤ f (B) for any A ⊆ B ⊆ D .Since weak submodularity is typically used to characterize utility maximization problems, in the remainder of this subsection, we consider an equivalent utility function U (θ∗(S)) of the upper-level loss L(θ∗(S)) in Eq. 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we present a novel probabilistic relaxation for the multi-source data selection problem, which only assigns probability mass to feasible subsets and thus naturally induces a lossless rounding scheme. Most importantly, we show that this relaxation admits an effective two-point gradient estimator, avoiding both the inverse-Hessian computation in 0-1 relaxation and the unbounded score in prior probabilistic relaxation . From a Bayesian perspective, we further derive a reducible representation that reduces the gradient-computation cost from O(h + Phi=1 ni) retrainings to O(h),where h is the number of sources and Phi=1 ni is the total sample size. Building upon this reducible representation, we develop a stochastic algorithm, termed ReSGD and prove that, under (γ, β )-weak submodularity, its returned subset achieves a   γ2

Bilevel Continuous Optimization. Bilevel continuous optimization (BCO) provides a natural framework for problems whose objective depends on the solution of another optimization problem, and has been widely used in hyperparameter optimization [37, 38, 97], meta-learning [36, 91], and neural architecture search [31, 69, 50]. Since its early formulation in mathematical programming [10], BCO has attracted extensive attention in both optimization and machine learning [114]. A major line of work focuses on hypergradient-based methods, which can be broadly categorized into approxi-mate implicit differentiation (AID) methods [42, 44, 45, 56, 89] and iterative differentiation (ITD) methods [37, 56, 75]. AID methods compute the hypergradient by differentiating the optimality conditions of the lower-level problem, typically involving Hessian inverse-vector products or their ap-proximations, while ITD methods differentiate through the trajectory of the lower-level optimization procedure. Another line of work adopts penalty-based reformulations, which transform the bilevel problem into a constrained or penalized single-level problem by replacing the lower-level solution requirement with its optimality conditions [21, 74, 99, 68]. 

Continual Learning. Continual learning aims to train a model on a sequence of tasks while preserving the knowledge acquired from previously seen data. A central challenge in this setting is 

catastrophic forgetting [39, 77], where the model quickly loses its performance on old tasks after being updated on new ones. Existing methods address this issue from different perspectives, including regularization-based methods [16, 61, 66, 94, 109, 112], parameter isolation based methods [87, 105, 110], and replay-based methods [8, 9, 12, 72, 104, 111, 116]. Among them, replay-based continual learning combats forgetting by maintaining a small memory buffer of representative samples from previous tasks and replaying them during future training. Since the memory budget is typically much smaller than the accumulated training data, the quality of the retained samples becomes critical to the effectiveness of rehearsal. This has motivated a series of coreset selection methods for continual learning [8, 9, 116, 48, 104]. For example, [8] extends coreset selection to deep neural networks by formulating it as a bilevel optimization problem. After that, [116] further introduces probabilistic masks to perform global subset selection. Next, [48] revisits bilevel coreset selection in continual learning and proposes a new 0-1 relaxation formulation. More recently, [104] design a greedy method according to the reducible loss proposed by [78]. 

Weakly Submodular Maximization. The notion of γ-weak submodularity was originally intro-duced by [24], who showed that the standard greedy algorithm achieves a (1 −e−γ )-approximation for maximizing a monotone γ-weakly submodular function under a cardinality constraint. Subsequently, [59] studied weakly submodular maximization beyond simple cardinality constraints and proved that the standard greedy algorithm achieves a 0.4γ2 

> √rγ +1

-approximation for maximizing a monotone 

γ-weakly submodular function under a matroid constraint, where r denotes the rank of the matroid. Unfortunately, this approximation guarantee explicitly depends on r and therefore deteriorates as the matroid rank increases. Note that the partition constraints considered in this paper are a special case of matroid constraints. Later, [102] introduced the upper submodularity ratio β and developed a distorted local search algorithm for monotone (γ, β )-weakly submodular maximization. This distorted local search typically requires repeated swap operations and will incur substantial computational overhead, making it difficult to apply to large-scale selection problems.

I Experiment with Adversarial Initialization 

In this section, we detail the empirical setup designed to rigorously evaluate the error-correction capabilities and robustness of the ReSGD algorithm. The experiment specifically investigates the algorithm’s capacity to filter corrupted labels and recover a high-quality coreset when subjected to an extreme, adversarial initialization state. 

Dataset Partitioning and Asymmetric Label Corruption. The experiment utilizes a sub-set of the MNIST dataset, partitioned into two disjoint subsets: Holdout Set Dholdout consists of 

10 , 000 pristine samples and serves as the reference distribution; Training Pool Dtrain consists of 

10 , 000 separate samples, acting as the active selection pool from which the coreset is sampled. To simulate severe, systematic data corruption, we introduce asymmetric label noise. We uniformly sample a subset C ⊂ D train containing 1, 000 instances. For each instance (xi, y i) ∈ C , the ground-truth label yi is deterministically distorted according to the following mapping: 

ynoisy i = ( yclean i + 2) (mod 10) 

The indices of the corrupted subset C are recorded for downstream evaluation of the selection purity. 

Reference Model Pre-training. Prior to the ReSGD sampling loop, a baseline reference model, denoted as θ∗(D), is trained to convergence exclusively on the clean dataset Dholdout . During the subsequent ReSGD iterations, the parameters of θ∗(D) are strictly frozen. This model acts as an oracle to compute the reference loss ℓ(xi, y i; θ∗(D)) and does not undergo further parameter updates. 

Adversarial Probability Initialization and ϵ-Exploration. To stress-test the ReSGD frame-work, we initialize the sampling probability vector m(1) ∈ R10000 in a highly adversarial manner. We randomly select a further subset A ⊂ C , where |A| = 200 . To prevent algorithmic stagnation (where clean samples would never be selected or evaluated if assigned a probability of 0), we introduce an ϵ-exploration constant. K Experimental Details 

We note that different baselines in the literature are evaluated under different training and evaluation protocols (e.g., number of training epochs, data repetition strategies, and evaluation schedules). A naive unification of these protocols—for example, adopting the evaluation setting of baseline A for all methods—can lead to suboptimal or misleading performance for other baselines such as B and C, whose effectiveness is tied to their original selection regimes. Therefore, to ensure a fair and faithful 35 comparison, we follow the evaluation protocol used in each original work when reporting results, rather than enforcing a single unified protocol across all methods. To ensure statistical significance, all experimental results are reported as the mean ± standard deviation across 5 independent runs with different random seeds. All experiments were conducted on a server equipped with four NVIDIA H20 GPUs. The total computational cost for reproducing the complete set of experiments is approximately 800 GPU hours.

K.2 Continual Learning Experiment Details 

We evaluate our data summarization approach for continual learning across four standard benchmarks: Split MNIST [112], Perm MNIST [43], Split CIFAR-10, and Split CIFAR-100. 

Datasets and Augmentations. Following the evaluation protocol established by [8, 104], the dataset splits are constructed as follows: Split MNIST and Split CIFAR-10 are both divided into 5 tasks with 2 classes per task. Split CIFAR-100 is divided into 10 tasks, each containing 10 classes. Perm MNIST consists of 10 tasks, where each task corresponds to a randomly permuted version of the original MNIST images. For the training data formulation, we randomly sample 1,000 images per task for Split MNIST, Split CIFAR-10, and Perm MNIST. For Split CIFAR-100, we utilize the full dataset for each task. Regarding data augmentation, we apply standard normalization to both Split MNIST and Perm MNIST. For the more complex CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets, our augmentation pipeline includes random cropping, random horizontal flipping, and normalization. 

Network Architectures. We employ distinct backbone architectures tailored to the complexity of each dataset: • Split MNIST: A standard CNN architecture (as detailed in section K.1). • Split CIFAR-10: A standard ResNet-18 architecture (as detailed in section K.1). • Split CIFAR-100: A modified ResNet-18 architecture, wherein a Batch Normalization layer is injected into each convolutional block. • Permuted MNIST: A fully connected network consisting of two hidden layers with 100 units each, ReLU activation functions, and dropout regularization with a probability of 0.2

applied to the hidden layers. 

Optimization and Hyperparameters. Our training procedures follow the methodologies of [8, 116, 104]. The optimization process involves a specific loss factor, denoted as α (refer to the setting in [104]). The hyperparameter configurations are specifically tuned for each dataset as follows: • For Split MNIST, Split CIFAR-10, and Perm MNIST, we utilize the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of 5 × 10 −4. Models for these three datasets are trained for 400 epochs. The loss factor α is set to 100 .0 for Split MNIST, 20 .0 for Split CIFAR-10, and 0.15 for Perm MNIST. 37 • For Split CIFAR-100, we employ the SGD optimizer with a smaller batch size of 32 and a higher learning rate of 2 × 10 −2. Limitations. In this work, we introduce a new probabilistic relaxation for the multi-source bilevel data selection problem, which only assigns probability mass to feasible data subsets and thus naturally yields a lossless rounding scheme. In addition, we show that this new probabilistic relaxation admits an effective two-point gradient estimator, which can simultaneously avoid the inverse-Hessian computation required by existing 0 − 1 relaxation and the unbounded score term appearing in prior probabilistic relaxation. Built upon this reducible two-point estimator, we further design a novel stochastic gradient algorithm, termed ReSGD , for the multi-source bilevel data selection problem, and establish a rigorous approximation guarantee for the subset returned by ReSGD under weak submodularity. A limitation of the current analysis is that ReSGD follows the standard random-output scheme in stochastic optimization: it needs to sample τ uniformly from {0, 1, . . . , T − 1} and returns a subset drawn according to the historical prior π(τ ). Although theoretically convenient, in practice, this random-output scheme may be inconvenient, since it requires storing all T historical iterates or recovering the sampled historical iterate after training. Fortunately, recent advances [5, 52, 71] in last-iterate convergence of stochastic gradient methods provide a promising way to remove this random-sampling step, so that one can directly return the subset sampled from the final prior π(T )

while hopefully preserving a comparable approximation guarantee. We leave this last-iterate variant of ReSGD as an important direction for future work. 

Broader Impact. This work mainly focuses on theoretically exploring the multi-source bilevel data selection problem. Therefore, we do not foresee any direct negative societal impact induced by our proposed method. 39
