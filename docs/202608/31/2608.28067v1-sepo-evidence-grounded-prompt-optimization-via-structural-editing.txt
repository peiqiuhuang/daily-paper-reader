Abstract 

Existing API-only prompt optimisers are of-ten described as interpretable , but in practice, this usually means only post-hoc inspectability: each iteration still rewrites the prompt as one opaque string, leaving a trace of full-prompt diffs rather than localisable, machine-readable edits. This paper introduces S EPO (Structural, Evidence-grounded Prompt Optimization), a multi-trajectory prompt optimiser centred on 

edit-effect lineage feedback . Rather than treat-ing each iteration as an isolated whole-prompt rewrite, S EPO locally edits stable, typed units in a two-layer prompt schema, links the tar-get and realised structural operations of each edit to the examples it newly fixes or breaks, and carries this edit–effect record forward to guide later architect calls on the same search branch. This makes prompt optimisation ad-dressable , attributable , and actionable . Across a 14-task held-out suite, S EPO improves over the strongest baseline, GEPA, by 3.1 pp on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and 2.2 pp on Qwen3-8B, reaching 61.9% and 73.3% macro accuracy. SEPO also lies on both the optimisation-time and test-time Pareto frontiers, spending 2.9M optimisation tokens versus 4.1M for GEPA and producing prompts over 5 × shorter. 

1 Introduction 

Prompt optimisation has become a de-facto recipe for adapting frozen LLMs to new tasks without weight updates: an architect LLM proposes a rewrite, the candidate is scored on a training set, and the cycle repeats (Ramnath et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Pryzant et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2026; Yuksekgonul et al., 2025). Despite being commonly described as interpretable , most API-only prompt optimisers still expose the same under-lying interface: each iteration rewrites the prompt

as one opaque string. The resulting trace is there-fore a sequence of full-prompt diffs: it shows that 

the prompt changed, but not where the relevant change occurred, why it was made, or which exam-ples it helped or harmed. This limitation persists whether the rewriter is driven by text-gradient feed-back or by LLM-as-optimiser sampling: the unit of edit remains the entire prompt. A handful of recent structured optimisers begin to challenge this single-string interface. Some ex-pose local edit targets, such as factor-, section-, token-, or tree-level units (Schnabel and Neville, 2024; Liu et al., 2026c; Sharma and Henley, 2026; Jain and Chowdhary, 2025; Kumar et al., 2025); others attribute failures to semantic units or agent contributions (Chen et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025); and reflective or self-evolving optimisers improve search with archives, critique, and synthesis (Agrawal et al., 2026; Agarwal et al., 2025). Existing prompt optimisers typically update a prompt based on failures surfaced in the current arXiv:2608.28067v1 [cs.AI] 28 Aug 2026 round. However, rules added to address those fail-ures can also change the worker’s behaviour on examples that it handled correctly under the parent, thereby introducing new regressions. Without this parent-to-child transition, later iterations may con-flate persistent failures with regressions introduced by the preceding edit, leading to repeated rule ac-cretion or another broad prompt rewrite. This can cause prompt bloat and make effective improve-ments harder to preserve across iterations. In this paper, we propose SEPO , a multi-trajectory prompt optimiser that addresses this problem through edit-effect lineage feedback . Each edit targets a stable typed unit in a two-layer prompt schema; after the candidate is evaluated, S EPO 

links the target and realised operations of the edit to the examples it newly fixes or breaks and reuses this record in later architect calls on the same search branch. To preserve trajectory diversity, S EPO 

combines example-level admission with Lexicase-based parent selection (Spector, 2012), keeping specialists alive for rare training examples. We evaluate S EPO on a 14-task held-out suite using Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B as worker models. We cap optimisation at 2000 met-ric calls per task and seed—one metric call eval-uates μ on one training example, consuming one Worker LLM forward pass. Under this matched budget, S EPO reaches 61.9/73.3% macro accuracy on Llama/Qwen— +3 .1/ + 2 .2 pp over the best of six API-only baselines (GEPA)—while landing on both optimisation- and test-time Pareto frontiers. 

Our contributions are four-fold. (i) We introduce S EPO and its core mechanism, 

edit-effect lineage feedback . This mechanism links the target and realised operations of each local edit to the examples it newly fixes or breaks and reuses this record in subsequent edits on the same search branch, making prompt optimisation addressable ,

attributable , and actionable .

(ii) We provide a controlled API-only compari-son on a 14-task held-out suite covering reasoning (BBH, GSM-Hard), coding (MBPP+), multi-hop question answering (HotpotQA), and multidomain tasks from MMLU-Pro. Under the matched 2000 -metric-call budget, S EPO consistently matches or exceeds six API-only baselines across both worker models, winning at least 11/14 tasks against every baseline. 

(iii) We show that the gains from structured, lineage-aware editing are strongest on procedu-ral, multi-step tasks—most clearly Word Sorting (+18 .9 pp over GEPA on Llama) and MBPP+ (+8 .7 pp on Qwen). 

(iv) We show that SEPO sits on both the optimisation-time and test-time Pareto frontiers, spending 2.9M optimisation tokens and 203k de-ployment tokens per cell—compared with 4.1M and 424k for GEPA—with prompts over 5 ×

shorter than GEPA’s and remaining 6.9 pp above APSF, the only other method on both frontiers. 

2 Related Work 

Monolithic whole-prompt rewriting. A first family rewrites the prompt as an opaque string un-der a scalar validation reward (Ye et al., 2024): APE (Zhou et al., 2022) samples and re-scores in-structions, OPRO (Yang et al., 2024) casts the LM as a numerical-feedback optimiser, GrIPS (Prasad et al., 2023) searches with gradient-free opera-tors, and ProTeGi (Pryzant et al., 2023) intro-duces “textual gradient” beam search, extended by TextGrad (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025) to text-level backpropagation. Lacking component-level addresses, a rewrite that breaks one example yields no localisable change to attribute, and its trace col-lapses to untyped full-prompt diffs. 

Structure-aware factorised optimisers. A sec-ond family exposes components as first-class units: 

scaffold-based methods optimise per-module in-structions and demonstrations over a user-specified program (DSPy+MIPROv2 (Khattab et al., 2024; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024)); fixed-template methods refine each section of a cross-task schema (Modular Prompt Optimisation (Sharma and Henley, 2026)); 

decomposition-based methods use a predefined tax-onomy (Task-Facet (Juneja et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a)) or self-discovered factors (aPSF (Liu et al., 2026c)); and principle-aware variants refine via multi-aspect critique (CriSPO (He et al., 2025)) or generalisable criteria (ZERA (Yi et al., 2025)). Although these factorisations expose local editable units, they typically do not explicitly link each re-alised edit to the examples it newly fixes or breaks, or carry this edit–effect relation forward along the search branch. 

Reflective and evolutionary search with diver-sity. A third family uses natural-language reflec-tion (Shinn et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2026b) and pop-ulation diversity. Yet even when archives or search trees pre-serve multiple candidates, reflection often remains centred on the current candidate’s traces, without linking an edit’s target and realised operations to the examples newly fixed or broken in the parent-to-child transition, or carrying this record forward to subsequent edits on the same search branch. 

3 Method 

SEPO is a multi-trajectory prompt optimiser: each iteration applies one local edit to a structured prompt and records the edit together with its example-level outcomes as an edit-effect lineage state, making the optimisation process auditable and providing reusable feedback for later edits on the same search branch. S EPO rests on three prin-ciples. (P1) Localised edits over stable, address-able prompt units give every edit an explicit target (§3.2). (P2) Edit-effect lineage feedback uses two architect calls, attribution and patch . Both calls share an evidence packet comprising the parent’s preceding edit, the examples that edit newly fixed or broke, and the associated breadcrumb (§3.3). 

(P3) Trajectory-diverse archiving uses example-level admission and Lexicase-based parent selec-tion to determine which candidate branches merit further search (§3.4). Figure 2 and Algorithm 1 detail one iteration.

3.4 Example admission and Lexicase sampling 

To control evaluation cost, S EPO first evaluates each child on the Mtrain probe slot and discards candidates that do not strictly improve over the parent on those probes. 4 Experiments 

Experimental questions. We evaluate S EPO 

through four questions: • Q1: Held-out effectiveness. Under a matched API-only optimisation and deployment protocol, does S EPO produce stronger final prompts than strong prompt optimisers? • Q2: Edit-diagnostic mechanism. Are S EPO ’s gains driven by its edit-aware diagnostic loop, rather than by generic reflection, static factorisa-tion, or unlocalised prompt rewriting? • Q3: Behavioral scope and auditability. Where does S EPO help or fail, and do its edit traces make these outcomes inspectable at evidence, target, operator, and example-level effect? • Q4: Cost-effectiveness. What optimisation-time and deployment-time cost do S EPO ’s gains re-quire, and does S EPO remain competitive on accuracy–cost trade-offs?

5 Conclusion 

We presented S EPO and its core mechanism, edit-effect lineage feedback , which links each local edit’s target and realised operations to the exam-ples it newly fixes or breaks and reuses this record in later architect calls on the same search branch. A two-layer prompt schema provides a stable, ad-dressable implementation. Across 14 tasks and two workers, S EPO beats six API-only baselines (+3 .1/+2 .2 pp over the strongest, GEPA) and lies on both the optimisation- and test-time Pareto fron-tiers, yielding a stronger accuracy–cost trade-off. 

Limitations 

Instruction-space scalability. Our evaluation cov-ers prompt lengths and instruction spaces repre-sentative of existing prompt-optimization work. Whether S EPO ’s efficiency advantage extends to substantially longer prompts or more compositional agentic skills involving multiple steps, tool calls, or state tracking remains open. Task-tailored schemas. By design, S EPO tailors a schema per task, yielding a task-specialised artefact rather than a universal prompt; cross-task transfer is left to future work. Evaluation setting. We eval-uate S EPO only on single-turn, single-call, text-based tasks; its structured, attributable edits may suit broader domains—agentic pipelines and multi-modal prompts—which we leave to future work.

> Step 3. Output the final answer. - If the task asks for a function, output the full Python code block containing the function and the test. - Output the final result on the last line as “ Answer: (X) ”, where X is the return value of the function or the code itself if requested. Example schema (HotpotQA Medium, seed 45, test acc. 68%) Step 1. Parse the question and locate the relevant con-text. - Identify the key entities and constraints in the ques-tion (e.g., specific dates, titles, roles, locations), then scan the provided passages for sentences mention-ing those exact entities.
> Step 2. Verify, link, and answer. - Strictly ground your answer in the provided text; do not use outside knowledge or hallucinate details not present in the context. - Verify entity matches before extracting attributes: if the question asks about Entity A, do not extract facts about Entity B even if they appear in the same passage or share a category. - For multi-hop questions, explicitly trace the chain across passages (e.g., Entity A in passage 1 to Prop-erty B in passage 2); once the answer is reached, stop reasoning immediately and do not repeat the same logical loop.
> Step 3. Output the final answer. - The final line must be exactly “ Answer: (X) ”where X is the concise value with no introductory phrases, explanations, or repetitions of the question.

E.4 Failure-Mode Analysis 

SEPO ’s Word Sorting schema repairs the dominant failure modes (entity hallucination, sort-method loops, output-format collapse) but its effect sat-urates with input length. Word Sorting uses the same 50 -train / held-out-test convention as the other BBH tasks, with a disjoint 200 -example held-out test split. Table 8 bins a 100 -example diagnostic subset of that held-out test split by the number of words to be sorted: short lists ( ≤ 5 words) reach 

79% while long lists ( ≥ 16 words) collapse to 31% .The degradation is monotone and held across ev-ery schema variant we examined. The cause is that the schema’s discipline is instructional , not 

algorithmic : rules such as “copy the words exactly” and “trust the initial sort” raise the probability of a correct single pass but do not give the worker an external scratchpad, so the per-token error of an 

n-word sort still compounds with n. This delin-eates the scope of prompt-only structural editing: it repairs systematic, length-independent failure modes (format, hallucination, verification loops) but cannot substitute for an algorithmic execution mechanism on long inputs.
