Abstract 

Automatic prompt optimization (APO) has been widely employed to adapt large language models without updating their weights, yield-ing promising results. However, existing meth-ods such as GEPA and OPRO assume hun-dreds to thousands of subject-model calls, far more than is practical behind paid, rate-limited APIs. Under tight budgets they fail in two ways: multi-stage pipelines can exhaust the budget and return the seed prompt unchanged, while single-stage methods compare candidates on fixed-size minibatches, regardless of each task’s noise. As a remedy, we introduce B UD -

> GET

APO, a single-stage optimizer for the tight-budget regime. B UDGET APO incorporates (1) a noise-adaptive rule that sizes the evaluation slice to each task’s noise, measured by a short probe; (2) a fixed slice that turns every ac-cept/reject decision into a paired comparison; and (3) a reflective operator that rewrites rea-soning strategy and output format jointly. Ex-tensive results across seven benchmarks and five subject models demonstrate that B UD -

> GET

APO ranks first on every subject and beats every baseline under Holm-corrected paired tests, while returning the seed in 13% of runs at 250 calls against 86% for GEPA. On GPT-OSS-20B, GEPA needs 5 times as many calls to match B UDGET APO’s 100-call score.

2 Related Work 

Prompting shifts model behaviour without parame-ter updates, through few-shot examples (Brown et al., 2020) or elicited reasoning (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022), but the best prompt varies across models, tasks and inputs (Liu et al., 2023b): formatting alone moves accuracy by sev-eral points (Sclar et al., 2024), and single-prompt evaluation can reverse a model ranking (Mizrahi et al., 2024). A line of work therefore automates the search. APE (Zhou et al., 2022) selects among gen-erated candidates; OPRO (Yang et al., 2024) shows the optimizer scored prompts and asks for a better one; GrIPS (Prasad et al., 2023) edits the string; ProTeGi (Pryzant et al., 2023) propagates textual “gradients”; PromptBreeder (Fernando et al., 2023), EvoPrompt (Guo et al., 2024) and EvoPrompt-ing (Chen et al., 2023) evolve prompts; Promp-tAgent (Wang et al., 2024) plans over an error tree. MIPROv2 (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024) extends DSPy (Khattab et al., 2023) with Bayesian opti-mization over instruction and demonstration config-urations, and GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) screens a broad candidate pool on small batches and reranks survivors on larger ones; aPSF (Liu et al., 2026) 2factorizes a prompt into semantic components and updates one at a time. However, all of them were evaluated with hundreds to thousands of subject-model calls; our paper focuses on the regime below that. Cheap evaluation is a cross-cutting concern: successive halving (Li et al., 2018), Bayesian search (Bergstra et al., 2011) and meta-learned Au-toML (Feurer et al., 2022) early-stop unpromis-ing hyper-parameter candidates, while Polo et al. (2024) estimate benchmark scores from small sub-sets and Vivek et al. (2024) from a few anchor examples, trading 10–100 × less cost for estima-tion noise; Perlitz et al. (2024) show how far a benchmark can be subsampled before its rankings move. In APO the tradeoff is sharper, because subset noise interferes with the optimization sig-nal itself. CAPO (Zehle et al., 2025) races candi-dates and TRIPLE (Shi et al., 2024) casts prompt selection as fixed-budget best-arm identification, both allocating evaluations per candidate during search. B UDGET APO instead calibrates one evalu-ation size to each task’s noise before search with a closed-form rule, a design that also suits stacked-instruction tasks (Anand and Chattaraj, 2026; Liu, 2026).

3.1 Noise-Adaptive Slice Sizing 

In this section, we present noise-adaptive slice siz-ing, which eliminates the need for choosing a mini-batch size per task. 

Fixed minibatches and their limitations. 

Single-stage methods score candidates on mini-batches of a fixed size m, whose standard error 

σ/ √m depends on the task’s per-row standard deviation σ. Two limitations follow: 1. on a noisy task comparisons are imprecise (10 SuperGPQA rows, σ ≈ 0.48 , give a standard error near 0.15); 2. on a stable task extra rows buy no precision but cost search depth (Equation 5).

3.2 Reflective Refinement Operator Existing feedback and its limitations. 

OPRO (Yang et al., 2024) shows the optimizer past prompts with their scores, and ProTeGi (Pryzant et al., 2023) derives textual “gradients” from errors. Under a tight budget two limitations matter: 1. a score or an aggregate does not say on what a prompt fails; 2. a rewrite that improves reasoning can break the output format. 

Addressing the limitations by reflective refine-ment. To address them, the operator shows raw failing rows and asks for strategy and format to-gether. 4.1 Setup 

To answer these questions, we evaluate six APO methods with five subject models on seven bench-marks, as detailed below. 

Subject models. The main comparison uses five subject models spanning 8B–120B parame-ters and five families: Gemma-4-31B, Qwen-3.5-27B, GPT-OSS-20B, Nemotron-3-Super-120B and Ministral-8B (endpoints in Appendix C). Gemma-4-31B (Team et al., 2026) is our primary subject, on which the ablations, hyper-parameter sweeps and optimizer swap run; the rewrite and seed-return counts pool all five subjects. Within each subject, all methods share the decoding configuration and the same five-word seed, “You are a helpful assis-tant.” Temperature is 0.6 and top-p is 0.95. The de-fault optimizer is GLM-5-FP8 (Zeng et al., 2026); §4.5 swaps in an alternative. 

Benchmarks. Seven tasks span multi-hop QA, instruction following, graduate-level knowledge and code: HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), Col-lIE (Yao et al., 2024), IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2025), SuperGPQA (Du et al., 2026), a gener-ated instruction-stacking task (labeled StackEval in our tables), MBPP+ (Liu et al., 2023a) and LiveBenchIF (White et al., 2025). We report each task’s normalized score on a percentage scale and average tasks with equal weight. 

Baselines. We compare against five APO methods using their official implementa-tions: GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) (v0.1.1), 

MIPROv2 (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024) (DSPy 3.3.0), 

OPRO (Yang et al., 2024), GrIPS (Prasad et al., 2023), and aPSF (Liu et al., 2026). 

Evaluation protocol. We instantiate the tight-budget regime with a cap of B = 250 subject-model calls per run; every method receives the same cap. The returned prompt is scored on a held-out test pool shared by all methods, outside the budget. Each (subject, method, benchmark) triple is reported over seeds 0, 1 and 2 as mean test score ± sample standard deviation; runs fail-ing a coverage or budget guard were re-run (Ap-pendix C). Significance is assessed by two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm cor-rection (Holm, 1979; Wilcoxon, 1945) over the 5n = 35 (subject, benchmark) pairs, seeds averaged within a cell so each pair contributes one observa-tion. Effect sizes are Cohen’s dz (Cohen, 2013): the mean paired difference divided by its sample standard deviation.

5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduce B UDGET APO, a single-stage prompt optimizer that sizes a fixed evaluation slice to each task’s noise. Under a tight budget, it ranks first on all five subject models, significantly ahead of every baseline, and returns the seed in 13% of runs against 86% for GEPA. On GPT-OSS-20B, GEPA needs 5 times as many calls to match the score B UDGET APO reaches with 100, and one set of defaults serves every task and subject model without per-task tuning. 8Limitations 

Our approach has two main limitations. First, BUDGET APO depends on the capability of the optimizer LLM that reads the failing rows and writes each candidate; the quality of its rewrites bounds what the slice can select, and a less capa-ble optimizer may yield smaller gains (§4.5). Sec-ond, our experiments currently cover text bench-marks only; extending the evaluation to multimodal benchmarks is left for future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

BUDGET APO is a general prompt-optimization method and, like other APO methods, could be used to optimize prompts for harmful or policy-violating objectives, depending on the subject model and usage context. Our study uses public benchmarks and one generated instruction-stacking task, and does not collect new user data or involve human subjects. In practice, responsible use should follow applicable platform policies and safety fil-ters, and any released code or prompts should in-clude usage guidelines that discourage harmful ap-plications.

A.4 Relation to the Implemented Rule 

Equation 2 motivates s ≈ σ2/τ 2 for estimating one fixed prompt’s mean, while Equation 14 con-cerns a particular comparison and depends on its gap and difference variance. C Experimental Setup Details 

Benchmark splits. Table 4 reports the sizes of the training, validation and test pools, denoted 

Rcal , Ropt and Rtest . These symbols denote finite row collections, while R in §3 denotes the target distribution. B UDGET APO takes both the probe (nprobe = 16 ) and the fixed slice from Ropt = V ;OPRO, GrIPS and MIPROv2 sample Rcal , aPSF samples Ropt , and GEPA uses Rcal as its dataset and Ropt as its valset. The designated test pool is scored after optimization and outside the budget. For IFBench, training and validation come from IF-RLVR Train, while testing uses the first 200 released IFBench examples. HotpotQA first sam-ples a run-seed-dependent pool and then partitions it into training, validation and test rows. StackE-val pools use seeded generation with split-specific row IDs, without content-level deduplication across pools, and are shared by all methods. 

Task scores. HotpotQA uses the local token-overlap answer-F1 scorer, with answer extraction from the last Answer: label or the final reply line. CollIE scores whether all requested constraints pass. IFBench, StackEval and LiveBenchIF av-erage the binary checks within each row. SuperG-PQA scores the extracted option letter, and MBPP+ scores whether the generated program passes its tests. Reported percentages are 100 times the mean row score. 

Decoding parameters. All five subject models use temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, and a maximum output length of 2 048 tokens. Top-k is 20 where supported; the Mistral endpoint omits this parame-ter. Every method evaluated on a given subject uses the same decoding configuration. The optimizer model (GLM-5-FP8 or Qwen-3.5-27B) is decoded with temperature 1.0 and no nucleus/top-k filtering, to encourage diverse candidate generation. 

Significance tests. The paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests use the tie-corrected normal approxima-tion, omit zero differences, and apply no continu-ity correction. Differences on the [0 , 1] scale are rounded to 12 decimal places before ranking to remove floating-point tie artifacts. 

Model endpoints. Two subject models, Gemma-4-31B and Qwen-3.5-27B, are served locally via vLLM on 8 ×NPU accelerators with tensor par-allelism. GPT-OSS-20B and Nemotron-3-Super-120B are queried through the NVIDIA API, and Ministral-8B through the Mistral API. The opti-mizer models (GLM-5-FP8, and Qwen-3.5-27B in §4.5) are served locally throughout. Within a sub-ject model, all six methods use the same configured endpoint, decoding settings and seed prompt. The probe-size and lower-clamp sweep of Figure 6(c, d) uses a separate single-GPU deployment of Gemma-4-31B (Appendix L). 

Budget accounting. The budget counts sched-uled subject-model evaluations on search-time rows. It increments by 1 for each (prompt, row) evaluation; transport retries remain part of that eval-uation and are not counted as additional budget units. The initial probe ( nprobe rows, 16 by default) and each candidate evaluation ( s rows, where s is the slice size) count toward the budget. Optimizer-model calls (prompt rewriting) are free for all meth-ods. Test-set evaluation at the end of each run does not count. 

Landing guards. To ensure data quality, we ap-ply two post-hoc guards to each run: (1) Coverage guard : an API failure drops a row from a test eval-uation silently, so a cell in which fewer than 95% of the intended test rows were successfully scored is not written at all and is retried on the next pass. (2) Budget guard : at caps of 250 calls and above, a run that spends less than 80% of the cap (e.g., <200 calls out of 250) is discarded. Below 250 no floor is applied, since a single round can legitimately leave more than 20% unspent: at B = 100 , a 21-row slice with k = 2 stops after 16 + 21 + 42 = 79 

calls, too few remaining for another round. aPSF is exempt from the budget floor. It scores each prompt on min(50 , max(10 , ⌊B/ 10 ⌋)) validation rows (25 at B = 250 ), evaluates four candidates per update step, and stops when fewer than two such evaluations remain, after ten steps, or at a perfect validation score.
