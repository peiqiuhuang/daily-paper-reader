ABSTRACT 

Recent methods for injecting graph structure into LLM prompts include graph soft prompting , in which a graph neural network (GNN) learns graph-specific tokens that capture relational information among entities in textual prompts. A major development in this line of work is G-Retriever, which combines graph soft prompts with a verbalized description of the same graph to strengthen the textual prompt signal. In this paper, we conduct an in-depth analysis of G-Retriever and uncover a pathological behavior we term token collapse . Across three benchmarks, three graph encoders, two LLM families, and both frozen and LoRA-adapted models, G-Retriever’s graph tokens become almost indistinguish-able across inputs. We provide evidence that the same failure is inherited by subsequent methods to G-Retriever, even with multi-token pooling. Under the standard setup, swapping a token with the dataset mean, a token from another ex-ample, or one derived from a corrupted graph produces no resolved answer-level effect in accuracy. Moreover, a learned constant prompt that never accesses the graph matches or surpasses G-Retriever on all three benchmarks. These findings indicate that, rather than conveying graph content as intended, the collapsed to-ken functions primarily as a generic soft prompt. We examine conditions linked to this collapse and propose a two-stage procedure that yields non-collapsed to-kens while maintaining competitive performance in most cases. However, to-ken variability alone does not prove that the model is using graph information: via interventions and decoding probes, we show that depending on the configu-ration, a token can reflect the question, an answer prior, or graph-derived con-tent that the LLM may or may not actually leverage. Overall, our results offer a practical approach to evaluating graph-token alignment rather than inferring it from task accuracy alone. Code to reproduce our experiments is available at 

https://github.com/difra100/Token_Collapse_KGQA .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) store knowledge in their parameters, making information outside or newer than their pre-training data hard to access or update (Lewis et al., 2020). Such external knowledge may be organised as text-attributed graphs (He et al., 2024). We can incorporate this in-formation into an LLM either by textualizing the graphs , i.e., constructing verbalized descriptions of nodes and edges, or through graph soft prompting , i.e., providing a learned representation generated by a graph neural network (GNN). Verbalisation has two limitations: its token cost grows with the amount of graph content included, and serialisation imposes a sequence order on facts that have no intrinsic order. Expressive Graph soft prompting aims to offer an alternative interface for convey-ing relational information without verbalising every node and edge. G-Retriever (He et al., 2024) supplies the same underlying graph via both channels. However, this design introduces an identifi-ability issue: because the graph content is already present in textual form, improvements in answer 1arXiv:2610.31572v1 [cs.LG] 22 Oct 2026 Preprint accuracy alone cannot distinguish whether the model relies on the graph token, the verbalized graph, or both. Furthermore, the training objective does not explicitly enforce that the graph-derived to-ken encodes graph-specific information when the textual channel suffices. Consequently, a token computed from the graph may degenerate into a graph-invariant soft prompt. 3 0 3

G-Retriever interprets its ablations as evidence that the graph encoder and verbalised graph provide comple-mentary graph representations (He et al., 2024). We revisit this interpretation by testing what the graph to-ken contains and whether the LLM uses it. This study evaluates the proposed mechanism by quantifying both the information encoded in graph-derived tokens and the information that the large lan-guage model (LLM) causally exploits during infer-ence. The experimental protocol integrates token-space statistical analyses with fixed-weight causal in-terventions: individual tokens are substituted with (i) the training-set mean token, (ii) a token sampled from a different example, or (iii) a token recomputed from corrupted or different graph inputs. In addition to an-swer accuracy, we assess gold-answer log-likelihood to capture subtler shifts in the model’s output distribu-tion, and we employ decoding-based probes to measure the recoverability of question, answer, and graph attributes from internal representations. Graph-free control conditions preserve the projector architecture and the full training pipeline while ablating the graph input itself. Applying this protocol to G-Retriever uncovers an unanticipated phenomenon that we call token col-lapse , which also recurs in subsequent graph-prompting methods. Specifically, across several graph encoders, benchmark datasets, LLM families, and fine-tuning regimes, the token representations generated by G-Retriever are nearly invariant with respect to the input graph (Figure 1). More-over, substituting these input-dependent projected tokens with a fixed alternative yields no resolved answer-level effect under the default configuration; indeed, a learned constant token that never ob-serves any graph matches or even surpasses the performance of the full model. These results indicate that the collapsed token operates primarily as a soft prompt rather than as a carrier of graph-specific information. Although likelihood-based analyses show that perturbing the token can modulate the output distribution, such sensitivity alone is insufficient to demonstrate input- or graph-conditioned utilization. We associate collapse with two factors: a projector that suppresses variation in the encoder repre-sentations and an answer objective that does not require this variation to be preserved. Our ablations implicate both factors, although they do not isolate either as the sole cause. We address them with a two-stage pipeline that first aligns and freezes the graph encoder, then trains separate graph-token and prompt-token pathways. The resulting tokens no longer collapse, but non-collapse alone guar-antees neither graph use nor improved accuracy. Depending on the benchmark and training con-figuration, they may encode the question, an answer prior, graph information made redundant by the text, or graph information that the LLM uses to answer. This distinction matters because task improvements may otherwise be attributed to graph–language alignment even when they arise from the verbalised graph or a graph-independent soft prompt. By separating token variation, graph con-tent, and downstream use, our protocol makes alignment empirically testable and provides concrete criteria for developing graph–language models that preserve and use task-relevant graph information beyond what is already supplied by the textual channel. 

Contributions. We identify token collapse across G-Retriever benchmarks, graph encoders, LLM families, and tuning regimes, and find the same failure in recent graph-prompting methods. Using matched graph-free controls and fixed-weight interventions, we show that the collapsed token acts primarily as a soft prompt rather than as a graph representation used to answer. We associate collapse with the projector and end-to-end answer training, and propose a two-stage pipeline that mitigates it across the tested settings. Finally, we show that a non-collapsed token may encode question or 

answer priors , unused graph information , or graph information that changes the answer. Together, 2Preprint these results provide a practical protocol for testing graph-token alignment rather than inferring it from task accuracy. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Graph soft prompting. Soft prompting conditions an LLM with learned continuous vectors (Lester et al., 2021; Li & Liang, 2021); graph soft prompting instead projects graph-encoder outputs into one or more such tokens. GraphToken (Fatemi et al., 2024), GraphLLM (Chai et al., 2026), GNP (Tian et al., 2024), GraphGPT (Tang et al., 2024), and G-Retriever (He et al., 2024) differ in their en-coders, token counts, retrieval, and LLM adaptation. In this paper, we test whether G-Retriever and subsequent methods preserve and use graph-specific information in their projected tokens. 

Evaluating graph–language models. Recent work questions whether existing objectives and benchmarks require graph–language integration. Zhang et al. (2026) show that autoregressive train-ing can favour a text-dominant solution, while Petkar et al. (2026) find that single-modality and raw soft-prompt baselines remain competitive and introduce CLEGR to test compositional graph–text reasoning. We complement these studies by auditing the graph token– LLM interface of graph soft prompting. Extended related work discussion is in Appendix S. 

3 METHOD 

3.1 PRELIMINARIES : GRAPH SOFT PROMPTING IN G-R ETRIEVER 

A textual graph G = ( V, E, {xv }v∈V , {xe}e∈E ) associates every node v with a text attribute xv and every edge e with a text attribute xe. Each example i consists of a graph, a question qi, and an answer yi.

Retrieval. Node, edge, and question texts are embedded by a frozen sentence encoder LM( ·) into 

Rd (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). The respective representations are zv = LM( xv ), ze = LM( xe),and qi = LM( qi).When the complete graph exceeds the LLM’s context length or a predefined budget, only a relevant subgraph can be supplied to the model. Following G-Retriever, similarities between qi and the node and edge embeddings define retrieval relevance, and a prize-collecting Steiner tree is used to select a compact, connected subgraph (Bienstock et al., 1993). We denote the resulting question-specific subgraph by Gi. When the complete graph fits within the budget, no retrieval is needed and Gi = G.

Graph token. A graph encoder fϕ processes Gi using node features zv , edge features ze, and optionally the question representation qi. G-Retriever uses a graph transformer by default (Shi et al., 2020); we also evaluate GAT (Veliˇ ckovi´ c et al., 2018) and GCN (Kipf & Welling, 2017). The node representations are mean-pooled into hi ∈ Rdg , and a projector Pψ maps the result into the LLM embedding space: 

ti = Pψ (hi) = W2σ(W1hi + b1) + b2 ∈ Rdℓ , (1) where σ is the logistic sigmoid and the projector has hidden width dp. We refer to ti as the graph token .

τ (Gi), and truncated to a budget of Lmax tokens. In the default frozen-LLM setting, the model receives 

[ ebos ; ti; Emb( τ (Gi)); Emb( qi) ] .

Figure 2: Two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 aligns the question-conditioned graph encoder. Stage 2 freezes it and trains separate graph-token and prompt-token pathways. Orange modules are trained; blue modules are frozen. 3.2 THE GRAPH TOKEN COLLAPSE PHENOMENON 

Task accuracy conflates three distinct questions: Does the token vary across inputs? Does the LLM use the graph token? What information does it contain? We address them with complementary mea-surements of token geometry, fixed-weight interventions, gold-answer likelihood, and decodability. 

Does the token vary across inputs? We measure inter-sample cosine, the input-specific residual relative to the mean token, and token norm relative to ordinary word embeddings. Collapse appears as cosine near one and a small residual ratio. We also compute inter-sample cosine before projection, on the pooled encoder representations hi, to determine where collapse arises. Formal definitions are given in Appendix D. 

Does the LLM use graph-specific token variation? At fixed weights, REAL uses the original to-ken, MEAN replaces it with the training-set mean, and SWAP uses another example’s token. Our main graph-specific intervention, GSWAP , recomputes the token from another graph while preserv-ing the original question conditioning. NODE , EDGE , and REL corrupt individual graph components, while NOTXT removes the verbalised graph. Retrained no-entity and no-question variants isolate the information available during training. Because accuracy observes only changes in the decoded answer, we also measure the paired change in gold-answer negative log-likelihood under teacher forcing. This detects smaller changes in the output distribution. Full details on our interventions are in Appendix D. 

What does the token contain? Recoverable content and downstream use are not equivalent: a token may contain information that the LLM ignores. This matters for our pipeline because Stage 1 is conditioned on the question and trained with an answer-related target. A non-collapsed token may therefore encode the question , an answer prediction or prior, the graph , or a mixture . The answer can be a supervision target rather than an input, so it cannot be removed through a fixed-weight intervention; appendix Table 8 maps the information retained by the remaining conditions. We complement the interventions with a post-hoc probe that maps stored tokens to frozen embed-dings of the question, answer, or verbalised graph. The map is fitted on half of the test examples; on the held-out half, we report the median rank of each example’s correct target among all candi-dates. A constant token provides the control rank. Estimation details and limitations are given in Appendix D. 

Graph-free controls. CONST passes a learned graph-independent vector through G-Retriever’s projector, while MLPQ replaces the graph encoder with an MLP over the question. PT is the raw prompt-tuning baseline, the same as that used by He et al. (2024), and does not use the projector. These controls distinguish the contribution of graph input from that of token parameterisation. 4Preprint 3.3 PREVENTING COLLAPSE : A TWO -STAGE PIPELINE 

Our ablations associate collapse with two factors: a Sigmoid projector that suppresses encoder vari-ation and end-to-end answer training that does not require this variation to be preserved. We address both while retaining G-Retriever’s data, prompt, answer loss, and optimisation setup (Figure 2). 

Stage 1: preserve input-specific variation. Without the LLM, we train the graph encoder with an InfoNCE objective (van den Oord et al., 2019). Its pooled representation hi is aligned with a frozen embedding of a sample-specific target and contrasted against negative targets. The question conditions each node through xv = zv + Wq qi, with Wq initialised at zero. We use the answer as the target, or the verbalized graph, when the answer space is too small to distinguish samples (e.g., answers are Yes/No). The encoder is then frozen. 

Stage 2: separate graph representation from prompting. Using the original answer loss, we jointly train a GELU projector over the frozen encoder and a separate graph-independent prompt token. Freezing protects the variation learned in Stage 1, while the second pathway gives the shared prompting function its own token rather than forcing it to compete with input-specific information. The full objective, targets, and design ablations are reported in Appendix N. 

4 EXPERIMENTS 

We investigate four questions. RQ1: Do graph tokens collapse, and how general is this behaviour? 

RQ2: What role does the collapsed token play when the same graph is also verbalised in the prompt? 

RQ3: What is collapse associated with, and can it be prevented without sacrificing task perfor-mance? RQ4: What do non-collapsed tokens encode, and which of that information does the LLM use? 4.1 SETUP 

Benchmarks. We use the three GraphQA benchmarks introduced by He et al. (2024): 

ExplaGraphs , SceneGraphs , and WebQSP . Following their official splits, retrieval proce-dures, and evaluators, we report accuracy on ExplaGraphs and SceneGraphs and Hit@1 on 

WebQSP . Dataset statistics and retrieval settings are in Appendix B. 

Models. We reproduce G-Retriever with its default graph transformer (GT) and frozen Llama-2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023). Robustness experiments use GAT, GCN, Qwen2.5-3B (Qwen Team, 2025), and LoRA adaptation. Architecture, optimisation, and pipeline settings are in Appendix A. 

Evaluation stability. Re-evaluating identical WebQSP weights in a separate process changes Hit@1 by 1.04 points. We therefore require an answer-level difference larger than 2 points with p < .05 . Gold-answer likelihood is more stable; we require |∆NLL | > 0.03 and a bootstrap interval excluding the replicate floor. Calibration results are in Appendix D. 

Statistical testing. Accuracy and Hit@1 comparisons are paired with the exact McNemar test (Mc-Nemar, 1947). We denote p < . 10 , .05 , .01 , and .001 by †, *, ** , and *** , respectively; unmarked comparisons have p ≥ .10 . Reported p-values are unadjusted, so claims rely on the effect-size criteria above and consistent evidence rather than isolated significant cells. 4.2 RQ1: D O GRAPH TOKENS COLLAPSE , AND HOW GENERAL IS COLLAPSE ?

Collapse occurs in every tested configuration. Token cosine ranges from 0.985 to 0.998 , while the input-specific residual is only 4–12% of the shared mean, whose norm is 22–97 times that of an ordinary word embedding (Appendix E). 4.4 RQ3: W HAT IS COLLAPSE ASSOCIATED WITH , AND CAN IT BE PREVENTED ?

Factors associated with collapse. Table 3 identifies two associations. With the aligned Stage 1 encoder frozen, its representations are highly distinct (cosine 0.024 ), yet the Sigmoid projector produces nearly identical tokens before and after training ( 0.9870 and 0.9918 ). GELU instead yields cosine 0.0910 before training and 0.3229 afterwards. Untrained-projector experiments reproduce this contrast on random inputs (Appendix H), linking the Sigmoid parameterisation to suppression of encoder variation. Changing the projector alone is insufficient: under end-to-end answer training, GELU still pro-duces highly similar tokens (cosine 0.9139 ). Conversely, combining the aligned frozen encoder with GELU prevents collapse, but the single-token model falls to approximately majority-class per-formance ( 57 .76 versus 58 .8). Thus, both factors are associated with collapse, but this experiment does not identify either as the sole cause. The cross-method audit supports this qualification (Ap-pendix F). GRAG (Hu et al., 2025) and GRETPool (Grover et al., 2026) inherit the same Sigmoid projector and also collapse, while GraSP (Wang et al., 2026) collapses despite using the hyperbolic tangent.

The evidence therefore establishes recoverable graph information, not downstream use. Together, these results answer RQ4 : preventing collapse still does not uniquely determine what a token represents or whether the LLM uses it. Depending on the benchmark and training inputs, a non-collapsed token may encode a question or answer prior, unused graph information, or graph information that changes the answer. 

5 DISCUSSION 

What standard evaluation misses. Task accuracy cannot isolate graph-token use, while raw prompt tuning changes the token parameterisation and no-text retraining changes the learning problem. A minimal audit should combine token geometry, a graph-free control using the same projector, and fixed-weight MEAN , SWAP , and GSWAP interventions. The controlled WebQSP audit in Appendix F finds collapse with query-conditioned and multi-token approaches, extending the phenomenon be-yond G-Retriever up to most recent graph soft prompting methods. 

Graph use and accuracy are different outcomes. Our pipeline can make answers depend on graph-specific token variation without improving accuracy. The textual channel may be easier to exploit, while continuous graph tokens may be lossy or difficult for the LLM to interpret. The Qwen2.5-3B results show that this trade-off is not inevitable; alignment claims should therefore report graph use and performance separately. 

Limitations and future directions. Our detailed analysis covers G-Retriever on three question-answering benchmarks; the cross-method audit is limited to WebQSP , runnable implementations, and controlled variants using G-Retriever’s retrieved subgraphs. The mechanism evidence is asso-ciative and does not isolate the projector or end-to-end training as a sole cause. The audited recipe trains from scratch with answer cross-entropy; collapse may differ with pre-trained encoders or explicit contrastive, reconstructive, or counterfactual objectives (Zhang et al., 9Preprint 2026; Xu et al., 2025). Stage 1 explores one alternative, but its conditioning and targets can intro-duce priors. G SWAP tests graph–question pairing rather than fact-level reasoning, and decodability measures recoverability rather than use. Our pipeline is therefore a diagnostic proof of concept. Future work should preserve task-relevant graph information and verify that the LLM uses it. 

6 CONCLUSION 

We uncover token collapse in G-Retriever across benchmarks, graph encoders, LLM families, and tuning regimes, and find the same geometry in controlled tests of later single- and multi-token in-terfaces. Despite competitive task scores, the projected tokens are nearly identical across inputs. We also find that graph-free controls and fixed-weight interventions match the performance of the original G-retriever model. These findings show that strong task performance can conceal a graph-token interface whose principal contribution is a graph-independent soft prompt, while the ver-balised graph provides the dominant graph-information channel. Our two-stage pipeline mitigates this collapse, but also demonstrates that distinct tokens do not guarantee graph use or improved accuracy. Token variation, recoverable graph information, downstream use, and task performance must therefore be evaluated separately. Our protocol provides a practical way to distinguish these outcomes and turns alignment from an architectural assumption into an empirical claim that will foster the development of future graph–language models. A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

G-Retriever reproduction. We use the authors’ public implementation at a pinned commit. For the baseline reproduction, our only modification is explicit device placement, allowing each run to fit on a single GPU. The frozen sentence encoder is all-roberta-large-v1 with output width d = 1024 . The default graph encoder is a four-layer, four-head graph transformer with pooled width dg = 1024 . Its representation is mapped to the LLM embedding space by a two-layer projector with hidden width 

dp = 2048 . The default LLM is frozen Llama-2-7B, whose embedding width is dℓ = 4096 . The verbalised graph is truncated to Lmax = 512 tokens, and generation is limited to 32 tokens. Optimisation follows the authors’ defaults: AdamW with learning rate 10 −5, weight decay 0.05 ,and at most 10 epochs, with early stopping after two epochs without validation improvement. Every model in this paper is trained on a single 80 GB GPU. 

Stage 1. InfoNCE with temperature 0.07 against a memory bank of about 2,000 targets, with no LLM loaded. Trainable: 20,994,048 parameters in the encoder and 1,049,600 in the question-conditioning layer Wq .

Stage 2. The Stage-1 encoder and Wq parameters are frozen, while their running normalisation statistics remain in training mode. The projector’s Sigmoid is replaced by GELU, and a learned constant of width dg passes through its own Linear → Sigmoid → Linear projector to produce the prompt token. The two projector pathways are initialised and optimised jointly; checkpoint-derived trainable parameter counts are reported in Table 64. Interventions modify only the graph token, never the prompt token. 

Controls and parameter counts. Table 64 reports checkpoint-derived trainable parameter counts for the evaluated models. Stage 1 separately trains 22 ,043 ,648 parameters; because the two stages are trained sequentially, we do not report their sum as a simultaneous trainable count. Accuracy does not track capacity: CONST improves over PT by 19 points, while our larger Stage-2 model scores 1.35 points below CONST. Since PT and CONST differ in both parameterisation and capacity, their gap should not be attributed to the projector alone. The controlled graph-input comparison is instead between CONST and G-Retriever, which share the projector parameterisation.

F BEYOND G-R ETRIEVER : TOKEN COLLAPSE IN SUBSEQUENT GRAPH -PROMPTING METHODS 

Motivation and scope. We test whether collapse is confined to G-Retriever or recurs in later graph-prompting methods. We consider methods evaluated on WebQSP that construct continuous prompts from graphs and have runnable public implementations. These criteria retain GRAG (Hu et al., 2025), GRETPool (Grover et al., 2026), and GraSP (Wang et al., 2026). We exclude Graph2Token (Wang et al., 2025), which targets molecular graphs, and GraphAdapter (Li et al., 2023), which adapts vision–language models. Align-GRAG (Xu et al., 2025) could not be repro-duced because its anonymous repository was no longer available. 

Controlled setup. All methods use the 1,628-example WebQSP test split and the PCST subgraphs retrieved for G-Retriever. Holding retrieval fixed prevents graph size and relevance from explaining differences in token geometry, but makes GRAG-PCST and GraSP-PCST controlled adaptations rather than complete reproductions of the published systems. GRAG-PCST retains the released query-conditioned GAT, projector, and LoRA-tuned Llama configuration. GRETPool uses its re-ported TopK (Gao & Ji, 2019) and SAG (Lee et al., 2019) ratios of 0.44 and DiffPool (Ying et al., 2018) budgets of 8 and 100 tokens. GraSP-PCST uses the released S UBGRAPH GNN (Alsentzer et al., 2020) and G RAPH 2P REFIX with a local Llama model, producing one token per retained node. Diagnostics across cardinalities. For inter-sample comparison, we mean-pool each example’s K

prompt tokens and compute the geometry statistics of Appendix D. This pooling is used only for measurement and does not alter inference. For K > 1, we also report the mean pairwise cosine among tokens from the same graph. All interventions preserve cardinality: donor examples have the same K, and node randomisation and edge rewiring preserve node and edge counts. 

Results. Collapse occurs in all six configurations and every tested cardinality: inter-sample co-sine is 0.998 –1.000 , and the multi-token methods are also internally almost collinear. GRAG and GRETPool share G-Retriever’s Linear–Sigmoid–Linear projector, so their results show that collapse persists under alternative encoders and pooling operators without changing this bottleneck. GraSP provides the independent case: despite using a Linear–Tanh–Linear projector and one token per re-tained node, it reaches inter-sample cosine 0.9978 , within-example cosine 0.9995 , and residual ratio 

0.039 . Its smaller norm ratio ( 4.27 ) shows that collapse need not involve the unusually large token norms observed with the Sigmoid projectors. Thus, collapse can persist with multiple tokens and with either Sigmoid or Tanh projectors. H MECHANISM DIAGNOSTICS 

Table 13 supports the first association in Section 4.4. An untrained projector, at its default initialisa-tion, is applied to 500 random inputs of fixed norm, and the tokens it produces are almost identical to each other unless the input norm is two orders of magnitude larger than anything a trained encoder produces. Table 13: Untrained projector (Equation 1, default initialisation) applied to 500 random inputs of fixed norm. Collapse precedes training and is a property of the parameterisation.             

> Input norm Token norm Token cosine Residual ratio 118.4 0.9999 0.009 10 18.4 0.9919 0.090 100 21.4 0.7346 0.600

Why Sigmoid can create a shared direction. Let x = W1hi + b1. If these preactivations are near zero, then σ(x) ≈ 12 1 + 14 x, and the projector is approximately 

Pψ (hi) ≈ 12 W21 + 14 W2b1 + b2 + 14 W2W1hi.

The first three terms are input-independent, while the factor 14 attenuates input-specific variation. This provides one possible explanation for the shared direction produced by the untrained projec-tor. It is not a complete explanation: GraSP remains collapsed with a zero-centred Tanh projector (Appendix F), showing that Sigmoid is neither necessary nor the sole cause. The approximation therefore motivates a mechanism consistent with Table 13, rather than establish-ing that the projector operates entirely in this local regime.

K COMPLETE GRAPH -FREE CONTROLS 

Table 64 gives the controls in full. All four models share G-Retriever’s prompt, answer loss, op-timiser and training schedule. Must the two tokens be trained together? If the split alone were what matters, adding the prompt token to a converged projector should work as well as training both from the start. R THE VERBALISED GRAPH IS NOT ENOUGH 

In this section, we bring some qualitative evidence that LLMs generally struggle to deal with ver-balized graphs only. ExplaGraphs is the clean test, because its graph is not retrieved: it is the human-annotated explanation graph of the stance, given in full, so an error cannot be blamed on missing information. We give a frozen Llama-2-7b-chat 1 the complete graph as a list of triples in-side G-Retriever’s prompt, zero-shot, and score it with the benchmark’s evaluator on all 554 test questions. The majority class is 58.8%. The arguments alone give 61.0%, and adding the complete explanation graph gives 64.3%, with 198 of 554 answers still wrong. The graph fixes 68 questions and breaks 50, so verbalising it misleads the model about as often as it helps. Part of this is a bias towards “support”, which accounts for 451 of 554 answers and for 161 of the 198 errors, but not all of it: Figure 7 shows an error in which the graph states the opposition explicitly.

S EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Graph soft prompting. A growing line of work connects graph encoders to frozen or adapted LLMs by projecting graph representations into continuous tokens. GraphToken (Fatemi et al., 2024) learns 

> 1The chat model is used here because the base Llama-2-7b does not answer zero-shot with this prompt; every other experiment in the paper uses the base model, as G-Retriever does.

53 Preprint an encoding function that emits soft tokens for a frozen LLM on synthetic graph-reasoning tasks, while GraphLLM (Chai et al., 2026) targets graph reasoning with a tuned decoder and GNP (Tian et al., 2024) adds a cross-modality pooling module for multiple-choice knowledge-graph questions. Among these, GNP and G-Retriever (He et al., 2024) are the only ones that retrieve a question-specific subgraph before encoding; the others operate on the full graph or on synthetic inputs. G-Retriever additionally verbalises the retrieved subgraph in the prompt, creating a redundant con-figuration that is the focus of our audit. GraphGPT (Tang et al., 2024) combines a text-grounded structural encoder with a lightweight graph–text alignment projector and dual-stage instruction tun-ing. These systems share the interface we study: a pooled graph representation, a trained projector, and one or a few tokens prepended to the prompt. 

Multi-token and pooling-based methods. Very few works have explored multi-token methods for graph-to-LLM systems. The single-token bottleneck created by mean pooling over a subgraph has only recently been identified as a structural limitation. Grover et al. (2026) argue that aggressive mean pooling compresses entire graph substructures into a single token, creating a severe informa-tion bottleneck, and propose multi-token pooling operators to increase the bandwidth of the graph-to-LLM interface. Their work also reports that pooling methods are unstable under soft prompt tuning with a frozen LLM and require LoRA fine-tuning to stabilise. This is complementary to our diagnosis: we study a single-token approach and show that its collapse is associated with both the projector parameterisation and end-to-end answer training. 

Graph–language alignment. Alignment-based methods add an auxiliary objective intended to bring graph representations closer to language representations. Align-GRAG (Xu et al., 2025) is closest to our Stage 1: it contrastively aligns a pooled graph representation with a textual target using KL divergence and contrastive losses. G-Retriever (He et al., 2024) can also be viewed as an implicit alignment method, since its projector is trained to map graph representations into the LLM’s embedding space under the answer loss. Our use of contrastive pretraining is similar to Align-GRAG, but our question is different. Similarity between graph and text spaces does not es-tablish that different graphs produce different tokens after projection, nor that the LLM uses their graph-specific content. We therefore complement representation alignment with measurements of token variation and fixed-weight interventions on the trained model. 

Why graph use is not guaranteed. Two recent works already point out our diagnosis from dif-ferent perspectives. Zhang et al. (2026) prove that the autoregressive language objective of graph-tokenizing LLMs decomposes into a textual dependency term and a graph–text alignment term, and that current methods optimize only the former explicitly. Graph–text alignment is therefore implicit: the training objective does not reward the model for using graph information, and a text-dominant solution can be optimal under the loss. Petkar et al. (2026) show empirically that ex-isting graph–language benchmarks can be matched by graph-only or language-only baselines, and introduce CLEGR to require compositional reasoning over structure and text; even there, raw soft-prompted LLMs remain competitive with GNN-backed models. Together, these results establish that neither the training objective nor the benchmark forces a model to use graph information. Our work is complementary in level rather than in kind: we study the interface itself and show that a graph token can collapse to a near-constant vector even when the encoder representations are distinct, and that preventing collapse does not guarantee that the LLM uses the token’s graph-specific content. This is what motivates the diagnostic protocol we introduce. 

Retrieval for graph-grounded generation. G-Retriever (He et al., 2024) formulates subgraph retrieval as a prize-collecting Steiner tree problem (Bienstock et al., 1993) and is the reference implementation we study. GRAG (Hu et al., 2025) retrieves subgraphs for generation with a different scoring rule, emphasising the importance of subgraph structure and topology. GraSP (Wang et al., 2026) encodes subgraphs into soft prompts using GNNs for multi-hop KBQA and combines this with a two-stage LLM reasoning procedure. GNN-RAG (Mavromatis & Karypis, 2024) uses a GNN to retrieve reasoning paths that are then verbalised, rather than projecting them as soft tokens. Retrieval quality is orthogonal to our question: our interventions keep the retrieved subgraph fixed and change only the token computed from it. 

Parameter-efficient tuning and continuous prompting. Fine-tuning an LLM can be done in sev-eral ways. Full-weight fine-tuning updates all parameters. Parameter-efficient methods update a small subset or add new parameters, and include LoRA (Hu et al., 2021), which learns low-rank up-dates to existing weights, and continuous prompting methods. Continuous prompting conditions a 54 Preprint frozen language model with learned continuous vectors. This family includes prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021), which learns input-independent embeddings, and prefix-tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), which learns continuous prefixes made available across the model’s layers. A graph token is a form of continuous prompting, but with a different intended role: it is computed from each input graph and is therefore expected to preserve input-specific information, unlike standard prompt tun-ing where the prompt is shared across all inputs. Our results connect these settings by showing that Graph Soft prompting, as it is, collapses into a nearly input-independent vector and consequently behaves like a conventional soft prompt.
