ABSTRACT 

On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own generated responses using token-level teacher supervision. However, uniform weighting overlooks differences in token learning value, while existing weighting methods rely on predefined map-pings from prediction signals to token weights. These mappings are not learned from the effectiveness of the resulting student updates, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving learning needs. In this paper, we propose MetaOPD , a bilevel optimization framework that jointly learns the student model and a lightweight token-weighting network. The inner objective updates the student through weighted OPD, while the outer objective optimizes the weighting network using validation loss on reference solutions after a virtual student update. Differentiating through this update connects weighting decisions to their effects on post-update perfor-mance, allowing the mapping from prediction signals to token weights to evolve alongside the student. Experiments on six mathematical reasoning and three out-of-domain datasets, covering two student scales and seven baselines, demonstrate the effectiveness of MetaOPD, with Avg@8/Pass@8 gains over OPD of 1.99/5.97 percentage points for the 0.6B student and 2.25/6.41 points for the 1.7B student. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Off-policy distillation trains a student on teacher-generated sequences, which can lead to a distribution mismatch between training and inference (Lin et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2024). To mitigate this issue, on-policy distillation (OPD) trains the student on its own generated trajectories, with a frozen teacher providing dense token-level supervision. OPD typically employs a mode-seeking reverse-KL objective that encourages the student to capture the teacher’s high-probability behaviors (Wen et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024). However, experiments by (Li et al., 2026b) show that tokens differ substantially in their learning value, a distinction that standard OPD’s uniform weighting fails to capture. Recent approaches address this limitation by allocating token-level weights through static proxy rules .For example, EOPD (Jin et al., 2026) uses predictive entropy, while TIP (Xu et al., 2026b) leverages teacher–student disagreement. These methods provide useful signals for allocating supervision based on predictive uncertainty or differences between model predictions. However, their mappings from proxy signals to token weights are predefined rather than adapted to the student’s actual learning gains. As training progresses, the student’s capabilities and generated contexts evolve, and the learning benefit associated with the same proxy signal may change accordingly. Thus, even when token weights vary across inputs, a fixed weighting rule may not transfer effectively across training stages or task contexts. The central challenge is to learn a weighting rule that adapts supervision to the student’s evolving learning needs through feedback from the resulting updates. 

To examine whether different token weighting strategies translate into actual learning gains, we conduct a motivating experiment. As illustrated in Figure 1(a), we compare assigning weights through predefined proxies with learning weights. Figure 1(b) reports validation loss reductions relative to Uniform OPD, with larger values indicating greater gains. These results suggest that, in this setting, predefined rules do not fully exploit the potential benefits of token weighting, whereas the student’s update performance provides valuable feedback for weight allocation. The key is therefore not only to distinguish tokens using prediction signals, but also to adapt the signal-to-weight mapping based on the effectiveness of the resulting updates. This leads to our central question: How can we use the student’s post-update performance to learn a token weighting rule that translates prediction signals into effective supervision? 

In this paper, we take a step forward by proposing MetaOPD , an on-policy distillation framework that jointly optimizes the student model and the token-weighting rule. Specifically, we introduce a lightweight token-weighting network that maps prediction signals to token weights. The student and the weighting network perform two coupled learning tasks: the student improves through weighted teacher supervision , while the weighting network learns to allocate supervision based on the effectiveness of student updates . To achieve this, we formulate their joint learning as a bilevel optimization problem. At the inner level, we update the student on its own generated responses using an OPD loss weighted by the network’s outputs. At the outer level, we evaluate the student’s loss on validation reference solutions after a virtual update and use this loss to optimize the weighting network. Differentiating through the virtual update allows the student’s post-update performance to guide token weight allocation, enabling the network to learn which allocations lead to greater subsequent improvements. Alternating between the two levels allows the student and the weighting rule to evolve together, replacing predefined proxy weighting with adaptive supervision allocation driven by update outcomes. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify a gap between static proxy weighting and actual learning gains in OPD, and empirically motivate learning token weights from post-update performance feedback. • We propose MetaOPD, a bilevel framework that jointly optimizes the student through weighted OPD and a lightweight token-weighting network through update validation loss. • We conduct extensive experiments across six reasoning and three out-of-domain datasets on two student scales against seven baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of our MetaOPD. 

2 RELATED WORK 

On-policy distillation (OPD). On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student to match teacher predic-tions on student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024). Compared with distillation on fixed data or teacher-generated sequences (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016), it reduces training–inference mismatch by providing teacher feedback at prefixes the student actually visits. One line of work improves OPD through the design of distillation objectives and optimization strategies (Gu et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026). Another focuses on selecting training prompts, improving rollout trajectories, and refining teacher supervision (Li et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2Preprint 2026a; Qu et al., 2026). Since tokens differ in learning value, assigning different weights to tokens within the OPD loss offers another way to improve OPD. 

Static proxy weighting OPD. Static proxy weighting OPD methods start from the observation that dense teacher feedback is not equally useful at every token. Existing approaches use signals such as entropy and teacher–student disagreement to identify informative tokens and emphasize their supervision through token selection, loss adjustment, or reweighting (Jin et al., 2026; Ko et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c). Another line accounts for changes in supervision reliability along a trajectory, using accumulated prefix discrepancy or token position to guide weighting (Xie et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). However, these methods use predefined mappings from proxy signals to supervision allocation, without learning from post-update feedback how those mappings should adapt to the student’s evolving learning needs. 

Bilevel Optimization. Bilevel optimization provides a framework for learning example or token weights using feedback on the updated model. Early meta-reweighting methods used this structure to learn example importance under class imbalance and label noise (Ren et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2019). Recent studies have extended this principle to language-model training, using outer-loop feedback to replace fixed weighting rules with update-aware supervision (Hu et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a). Across these settings, a common view emerges: the value of a training signal is not fixed, but depends on the model update it induces and the reference objective used to assess that update. MetaOPD applies this principle to token weighting within the OPD loss on student-generated trajectories.

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

5 CONCLUSION 

On-policy distillation provides dense teacher feedback on student rollouts, but local uncertainty or disagreement alone does not determine whether assigning a token a higher weight within the OPD loss will improve student learning. We presented MetaOPD, a one-step bilevel framework that learns to allocate token-level teacher supervision using the student’s validation loss after a virtual distillation update. By differentiating this loss on reference solutions through the virtual update, MetaOPD trains a shared weighting network whose token weights guide the student’s actual OPD update on its own rollouts. Across six mathematical-reasoning benchmarks and two Qwen3 student scales, MetaOPD achieves the highest six-benchmark averages for Avg@8 and Pass@8 among the compared methods. Ablations and one-step update comparisons support learning token weights within the OPD loss using feedback on the updated student. Extending these findings to broader models and tasks, relating local update quality to long-horizon learning, and reducing meta-update cost remain open directions. 9Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

Both the virtual and actual student updates evaluate the weighted OPD loss using the global valid-token count NB from the complete 32-example optimizer minibatch. The numerator is accumulated across all local microbatches and distributed ranks before division by NB ; the implementation does not average independently normalized microbatch losses. Response-level weight normalization in Equation (4) is applied before this global reduction. To compute validation loss, we similarly sum token losses on reference solutions over the micro-batches and participating student ranks, then divide by the global valid reference-solution token count Nref . Because completion lengths differ, NB and Nref are generally different even though both global example batches contain 32 examples. The inner gradient is globally reduced before norm clipping and the virtual optimizer map; the outer gradient is globally reduced before the optimizer VJPs. Clipping each rank’s partial gradient would define a different virtual update. B.4 VIRTUAL STATE AND LEARNING -R ATE CONVENTION

E OUT -OF -D OMAIN GENERALIZATION 

We evaluate the Qwen3-1.7B-Base checkpoints from the MATH main experiment on GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023), MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), and AlpacaEval 2.0 (Dubois et al., 2024), without additional training on these tasks. Distillation methods use the Qwen3-8B teacher. All entries in Table 2 are obtained from local evaluation under a common protocol for each benchmark; Base Model denotes Qwen3-1.7B-Base before MATH training. 

Evaluation protocol. Across these benchmarks, we use temperature 1.0, top-p 0.8, and a maximum response length of 8192 tokens, matching the underlying decoding settings in Table 5. The number of responses and evaluation metric are benchmark-specific. GPQA-Diamond contains 198 questions, for which we sample eight responses per question. Avg@8 measures average response accuracy, and Pass@8 measures the fraction of questions answered correctly at least once. MMLU-Pro contains 12,032 test questions and uses five-shot chain-of-thought demonstrations matched to the question’s category. We generate one response per question and report accuracy as Pass@1. AlpacaEval 2.0 compares model responses with GPT-4-Turbo reference responses using the weighted_alpaca_eval_gpt4_turbo annotator. We report both raw win rate (WR) and length-controlled win rate (LC-WR); these are preference-based scores, rather than answer-verification accuracy.
