# ABSTRACT 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards improves the reasoning capabilities of large language models, but remains computationally expensive and sample inefficient. On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level supervision from a stronger teacher, yet standard OPD distills every token regardless of whether the teacher’s preference actually improves task reward. Existing token-selection methods rely on proxies such as uncertainty or teacher–student divergence, which need not identify reward-improving supervision. We introduce Reward-Aligned Distillation (RAD), a bilevel framework that weights distillation signals based on their predicted effect on the student’s post-update reward. RAD differentiates through a temporary optimizer step to score each token’s supervision, then updates the token weights by projected ascent from the initial token weights. The resulting weights can strengthen, suppress, or reverse individual distillation-gradient contri-butions. At a matched training-response budget, RAD improves mean accuracy on six competition-math benchmarks over the strongest baselines by 6.5 percentage points for Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and 2.5 points for Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. RAD-Simple, a simplified variant, achieves comparable math accuracy at the same training-response budget. On LiveCodeBench-v6, RAD reaches 54.69% and 58.67% pass@1 with Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and OLMo3-7B-Instruct, respec-tively, exceeding the strongest baselines by 8.7 and 6.4 points. These results support reward-guided weighting of teacher supervision across model families and tasks. 10 20 30 40

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has been shown effective for improving mathematical reasoning (Luong & Lockhart, 2025) and code generation (Jaech et al., 2024; Ekbote et al., 2025) abilities, but its supervision is sparse: a long response often receives only a single sequence-level reward (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). On-policy distillation offers a comple-mentary signal by evaluating student-generated responses under a strong teacher and supplying dense token-level guidance (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). This approach is appealing because the student continues to explore on policy while learning from the teacher at every generated token. Standard OPD, however, implicitly treats the teacher as the target policy and every teacher preference as worth imitating. This would be justified if the teacher were optimal for the target reward, but a stronger teacher need not be reward-optimal. Teacher–student differences may reflect style, for-matting, verbosity, or reasoning choices that do not improve task reward (Armandpour et al., 2026). Consequently, reducing teacher–student divergence does not guarantee reward improvement: uniform distillation can spend capacity matching reward-irrelevant behavior or move the student away from reward-improving solutions (Xu et al., 2026; Akhondzadeh et al., 2026). The challenge is therefore to determine which teacher signals will benefit the student’s task per-formance. Token-selection methods (Xu et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026) use heuristic criteria such as uncertainty, teacher–student disagreement, and reasoning structure to selectively apply teacher supervision. These criteria identify potentially useful tokens, but do not directly assess whether the corresponding distillation update improves the student policy’s task reward. We therefore seek to choose each token’s distillation weight based on its predicted effect on reward: retain teacher signals that help the student solve the task, and suppress or reverse those that hinder it. 

To this end, we introduce reward-aligned distillation (RAD), which weights teacher supervision by its predicted effect on task reward. Token weights affect reward indirectly, by controlling how the student learns from teacher supervision. RAD captures this dependency with a bilevel objective: the inner problem trains the student with weighted distillation, while the outer problem selects weights to maximize the trained student’s reward (Equation 2). We approximate the inner optimization with one temporary optimizer step using token weights initialized to a tunable value. Differentiating the temporary student’s reward with respect to these weights yields look-ahead scores that guide a projected ascent update. The resulting signed weights can strengthen, suppress, or reverse individual distillation-gradient contributions. 

Main Contributions. 

• We introduce Reward-Aligned Distillation (RAD), a bilevel framework that assigns bounded, signed token weights according to their predicted effect on task reward (Section 4.2). A one-step approximation yields optimizer-aware scores computed jointly through a single Jacobian–vector product (Section 4.3). We also introduce RAD-Simple, which simplifies token scoring by removing the lookahead update and reusing distillation rollouts (Sec-tion 4.5). • At matched training-response budgets, RAD and RAD-Simple improve mean accuracy across six math benchmarks over the strongest baselines by up to 6.48 and 3.55 percentage points for Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, respectively. On LiveCodeBench-v6, the corresponding pass@1 gains for RAD are 8.7 and 6.4 points for Qwen3-1.7B and OLMo3-7B-Instruct (Section 5). • We demonstrate compatibility with an alternative distillation signal by applying RAD to OPD 2. This improves four-benchmark mean math accuracy by 2.66 and 6.51 percentage points for the 1.7B and 4B Qwen students, respectively (Section 6.3). 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

On-policy distillation for LLMs. On-policy distillation queries a teacher on student-generated responses, reducing the training–inference mismatch of offline distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Related self-distillation methods obtain teacher signals from the student itself using 2Preprint privileged information such as verified solutions or environment feedback (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). 

Unifying distillation and RLVR. KDRL (Xu et al., 2025) & RLAD (Zhang et al., 2026) combine verifiable rewards with teacher guidance, while RG-OPD (Akhondzadeh et al., 2026) uses verifier outcomes and teacher likelihoods to gate trajectory-level distillation. ExOPD (Yang et al., 2026) scales implicit teacher-derived rewards relative to KL regularization. 

Token-selective distillation. TIP (Xu et al., 2026) selects tokens using uncertainty and teacher– student divergence, while DEAR (Xiao et al., 2026) identifies decision tokens and supporting evidence. R EOPOLD (Ko et al., 2026) combines teacher-derived reward clipping with entropy-guided token sampling. Armandpour et al. (2026) develop an offline diagnostic of reward–distillation align-ment, estimating token-level Q-values by repeatedly sampling continuations after selected next-token choices, which is computationally expensive. They measure cosine similarity between local logit gradients and study oracle filtering of positively aligned tokens. RAD formulates token weighting as a bilevel optimization problem. A one-step approximation yields the sensitivity of post-update reward to each token’s weight, expressed as parameter-space alignment between its distillation gradient and an optimizer-adjusted reward direction. We estimate the reward gradient from minibatch rollouts, avoiding explicit branchwise Q-value estimation. Both the sign and magnitude of the resulting scores guide projected ascent on bounded, signed weights, which strengthen, suppress, or reverse distillation contributions during training. We compare against representative baselines from both reward-aware and token-selective distillation literature. Extended related work appears in Appendix B.

Choice of initial token weight. The initial token weight c determines both the temporary distillation update and the starting point for projected ascent. Under the symmetric constraint [c − 1, c + 1] , c = 1

Gradually expanding the negative range from [0 , 1] to [−1, 1] for c = 0 can help balance learning speed and performance. 4.4 RAD T RAINING ALGORITHM 

Distillation rollouts are used to update the student, whereas meta rollouts are used to estimate the reward gradient for token weighting. Both prompt batches are sampled from the same training dataset. RAD’s bilevel formulation makes token weighting depend on the student’s post-update reward, introducing additional computation before each weighted distillation update. At each step, we take a temporary AdamW step with initial token weights c1, generate separate meta rollouts, and estimate the reward gradient using GRPO objective. We compute the optimizer-aware token scores via a JVP (Equation (7)), update the weights with Equation (5), and train the original student with the updated weights. The meta batch is only used to learn the token weights; the committed student update uses the distillation rollouts. Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure of RAD, and Appendix A.2 gives the batch objective and implementation details. RAD can in principle combine any differentiable token-level OPD objective with any RLVR gradient estimator. Section 6.3 demonstrates compatibility with the OPD 2 (Heo et al., 2026) distillation signal. 4.5 RAD-S IMPLE : A S IMPLIFIED VARIANT OF RAD The bilevel formulation adds a temporary optimizer update, meta rollouts, a reward-gradient compu-tation, and a JVP to standard OPD. To simplify token scoring, RAD-Simple estimates the pre-update reward gradient by reusing the distillation rollouts. Replacing the post-update reward gradient in Equation (4) by the pre-update reward gradient gives 

It(y) = − ⟨∇ θ JR(θ), ∇θ ℓD,t (θ; y)⟩ .

We omit the common factor η, which can be absorbed into the weight learning rate γ, and use the same projected weight update in Equation (5). RAD-Simple also omits the optimizer Jacobian, while the student update still uses AdamW. Reusing the distillation rollouts avoids the temporary student update and separate meta rollout generation, halving the number of training responses per step. The only additional computation compared with OPD are reward-gradient estimation and a JVP. RAD-Simple ignores changes in the reward gradient after distillation and the optimizer’s effect on token scores. Table 1 compares RAD-Simple with RAD at a matched training-response budget. 

# 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We describe the experimental setup and evaluation protocol, then present math and coding results. Implementation details, including inference hyperparameters, appear in Appendix A. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS 

We evaluate RAD on mathematical reasoning and code generation, and additionally evaluate RAD-Simple on mathematical reasoning. 

Models. For mathematical reasoning, we use Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 as the teacher and Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as students (Yang et al., 2025). For code generation, we evaluate Qwen3-1.7B (non-thinking) with the same teacher, and OLMo3-7B-Instruct with OLMo3.1-32B-Instruct as its teacher. 

Training protocol. We train with verl (Sheng et al., 2025) using 64 prompts and eight responses per prompt. Within each domain and student setting, all methods share the training data, student initialization, rollout group size, sampling configuration, and optimizer settings. RAD additionally samples an equally sized, separate meta batch from the temporary student and updates token weights using exact JVPs. We compare 100 RAD updates with 200 baseline updates, matching the generated training responses, including RAD’s meta rollouts. At this budget, RAD uses 17–18% more training time than OPD and RLAD on Qwen3-1.7B math, but less than TIP and ExOPD. At the same training-response budget, RAD-Simple reaches 38 .42 ± 1.70% and 60 .84 ± 1.50% mean accuracy after 200 updates for the 1.7B and 4B students, respectively (Table 1). These results are 0.65 

points below and 1.04 points above RAD at step 100, respectively, and exceed the strongest baselines by 5.83 and 3.55 points. This shows that the simpler pre-update scores can also support effective token weighting. However, within the same 100 training steps, RAD-Simple learns slower than RAD as shown in Figure 3. RAD-Simple removes the temporary optimizer update and separate meta rollouts, and computes token scores using the current-policy reward gradient without the optimizer Jacobian. It achieves similar math accuracy at a matched generated-response budget, but requires more training time for Qwen3-1.7B. # 7 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

RAD uses optimizer-aware lookahead scores and projected ascent to align token-level distillation with reward. It improves math and coding accuracy at a matched training-response budget. Results with OLMo3-7B-Instruct extend the evidence to a different model family and a larger student, while OPD 2 results support compatibility with other distillation signals. RAD-Simple reuses distillation rollouts and removes the temporary update; it improves over the strongest math baselines for both Qwen students at the same training-response budget, demonstrating the effectiveness of simpler token scoring in these settings. 

Limitations and future work. RAD requires a temporary student update, fresh outer rollouts, and an exact JVP, increasing its per-step cost relative to standard OPD. RAD-Simple removes the temporary update and fresh outer rollouts but still requires reward-gradient estimation and a JVP. Further reducing these costs and understanding when optimizer-aware lookahead is beneficial remain important directions. Future work should explore longer training horizons, and learned or non-verifiable rewards.

# B EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

On-policy distillation for LLMs. Knowledge distillation transfers capabilities from a larger teacher to a smaller student (Buciluˇ a et al., 2006; Hinton et al., 2015). For LLMs, teacher supervision can take the form of token-level probability distributions or generated text (Kim & Rush, 2016; Sanh et al., 2020). Off-policy distillation trains on fixed sequences, often generated by the teacher (Hsieh et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). On-policy distillation instead queries the teacher on student-generated responses, exposing the student to supervision along its own trajectories and reducing the mismatch between training and inference (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024). Self-distillation paradigm obtains supervision from the student itself, with privileged information such as verified solutions or environment feedback providing a stronger teacher context (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). 

Unifying distillation and RLVR. Several methods combine dense teacher supervision with verifiable task rewards. KDRL (Xu et al., 2025) jointly optimizes a GRPO reward objective and reverse-KL distillation, while RLAD (Zhang et al., 2026) incorporates teacher guidance into policy optimization through a likelihood ratio anchored to a mixture of the teacher and the previous student policy. RG-OPD (Akhondzadeh et al., 2026) uses agreement between verifier outcomes and teacher likelihoods to gate trajectory-level distillation. A related perspective connects OPD to KL-regularized reinforcement learning: Yang et al. (2026) introduce a flexible reference model and scales the implicit reward derived from teacher probabilities, yielding the reward-extrapolation variant ExOPD. Our proposed method, RAD, uses task reward to determine how individual token-level distillation contributions should enter the student update. 

Token-selective distillation. TIP (Xu et al., 2026) and DEAR (Xiao et al., 2026) select teacher super-vision using uncertainty, teacher–student divergence, and reasoning structure. R EOPOLD (Ko et al., 2026) combines token log-ratio rewards with clipping and entropy-guided sampling. Armandpour et al. (2026) diagnose token-level alignment between distillation and reward-improving directions in logit space and study oracle filtering of positively aligned tokens. RAD operationalizes reward-guided weighting during training: it differentiates a reward surrogate through the student optimizer and shared parameters to assign bounded, signed token weights. These weights can also reverse teacher contributions; our positive-only ablation supports the benefit of this additional flexibility. We compare RAD with representative reward-aware and token-selective baselines. 

Bilevel reweighting. Learning example weights from a held-out objective is a standard use of bilevel optimization (Ren et al., 2018). Gradient similarity has also been used to adapt auxiliary losses toward a primary objective (Du et al., 2018). We apply this principle at token granularity in on-policy language-model distillation. The one-step approximation avoids solving the full inner problem or an implicit linear system (Liu et al., 2022; Kwon et al., 2023).

We apply RAD to an agentic software-engineering task. A Qwen3.5-4B 3 policy is trained on R2E-Gym (Jain et al., 2025b) with the OpenHands agent harness provided by R2E-Gym. The olmo3_layer_streamed backend evaluates the embedding, decoder layers, final normal-ization, and output projection in sequence. For each parameter group, the FSDP executor gathers its parameters and the corresponding components of p, propagates both activations and activation 29 Preprint tangents, and releases the gathered tensors after use. It can prefetch the next group, so gathered parameter and direction storage is bounded by the current and prefetched groups. Activation states are retained for a bounded set of microbatches at a time, with microbatch grouping synchronized across ranks to preserve the order of collective operations. Within each OLMo decoder layer, the backend uses the same FlashAttention primal and Triton attention tangent as flash_triton (Algorithm 2), including the masks for full and sliding-window attention.
