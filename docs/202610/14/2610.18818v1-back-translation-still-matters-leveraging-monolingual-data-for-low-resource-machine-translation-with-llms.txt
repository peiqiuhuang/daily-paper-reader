## ABSTRACT 

Pre-trained Large Language Model (LLMs) are effective on many tasks in zero-shot and few-shot settings. For machine translation of low-resource languages, the obvious options are few-shot prompting and fine-tuning on a small parallel set. We ask: what if moderate amounts of low-resource monolingual data are also available? We argue that, rather than continued pretraining, a more effective use of this data is the pre-LLM era technique of back-translation (BT). The current fine-tuned LLM translates monolingual sentences in each language, and the resulting pairs train it in the reverse direction. Updates on the parallel set are interleaved for stable learning. With 1K parallel pairs on Odia and Swahili, BT beats continued pretraining into the low-resource language by 7.7 to 16.4 XCOMET-XXL points in domain. With beam-decoded intermediate translations, it also beats a model with 10 × more parallel data. In-context examples retrieved from the parallel data further improve BT. We also analyze the source, quality and amount of monolingual text. Across five low-resource languages, with Hindi as a high-resource control, and two 3B backbones, BT improves translation into the low-resource language by 16 to 173% XCOMET-XXL over parallel-only fine-tuning. Blind human evaluation on Odia and Sanskrit agrees with the automatic metrics. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

LLMs are effective on many tasks in zero-shot and few-shot settings (Brown et al., 2020), and they translate well between high-resource languages (Hendy et al., 2023). They are, however, pre-trained overwhelmingly on a handful of high-resource languages, while most of the world’s languages appear only sparsely. Open-source instruction-tuned models are therefore fluent in English but have weak or near-zero competence in many low-resource languages. Gold-standard parallel corpora for low-resource pairs are also typically two to three orders of magnitude smaller than those for high-resource pairs, and they are often confined to narrow domains (Ramesh et al., 2022; Gala et al., 2023; NLLB Team et al., 2022). The obvious ways to use such model for a low-resource language are few-shot prompting and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a parallel set. Few-shot prompting places a few translated examples in the prompt, and retrieving examples that are similar to the input helps further (Vilar et al., 2023; Zebaze et al., 2025a;b). SFT on a small, high-quality parallel set adapts an LLM to translation (Xu et al., 2024). Both depend on what the model already knows about the language. But what if we have a moderate amount of monolingual text available at our disposal. So, this paper asks: Can an LLM with a weak prior leverage monolingual text for better translation, and how should that text enter training?

1arXiv:2610.18818v1 [cs.CL] 13 Oct 2026 Preprint Two recipes have emerged for using monolingual text with LLMs. The first is continued pretraining (CPT) followed by SFT. ALMA (Xu et al., 2024) applies CPT on 7B & 13B models with 1–20B monolingual tokens before fine-tuning on a small parallel set, and LLaMAX (Lu et al., 2024) extends CPT, with added parallel data, to over 100 languages. ALMA’s CPT optimizes a generic language-modeling objective, so cross-lingual mapping is learned only in SFT. Whether CPT will still pay off with only few million token is unclear. The second recipe is self-distillation. CycleDistill (Halder et al., 2025) translates monolingual text with zero- or few-shot prompts, fine-tunes on its own translations, and repeats the cycle. Its training targets are therefore model-generated, and its authors report no gains if base model is weak. Before LLMs, monolingual text entered machine translation (MT) mainly through back-translation (BT) (Sennrich et al., 2016). A reverse model translates a human-written target-language sentence into the source language. The resulting pair has a synthetic source and a human target, and it is added to the training data. Later work sampled or noised the synthetic sources (Edunov et al., 2018), tagged them (Caswell et al., 2019), iterated the procedure (Hoang et al., 2018), and trained one bidirectional model for both directions (Niu et al., 2018). Dual learning and unsupervised MT learn from round trips over monolingual corpora in a similar way (He et al., 2016; Artetxe et al., 2018; Lample et al., 2018). These methods trained encoder–decoder models from scratch, first recurrent ones and later Transformers, and the BT studies used tens of thousands of parallel pairs or more. Their defining property is simple. The human-written sentence stays on the target side, so the model’s own errors enter training only as input. Self-distillation does the opposite and trains the model on its own outputs. We ask whether this property still matters for pretrained instruction-tuned LLMs. To test it, we port BT to such models as BackTransLLM. BackTransLLM interleaves BT updates on monolingual data with supervised updates on the small parallel set, which keep learning stable. In each BT update, the model translates a monolingual sentence into the other language (a pivot) and then learns to reconstruct the original sentence from that pivot. A stop-gradient on the pivot means every BT gradient comes from predicting original monolingual text. We call this property anchoring. Like Niu et al. (2018), we use one model for both directions, and like the semi-supervised variant of Artetxe et al. (2018), we back-translate each batch on the fly with the current model and alternate these updates with supervised steps. While many of these ideas predate LLMs, our contribution is a controlled test of whether they still work for instruction-tuned LLMs with weak priors. All tested recipes use the same backbone and same monolingual text. CPT and BackTransLLM also share the same 1K parallel pairs. We keep ALMA’s full-weight CPT but run it on our monolingual pools, and we call the result ALMA-style. We follow CycleDistill’s published recipe, with zero- or one-shot prompts and three cycles, and let our 3B model act as its own teacher. XCOMET-XXL (Guerreiro et al., 2024) does not check the output language, so we report ChrF++ (Popovi´ c, 2017) alongside and flag outputs if in wrong language. The results are consistent across Odia and Swahili. Self-distillation fails, which confirms that the prior is weak. After three cycles into Odia, 99% of the model’s outputs contain no Odia script, because training on its own outputs reinforces the errors of a weak prior. CPT helps on 15 of 16 measures, and into English it beats BackTransLLM with sampled pivots on XCOMET-XXL. Into the low-resource language, however, it trails BackTransLLM by 7.7 to 16.4 XCOMET-XXL points in domain. Pivot decoding also matters, as Edunov et al. (2018) found in resource-poor settings. With beam-decoded pivots, an option that only BT has, BackTransLLM beats CPT on 15 of 16 measures and a model trained on 10 × more parallel data on all 16. In-context examples retrieved from the parallel data, which every recipe can use, improve both CPT →SFT and BackTransLLM. We also analyze the source, quality and amount of monolingual text. Across five low-resource languages and two 3B backbones, BackTransLLM improves translation into the low-resource language by 16–173% XCOMET-XXL over the parallel data alone. Blind human evaluation on two languages agrees with the automatic metrics. We will make the code publicly available on acceptance. Our primary contributions are: • We study back-translation (BT) as a way to use monolingual data when adapting general-purpose LLMs to low-resource machine translation. We compare it with continued pretrain-ing, the standard LLM-era use of such data. • Our approach builds on pre-LLM BT. The fine-tuned LLM translates monolingual sentences in each language, and the resulting pairs train it in the reverse direction. ## 3 BACK TRANS LLM: B ACK -T RANSLATION FOR INSTRUCTION -T UNED LLM SWITH WEAK PRIORS 

Our question is whether an LLM with a weak prior can leverage monolingual text for better translation, and how that text should enter training. The recipes in §2 differ in what the model learns to predict. CPT predicts original text but learns no mapping between languages. Self-distillation learns the mapping, but from its own translations, which are poor when the prior is weak. BT gives both, a translation signal and original text as every target. BackTransLLM keeps this anchoring in an instruction-tuned LLM. 

Training cycle: Figure 1 shows one training cycle. Training alternates between the two directions 

d ∈ { x → e, e → x}. For each direction, BackTransLLM takes one supervised step on a batch of parallel pairs, then r BT steps on batches of monolingual sentences, and then switches direction. The model generates the pivots on the fly with its current parameters, so the BT signal evolves during training. We use r=4 , a robust choice among r ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8} (Appendix D). An epoch is defined over the monolingual pools, and the parallel data is cycled as needed.

## 4 EMPIRICAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

We compare the recipes of §2 on Odia and Swahili with LLaMA, and then analyze BackTransLLM on six English-paired languages and two backbones. Our experiments address six questions. 1) Which recipe leverages monolingual data best (§4.1)? 2) Does in-context learning help (§4.2)? 3) Which monolingual text should be used (§4.3)? 4) How much monolingual text is needed (§4.4)? 5) Do the gains hold across languages and backbones (§4.5)? 6) Do human judges agree with the automatic metrics (§4.6)? 

Experimental setup: For each language pair, the 1K parallel pairs and both monolingual pools come primarily from one parallel corpus. The corpora are VaqasAI for Odia (Ahmed, 2025; Had-dow and Kirefu, 2020), Samanantar for Assamese (Ramesh et al., 2022), IITB En–Hi for Hindi (Kunchukuttan et al., 2018), Mitrasam . graha for Sanskrit (Nehrdich et al., 2026), and MAFAND for both African pairs (Adelani et al., 2022). CC-100 supplies Amharic monolingual data and augments Swahili (Conneau et al., 2020; Wenzek et al., 2020). When both monolingual pools come from the same parallel source, they use disjoint subsets, and all pools are disjoint from validation and test data (Appendix A). Hindi serves as a high-resource control. Our primary backbone is LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct (Meta AI, 2024), and Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) tests cross-family generality. Both are fine-tuned with 4-bit QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2022) under identical settings (LoRA rank 64, peak learning rate 2 × 10 −4, maximum sequence length 2,048) until the development score converges. Prompts use a simple translation instruction wrapped in the model’s chat template. We report XCOMET-XXL (X-XXL) (Guerreiro et al., 2024) and ChrF++ (Popovi´ c, 2017) on in-domain held-out test sets of 2K sentences and on Flores-200 (NLLB Team et al., 2022). Checkpoints are selected on validation BERTScore, so neither primary metric is used for selection. As baselines, 1K-SFT fine-tunes on the 1K parallel pairs. 10K-SFT uses ten times more pairs as a reference, except for Amharic, which has about 1.9K pairs. Few-shot prompts the base model, without training, with k=5 examples retrieved from the 1K pairs. CPT →SFT (ALMA-style) applies full-weight continued pretraining to the same 40K + 40K monolingual sentences and then fine-tunes on the parallel data. A variant uses only the first 100 parallel pairs. Self-distillation (CycleDistill-style) runs three cycles with sampled labels, either 0-shot (every prompt is zero-shot) or 1-shot (every prompt contains one gold example). CycleDistill translates into English, so translating into the low-resource language is our extension. We now analyze BackTransLLM further. These analyses use sampled pivots, which are cheaper than beam pivots, so they likely understate it. 

Monolingual source: Low-resource languages rarely have curated monolingual corpora, so prac-titioners rely on web text or other domains. ## 5 CONCLUSIONS AND LIMITATIONS 

We asked how an instruction-tuned LLM with a weak prior should use scarce parallel data and bounded monolingual text in a low-resource language. With identical data on Odia and Swahili, self-distillation failed, and continued pretraining helped but trailed back-translation into the low-resource language. Back-translation keeps original text on the target side. With beam-decoded pivots, it beat continued pretraining and ten times more parallel data, and retrieved in-context examples also improved it. Across five low-resource languages and two 3B backbones, its gains into the low-resource language were large and consistent, and human judges agreed. In practice, we recommend beam-decoded pivots and monolingual text chosen for quality before domain. In this regime the pre-LLM recipe remains the right default. Future work includes more languages and backbones, larger models and pairs that do not involve English. 9Preprint We study 3B instruction-tuned backbones with about 1K parallel pairs, translating only to and from English. The recipe comparison, the in-context learning study and the human evaluation each cover two languages on one backbone. Our ALMA-style and CycleDistill-style baselines are re-implementations at this scale, and self-distillation may work with stronger priors or larger models. We do not yet combine ICL with beam sampling for pivot generation and back-translation post CPT.

Corpora: Table 6 lists the parallel and monolingual corpora for each language pair. The VaqasAI corpus card states that its translations were produced with Gemini from English source text, so the Odia side of the VaqasAI seed, pools and held-out test set is machine-translated. Flores-200 is our human-translated Odia test set. 

Monolingual token budget: Each language has a pool of 40K monolingual sentences. We count the tokens of the pool text as the training loader reads it, without special tokens or the chat template. With the LLaMA tokenizer, the Odia–English pools contain 9.69M tokens (8.75M Odia and 0.94M English), and the Swahili–English pools contain 3.51M (2.36M Swahili and 1.15M English). With the Qwen tokenizer, the totals are 8.01M (7.06M Odia and 0.95M English) and 3.57M (2.41M Swahili and 1.16M English). Under LLaMA, an Odia pool sentence averages 219 tokens, a Swahili sentence 59 and an English sentence 24 to 29. Both totals are at least two orders of magnitude below the 1B to 20B monolingual tokens used by ALMA (Xu et al., 2024).

Automatic metrics: We report five automatic metrics, computed identically for both directions and both evaluation sets. For learned evaluation we use XCOMET (Guerreiro et al., 2024) in its reference-based setting, scoring each (source, hypothesis, reference) triple with the Unbabel/XCOMET-XXL 

and Unbabel/XCOMET-XL checkpoints. XCOMET does not check whether the hypothesis is in the requested language, so we always report ChrF++ alongside it. ChrF++ (Popovi´ c, 2017) uses the sacreBLEU (Post, 2018) CHRF implementation with word_order=2 (character n-grams plus word unigrams and bigrams). BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) uses the NLTK sentence-level implemen-tation with uniform 4-gram weights, Chen and Cherry smoothing method 2 (Chen and Cherry, 2014) and whitespace tokenisation. Both are averaged over sentences and scaled to 0–100 . For BERTScore F1 (Zhang et al., 2020) we use a target-language encoder, namely roberta-large (Liu et al., 2019) into English, google/muril-large-cased (Khanuja et al., 2021) for the four Indic targets, and xlm-roberta-large (Conneau et al., 2020) for Amharic and Swahili. We report the baseline-normalised value (F1 − b)/(1 − b). Into English we use the built-in baseline. For the other targets, b is the mean BERTScore F1 over 100K random sentence pairs from the corresponding monolingual corpus (Table 8).

## B TRAINING RECIPES 

This section gives the settings of the recipes compared in §4.1. All of them use the data, prompt template and metrics of Appendix A, the same settings on both backbones, and the quantisation and LoRA configuration of Table 10. 

BackTransLLM: One epoch is one pass over the back-translation data, which with 40K monolin-gual sentences per language is 780 optimizer steps (156 supervised and 624 back-translation). Each run is trained until its development score converges. CPT →BackTransLLM starts BackTransLLM from the stage-1 model of CPT →SFT. 

ALMA-style CPT →SFT: We follow ALMA’s 7B-LoRA setup (Xu et al., 2024): full-weight continued pretraining on the same monolingual pools that BackTransLLM back-translates, then LoRA fine-tuning on the 1K pairs in both directions with mixed-direction batches (Table 11). The same stage 2 without CPT is the matched control, so the two differ only in stage 1. Our setting is much smaller than ALMA’s: a 3B instead of a 7B or 13B model, about 9M instead of up to 20B monolingual tokens, and 1K instead of 58K parallel pairs. 

CycleDistill-style self-distillation: CycleDistill (Halder et al., 2025) does not state its epochs, learning rate, decoding or checkpoint selection (its released code sets defaults for some of these), so we use our shared settings (Table 12). Self-distill (0-shot) uses zero-shot prompts throughout, and self-distill (1-shot) places one gold example from the 1K seed in every labelling, training, validation and test prompt. Unlike CycleDistill, we use 40K instead of 20K pool sentences, and let a 3B model act as its own teacher instead of an 8B or 9B teacher. 17 Preprint

## E IN-C ONTEXT LEARNING 

We also ask whether the same 1K seed can be used more effectively at no annotation cost. With in-context learning (ICL), we prepend up to k=5 semantically similar examples, retrieved from the 1K seed, to each training and test sentence (Appendix A). Examples are dropped when the prompt would exceed 2,048 tokens. This happens mainly on Odia Flores-200 into English, where prompts contain 3.6 examples on average. The retrieved examples are used consistently in supervised and back-translation updates and at inference time. Table 16 gives all five metrics for the systems of Table 2, for Odia and Amharic, in domain and on Flores-200. In domain, 1K-SFT with ICL outperforms 1K-SFT under the same budget ( +16 .7% 

X-XXL EN →OR and +9 .2% OR →EN for Odia), and BackTransLLM with ICL improves over BackTransLLM on every metric in both directions for both languages. CPT →SFT gains less from ICL (1.6 and 1.3 X-XXL points on Odia). On Odia Flores-200, ICL changes the X-XXL of 1K-SFT and CPT →SFT by less than one point, and BackTransLLM with ICL gains 3.9 points into English and none into Odia. On Amharic Flores-200, ICL changes the X-XXL of both systems by less than one point, and BackTransLLM with ICL is below BackTransLLM on all five metrics into Amharic and on four of the five into English.

## J LANGUAGES AND BACKBONES : F ULL FLORES -200 R ESULTS 

This section gives the Flores-200 (NLLB Team et al., 2022) results (1,012 test sentences) for the systems of §4.5, all with sampled pivots, in the layout of Appendix I (Table 22). Into the low-resource language, BackTransLLM improves over 1K-SFT on all five metrics in ten of the twelve language and backbone combinations; the exceptions are LLaMA Hindi (all five metrics) and LLaMA Sanskrit (X-XXL and BERTScore, by at most 0.40). Out of domain, it exceeds 10K-SFT on X-XXL in only five of the ten combinations that have a 10K-SFT run, against eight of ten on the held-out test sets. Into English, BackTransLLM with sampled pivots stays below 1K-SFT on X-XXL in nine of the twelve combinations; only Amharic on both backbones and Swahili on Qwen improve, and they do so on all five metrics. 10K-SFT has the higher X-XXL into English in all ten. Beam-decoded pivots remove this gap on Odia and Swahili: on Flores-200 into English, BackTransLLM with beam pivots exceeds both 1K-SFT and 10K-SFT (Table 1). Finally, LLaMA zero-shot has the highest X-XXL of all systems into Assamese and Sanskrit, although its ChrF++ into Assamese is below that of both trained systems. 

## K SEED ROBUSTNESS AND SIGNIFICANCE (O DIA )

To check that the Odia results are not artifacts of one random initialisation, we train 1K-SFT, BackTransLLM and 10K-SFT three times each: the original run and two more runs with seeds 123 and 456. Table 23 reports the mean ± standard deviation over the three seeds on the held-out (2K) set. Standard deviations are at most 1.85 points, which is larger than the gap between BackTransLLM and 10K-SFT on X-XXL into Odia (66.52 against 66.42). We also test whether BackTransLLM and 10K-SFT differ per sentence. For each system, we first average each test sentence’s score over the three seeds, so that the seeds are not counted as extra test sentences, and then apply a two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Wilcoxon, 1945) to the 2K sentence pairs. We correct the ten p-values (five metrics, two directions) with the Holm procedure (Holm, 1979). The last row of each block in Table 23 gives the rank-biserial effect size, where a positive value means BackTransLLM is ahead. Into English, 10K-SFT is significantly ahead on every metric ( r from −0.20 to −0.36 ). Into Odia, BackTransLLM is significantly ahead on X-XL, BERTScore and BLEU, and the two are not significantly different on X-XXL ( p=0 .67 ) or ChrF++ (Holm-adjusted p=0 .12 ). A paired bootstrap over sentences (Koehn, 2004) does favour BackTransLLM on mean ChrF++ ( +0 .72 points, 95% CI 0.26 to 1.18 ), so the ChrF++ result depends on the test. These tests use sampled pivots and treat the three seeds as fixed.
