Abstract 

Large language models have recently been used to discover heuristics through iterative program search, offering a promising approach to au-tomated algorithm design. However, candi-dates selected by average performance may de-grade across instance families, random seeds, and problem scales because mean-based ob-jectives can obscure rare but severe failures. We propose CVaR-RMD, a risk-sensitive evo-lutionary framework that uses an LLM to mu-tate executable heuristic programs and ranks candidates by empirical conditional value-at-risk over heterogeneous evaluation conditions. Across three combinatorial optimization do-mains, CVaR-based selection improves upper-tail performance and reduces failure rates while maintaining competitive mean perfor-mance. These gains persist on larger unseen instances and independently curated within-domain benchmarks. Code is available here .

1 Introduction 

Heuristics and metaheuristics are central to com-binatorial, stochastic, black-box, or partially speci-fied optimization problems. Their practical value depends not only on benchmark performance dur-ing algorithm discovery but also on whether it per-sists when evaluation conditions change within the same domain. Such changes include shifts across instance families, problem scales, and ran-dom seeds. We call this setting within-domain transfer under benchmark distribution shift: the optimization problem and solution representation remain unchanged, but benchmark conditions dif-fer between discovery and testing. This concern is longstanding in heuristic optimization, where empirical success often depends on interactions among algorithm design, benchmark distributions, and stochastic variation (Talbi, 2009; Hoos and Stützle, 2004; Blum and Roli, 2003).

Automated algorithm design naturally supports studying this challenge because candidates are gen-erated and selected by their observed performance under specified evaluation conditions. However, searching algorithmic choices alone does not en-sure within-domain transfer, which depends criti-cally on the evaluation conditions and ranking ob-jective. Hyper-heuristics automate design through search spaces of heuristics or heuristic components, while algorithm configuration and selection eval-uate candidate procedures across instance sets to support performance beyond individual discovery instances (Burke et al., 2013; Rice, 1976; Hutter et al., 2011; López-Ibáñez et al., 2016; Ansótegui et al., 2009). A candidate heuristic may achieve strong aver-age performance yet fail on rare instances, different stochastic seeds, or larger scales. This issue is espe-cially relevant to LLM-generated metaheuristics be-cause generated code may exploit a fixed evaluation protocol, such as a narrow instance generator, few seeds, or a restricted scale range. The outer loop may therefore select heuristics with high expected performance but unstable tails. In evolutionary search, this selection pressure rewards favorable means even when outcome distributions contain se-vere failures (Bäck, 1996; Eiben and Smith, 2003; Goldberg and Deb, 1991). Because such failures occur only under adverse subsets of evaluation conditions, mean aggrega-tion may obscure them. A useful selection crite-rion should therefore emphasize adverse outcomes without allowing a single extreme observation to dominate candidate ranking. CVaR satisfies this requirement by averaging performance over a con-figurable worst-performing fraction of evaluation outcomes. We therefore study a minimal change to discovery: replacing mean-based fitness with CVaR-based fitness while leaving the outer loop unchanged. Its fitness uses conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), a coherent risk measure esti-mating expected loss within the worst fraction of outcomes at a chosen quantile level (Artzner et al., 1999; Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000, 2002; Pflug, 2000). The resulting CVaR-based Robust Meta-heuristic Discovery framework (CVaR-RMD) uses the LLM as a mutation generator to propose heuris-tic code, while estimated CVaR fitness determines candidate acceptance. As Figure 1 shows, mean-based selection tolerates severe tail failures that CVaR-RMD penalizes. Because selected programs become parents and prompt context, CVaR-based ranking affects both final selection and the subse-quent trajectory of LLM-driven program discovery. This formulation shifts selection toward worst-quantile behavior and discourages candidates de-pendent on favorable evaluation conditions. It fol-lows robust and distributionally robust optimiza-tion, where objectives accounting for adverse out-comes and distributional variation replace or sup-plement expectation-based criteria (Ben-Tal et al., 2009; Beyer and Sendhoff, 2007; Bertsimas et al., 2011; Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019; Duchi and Namkoong, 2021). It also aligns with optimiza-tion under noise and uncertainty, which uses re-peated evaluation and robust criteria to reduce the selection of brittle solutions (Jin and Branke, 2005; Branke et al., 2008; Beyer and Sendhoff, 2007). Our empirical analysis tests whether risk-based selection improves heuristic stability and transfer across evaluation conditions without adding archi-tectural components to the LLM-driven discovery pipeline. The contributions of this work are as follows: 1. We introduce CVaR-based selection into LLM-guided metaheuristic discovery, using empirical CVaR as the primary fitness crite-rion to emphasize adverse-quantile behavior across heterogeneous evaluation conditions. 2. We propose CVaR-RMD, an execution-driven, risk-sensitive framework that integrates CVaR-based candidate ranking, accumulated execu-tion outcomes, and tail-case feedback into an evolutionary program-discovery loop for gen-erating robust executable heuristics. 3. We conduct controlled, budget-matched exper-iments across three combinatorial optimiza-tion domains and show that CVaR-RMD im-proves upper-tail performance and reduces ex-2ecution failures while maintaining competi-tive mean performance. 

2 Related Work 

Automated Heuristic Design Automated heuris-tic design has been studied through hyper-heuristics, algorithm selection/configuration, and program synthesis. Hyper-heuristics search over heuristic spaces by selecting existing low-level op-erators or generating new components that general-ize across instances (Burke et al., 2013; Cowling et al., 2001; Ochoa et al., 2012). Algorithm se-lection and configuration address related questions from a statistical and empirical perspective, using instance features, performance observations, and iterative evaluation to identify robust algorithms or parameterizations under limited budgets (Rice, 1976; Hutter et al., 2009, 2011; López-Ibáñez et al., 2016; Ansótegui et al., 2009). Earlier genetic pro-gramming and grammatical evolution methods di-rectly searched over program structures via fitness-based selection and variation (Koza, 1992; O’Neill and Ryan, 2001; Langdon and Poli, 2002). These works frame heuristic construction as a search prob-lem whose evaluation protocol and selection objec-tive strongly affect generalization. Recent LLM-based methods extend this paradigm by coupling generative proposal mech-anisms with external evaluation. FunSearch evolves and validates LLM-generated code variants using task-specific scoring functions (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). EoH co-evolves natural-language “thoughts” and executable heuristics (Liu et al., 2024a), while ReEvo uses LLMs as hyper-heuristics with reflective evolution (Ye et al., 2024). LLaMEA evolves LLM-generated metaheuristics for black-box continuous optimization (van Stein and Bäck, 2024), and LLAMBO integrates LLM reasoning into Bayesian optimization pipelines (Liu et al., 2024b). Despite differences in representation, prompting, and search dynamics, these systems typically depend on scalar fitness values from limited empirical evaluations. 

Robust and Risk-Aware Optimization Robust optimization seeks solutions that remain effective under uncertainty, emphasizing robustness metrics and the trade-off between nominal performance and stability (Ben-Tal et al., 2009; Beyer and Send-hoff, 2007; Bertsimas et al., 2011). Distribution-ally robust optimization further optimizes against worst-case distributions within ambiguity sets (De-lage and Ye, 2010; Rahimian and Mehrotra, 2019; Duchi and Namkoong, 2021). Among risk mea-sures, conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) captures tail behavior and supports tractable sample-based formulations (Artzner et al., 1999; Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000, 2002; Pflug, 2000). Such mea-sures are widely used in finance, stochastic pro-gramming, reinforcement learning, and decision-making (Shapiro et al., 2009; Tamar et al., 2015; Chow et al., 2015). Robustness under noise and uncertainty has also been extensively studied in evolutionary computation (Jin and Branke, 2005; Branke et al., 2008; Beyer and Sendhoff, 2007). These studies motivate objectives that account for unfavorable outcomes rather than only average per-formance. 

Benchmarking, Robustness, and Transfer 

Evaluation protocols are central to empirical com-binatorial optimization because conclusions about algorithm performance depend strongly on the in-stances and conditions under which algorithms are assessed. Benchmark suites and heterogeneous in-stance collections are commonly used to evaluate whether algorithms remain effective beyond their development conditions (Johnson and McGeoch, 1997; Ochoa et al., 2012; Reinelt, 1991). In rout-ing and learned combinatorial optimization, prior studies have documented performance degradation when methods are evaluated across problem scales, instance generators, and instance families that dif-fer from those used during development (Vinyals et al., 2015; Bello et al., 2017; Kool et al., 2019; Joshi et al., 2019). These findings motivate evalu-ation protocols that account not only for average performance but also for robustness under hetero-geneous and shifted benchmark conditions. When candidate performance is summarized solely by the empirical mean, the selection process may favor heuristics that perform well on typical instances while remaining vulnerable to rare, difficult, or shifted cases. This concern is particularly relevant to LLM-driven heuristic discovery, where finite em-pirical evaluations repeatedly determine which gen-erated programs are retained and used to guide sub-sequent search (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Ye et al., 2024; van Stein and Bäck, 2024). 4.2 Baselines 

We compare four baseline groups. Handcrafted heuristics comprise first-fit and best-fit for bin pack-ing, Clarke–Wright savings and cheapest insertion for CVRP, and NEH with shortest-processing-time dispatching for flow-shop scheduling. Direct LLM sampling draws as many candidate programs as one discovery run without evolutionary replacement. FunSearch-style (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024) and EoH-style (Liu et al., 2024a) variants use the same infrastructure, generators, parsers, validators, bud-gets, and LLM settings as CVaR-RMD but retain mean-performance selection. Mean-RMD is the closest control, using the same evolutionary loop as CVaR-RMD but ranking candidates by empirical mean loss.

4.4 Transfer to Larger Instances 

We freeze each discovered program and evaluate it on larger unseen instances with new random seeds. Instance families remain unchanged, but their scale parameters exceed the discovery range. Relative to Mean-RMD, CVaR-RMD reduces transfer CVaR 0.90 by 7.8% ,maximum loss by 9.4% , and failure rate by 1.5 per-centage points, with all differences significant at

4.5 Comparison with Alternative Risk-Aware Objectives 

Mean-RMD controls the comparison between expectation-based and tail-risk selection but does not show whether CVaR is preferable to other ro-bustness objectives. We therefore evaluate four additional risk-aware variants using the same LLM mutation model, population size, search budget, validation procedure, and program–environment execution budget as CVaR-RMD. MEAN +S TD -RMD ranks candidates by the sum of empirical mean loss and standard deviation. QUANTILE -RMD uses empirical VaR 0.90 , while MAX -RMD uses maximum observed loss. To reduce dependence on synthetic discovery gen-erators, we also evaluate the frozen programs on independently curated public benchmark instances. 4.7 Ablations and Sensitivity 

We ablate components distinguishing CVaR-RMD from the mean-based control while fixing prompts, validators, budgets, populations, and held-out tests. Table 6 identifies CVaR ranking as the main im-provement driver. Replacing it with mean rank-ing increases CVaR 0.90 by 5.9% and raises failure rate from 1.4% to 2.3% . Removing accumulated losses performs worse because fewer evaluations yield noisier tail estimates. Prompted tail-loss sum-maries provide a smaller additional benefit by in-forming mutations about adverse execution modes. Tail-level comparisons reveal the expected tradeoff: 

q = 0 .80 offers less protection against rare failures, whereas q = 0 .95 suppresses them more aggres-sively but weakens mean performance. The default 

q = 0 .90 provides the best observed balance. Table 3 and Figure 3 show that CVaR-RMD re-mains stable under moderate changes in evaluation batch size and failure penalty. Smaller batches increase tail-estimation variance, whereas larger batches improve robustness at higher evaluation cost. Increasing the failure penalty further discour-ages infeasible programs but mildly reduces search flexibility in mean loss. These results support the default configuration as a practical robustness-cost compromise. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented CVaR-RMD, a risk-sensitive frame-work that replaces mean-based fitness with CVaR selection in LLM-driven metaheuristic discovery. Across three combinatorial optimization tasks, CVaR-RMD improved upper-tail performance and reduced failure rates while maintaining competitive mean performance. These benefits persisted under within-domain transfer to larger unseen instances and independently curated benchmarks. The re-sults show that tail-risk-based selection can pro-duce more reliable heuristics under benchmark dis-tribution shifts without adding architectural com-ponents to the discovery pipeline. 9Limitations 

The empirical evaluation focuses on three repre-sentative combinatorial optimization tasks, and fur-ther validation on additional problem classes would help assess the generality of the approach. CVaR-RMD also introduces a robustness hyperparameter through the tail level q, and while a single default value performs consistently well in the experiments, the optimal choice may vary across applications de-pending on the desired trade-off between average and worst-case performance.

A Environment Details 

A.1 Datasets and Task Settings 

We evaluate LLM-discovered metaheuristics on three combinatorial optimization benchmarks: on-line bin packing, capacitated vehicle routing, and permutation flow-shop scheduling. These tasks are selected to cover online allocation decisions, hard feasibility constraints, and long-horizon sequenc-ing interactions. For online bin packing, instances consist of se-quentially arriving items that must be assigned to bins of fixed capacity. Discovery uses item sequences with 100 –300 items drawn from uni-form, truncated-normal, and heavy-tailed beta dis-tributions, while transfer evaluation uses larger se-quences with 400 –800 items. For CVRP, each instance contains Euclidean customer locations, customer demands, a depot, and a vehicle-capacity constraint. Discovery uses uniform and clustered generators with 50 –100 

customers, and held-out transfer evaluation uses 

150 –300 customers generated with unseen random seeds. For flow-shop scheduling, each instance speci-fies processing times for a set of jobs across ma-chines, and the objective is to construct a job per-mutation with low makespan. Discovery uses 20 –

50 jobs from uniform, correlated, and bottleneck processing-time families, and transfer evaluation uses larger instances with 80 –120 jobs. All tasks are formulated as program-synthesis problems in which the LLM proposes a complete Python heuristic satisfying a fixed function signa-ture. Prompts include the task definition, interface requirements, validation constraints, the parent pro-gram, and a compact summary of high-loss cases. No held-out test instances are used for discovery or prompt construction. Hyperparameters are fixed before final evaluation, and the same discovery and evaluation splits are used for CVaR-RMD, Mean-RMD, and all implemented baselines. 

A.2 Preprocessing and Evaluation 

Generated programs are parsed as Python functions and checked against the required interface before execution. We reject malformed code, missing functions, invalid return types, infeasible solutions, runtime exceptions, and timeouts. Such cases re-ceive the fixed failure loss ℓfail = 25 . Candidate execution is capped at 2 seconds per training in-stance and 10 seconds per transfer instance. Losses are normalized by task-specific reference procedures to make results comparable across in-stance families and scales. Bin-packing losses are normalized by the continuous bin-capacity lower bound, CVRP losses by Clarke–Wright savings with deterministic 2-opt, and flow-shop losses by the NEH heuristic. Lower values indicate better performance. At each generation, candidates are evaluated on a shared batch of 64 environments. Loss histories are accumulated across generations until archived candidates have at least 512 evaluations, which is used to stabilize empirical tail estimates. CVaR-RMD ranks candidates by empirical CVaR q with default tail level q = 0 .90 , while Mean-RMD ranks candidates by empirical mean loss. For a loss sam-ple {ℓi}ni=1 , empirical CVaR 0.90 is computed as the average loss over the largest 10% of observed losses. Each method is run with 15 independent discov-ery seeds, {0, 1, . . . , 14 }. After discovery, the best archived program from each seed is frozen and evaluated on common held-out environments. We report mean loss, empirical CVaR 0.90 , maximum loss, and failure rate. Aggregate results are reported as mean, standard deviation, and standardized ef-fect size across discovery seeds. Statistical sig-nificance is assessed using paired two-sided boot-strap tests with 10 ,000 resamples over archived programs and shared test environments. Baselines include handcrafted heuristics, direct LLM sampling, FunSearch-style discovery, EoH-style discovery, and Mean-RMD. All discovery 12 baselines use the same generators, parsers, val-idators, mutation budget, population size, archive mechanism, and held-out evaluation protocol. 

B Implementation Details 

All methods are implemented in a common Python infrastructure with fixed package versions and sand-boxed execution for generated programs. CVaR-RMD and Mean-RMD share the same evolutionary loop and differ only in the candidate-ranking statis-tic. The population contains N = 24 programs, 

M = 8 parents are eligible for mutation at each generation, and each generation produces K = 16 

offspring. Each discovery run lasts 40 generations, yielding 640 LLM-generated offspring per seed and at most 664 evaluated candidate programs per run. The mutation model is GPT-4o. We use tempera-ture 0.7, top-p 0.95 , maximum output length 2048 ,and up to three retries for malformed code. The model is used only during discovery. Final held-out evaluation executes frozen programs without additional LLM calls. The default CVaR-RMD configuration uses tail level q = 0 .90 , batch size 64 , accumulated loss his-tories, tail-case summaries in prompts, and failure penalty ℓfail = 25 . Sensitivity experiments vary the batch size, tail level, and failure penalty while keeping all other components fixed. Experiments are run on an AMD EPYC 7543 workstation with 256GB RAM. Average wall-clock times for CVaR-RMD are 5.9 hours for bin packing, 

8.6 hours for CVRP, and 7.3 hours for flow-shop scheduling. Mean-RMD uses the same number of LLM calls, generated offspring, validation checks, and candidate executions; its runtime is within 4% 

of CVaR-RMD. The additional cost of CVaR-RMD is limited to sorting accumulated loss vectors for empirical tail-risk estimation.

D.5 Few-shot / Demonstration Prompts 

No few-shot natural-language demonstrations are used in the default CVaR-RMD prompt. Candidate source programs may appear as parent programs or archive programs in mutation prompts, but they are executable program inputs rather than labeled few-shot examples. Therefore, the discovery pro-tocol does not include a separate few-shot prompt template. 

D.6 Reasoning or Rationale Prompts 

No separate chain-of-thought, rationale-generation, or self-explanation prompt is used. The model is instructed to return code only. Diagnostic in-formation from high-loss environments is com-puted deterministically by the evaluation har-ness and inserted into the mutation prompt as 

{TAIL_RECORDS} .

E Case Study 

We provide qualitative examples to illustrate how CVaR-RMD changes the type of heuristics selected by the outer loop. The goal of this case study is not to compare aggregate scores, but to inspect the generated programs and the failure modes that the selection objective encourages or suppresses. In all examples, the LLM is used only as a muta-tion operator: it proposes executable variants of a fixed heuristic interface, while the evaluator ac-cepts or rejects candidates according to their behav-ior over heterogeneous instances, stochastic seeds, and problem scales. 

Bin packing: avoiding brittle “perfect-fit” behavior. A representative mean-selected bin-packing heuristic produced a compact rule that pri-oritized immediate utilization. Its core decision was to place each item into the bin that would leave the smallest remaining capacity after insertion: 

“For each item, scan all open bins and choose the feasible bin with minimum residual capacity. If no bin fits, open a new bin.” 

The code was syntactically clean and often behaved well on regular instances where item sizes admitted many near-perfect combinations. However, inspec-tion of its outputs revealed a brittle pattern. On instances containing a mixture of medium-sized and small items, the heuristic repeatedly consumed small residual spaces too early. Later medium items then failed to fit into existing bins, forcing the program to open additional bins. Observed mutation pattern. Across tasks, we observed a common qualitative pattern in the pro-grams selected by CVaR-RMD. Mean-based se-lection often retained concise greedy rules whose outputs looked strong on regular instances but de-pended heavily on favorable input structure. CVaR-RMD preserved the interpretable greedy back-bone while adding small protective mechanisms: slack preservation in bin packing, demand-aware capacity management in CVRP, and bottleneck-aware dispatching in flow-shop scheduling. These changes were not arbitrary complexity. They were localized modifications that directly addressed rare but consequential failure modes exposed by evalu-ating each candidate over varied environments. This case study supports the central claim of the paper: changing the outer-loop selection pres-sure can change the qualitative character of LLM-generated heuristics. CVaR-RMD does not require the language model to understand robustness ex-plicitly.
