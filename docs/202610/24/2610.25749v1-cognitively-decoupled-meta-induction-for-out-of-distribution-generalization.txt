Abstract 

In-context learning (ICL) tackles downstream tasks conditioned on demonstrations. This paradigm relies heavily on neighbor-based matching between queries and demonstra-tions, limiting out-of-distribution (OOD) per-formance. For better generalization, rule-based inductive reasoning derives task-level regularities, which we term task-level induc-tive representations (TIR), before directly an-swering. However, existing methods lack generalizable inductive ability across diverse tasks. Inspired by meta-learning, we propose Cognitive-Decoupling meta-Induction (CoDI) for TIR-based inductive reasoning on general tasks. CoDI comprises a Cognitive-Decoupling Framework for query-blind TIR induction, and a Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learning 

method for cross-task inducer optimization. Empirically, CoDI achieves substantial per-formance gains on five ID and OOD bench-marks, and preserves OOD gains without ex-plicit induction at inference. Code is available at https://github.com/Yuqian-Wang2277/ codi-meta-induction .

1 Introduction 

In-context learning (ICL) is a prevalent paradigm for tackling downstream tasks, allowing large lan-guage models (LLMs) to solve new queries condi-tioned on limited contextual labeled demonstra-tions (Brown et al., 2020). Despite achieving promising performance, ICL can rely heavily on demonstration format, label space, and nearest-neighbor-style similarity between queries and demonstrations (Min et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2025). Under distribution shifts and unseen task functions, such similarity-dominated behavior often suffers substantial performance degradation (Wang et al., 2025). To address this limitation, rule-based induc-tive reasoning derives generalizable principles for

straight answering, which we name task-level in-ductive representations (TIR). The difference be-tween ICL and TIR-based reasoning is shown in Figure 1. The former relies on surface matching with certain demonstrations, while the latter learns universal task patterns from all supports for better transfer. Some works elicit intermediate reason-ing through chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting and structured reasoning plans (Wei et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). Yet these reasoning structures are usu-ally produced at inference time for the prompted query or task context, so they are not learned as a reusable cross-task inducer (Li et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024). A complementary direction pursues task-level rules via automated prompt optimiza-tion (Zhou et al., 2023; Fernando et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026), but such methods optimize prompts per task or system and must re-optimize when the task setting changes. In comparison, human concept learning can infer compact, program-like rules from very few exam-ples (Lake et al., 2015), suggesting an induction-arXiv:2610.25749v1 [cs.LG] 19 Oct 2026 before-straight-answering pattern even on unfamil-iar tasks. We refer to this cross-task TIR inference capability as meta-induction. Recent work finds that LLMs are weak at this capability (Li et al., 2025), as their inductive behavior is dominated by neighbor-based matching rather than reusable ab-stract rules. However, instilling this capacity in LLMs presents two challenges: i) ensuring meta-induction generalizes across tasks without overfit-ting to instance-specific patterns or task-local rules; ii) learning TIR in the absence of ground-truth la-bels and established evaluation criteria. To address these challenges, we propose 

Co gnitively Decoupled meta-Induction ( CoDI ), a TIR-based inductive reasoning method on general tasks. As shown in Figure 2, CoDI comprises a 

Cognitive-Decoupling Framework and Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learning method. In the Cognitive-Decoupling Framework, a query-blind inducer in-duces TIR solely from demonstrations, and an ex-ecutor executes TIR for each query. Based on the above framework, Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learning optimizes the inducer across tasks via meta-RL, guided by execution feedback without ground-truth TIR labels, where an inner loop ap-plies candidate TIR and an outer loop refines induc-tion via two complementary rewards for extrinsic correctness and intrinsic certainty. Experiments on BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023), BIG-Bench (Srivastava et al., 2023), MATH-500 (Light-man et al., 2024), StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021), and ReClor (Yu et al., 2020) show that CoDI (Qwen3-4B) outperforms ICL on in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluations. On BIG-Bench, CoDI achieves +120% and +18% 

gains on ID and OOD splits, respectively. Further-more, a smaller trained inducer surpasses a larger untrained one on OOD tasks, confirming TIR qual-ity as an independently optimizable capability or-thogonal to model scale. Notably, OOD gains per-sist even without the induction step at inference, indicating that meta-inductive training internalizes inductive priors into model parameters. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We formulate TIR-based inductive reasoning as a learning problem that requires inferring reusable task rules from support examples alone before execution. • We present CoDI to instill meta-induction with-out TIR supervision, decoupling rule induction from straight answering and optimizing induc-ers across tasks via reinforcement learning on execution feedback. • Experiments on five benchmarks show CoDI outperforming few-shot ICL in- and out-of-distribution, with smaller trained inducers beat-ing larger untrained ones and OOD gains persist-ing without inference-time induction.

3 CoDI 

In this section, we present the proposed CoDI for TIR-based inductive reasoning, built on a cognitive-decoupling architecture (§3.2) and a dual-loop meta-inductive learning scheme (§3.3). 

3.1 Overview 

The proposed CoDI employs a cognitive-decoupling induction–execution framework: the query-blind inducer abstracts TIR and the executor solves new query based on it. To enhance the inductive reasoning ability for general tasks, a dual-loop meta-inductive learning approach is proposed: the inner loop is executed based on TIR, and improves answers through ICL; the outer loop is based on the feedback signal from the inner loop execution, and optimizes the abstraction of TIR through RL. This design is overviewed in Figure 2. Figure 2: Overview of CoDI. The Cognitive-Decoupling Framework induces query-blind TIR with SG and executes each query with AS. Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learning applies candidate TIRs in the inner loop and optimizes SG across tasks in the outer loop via GRPO with EIR and ICR.

3.3 Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learning 

Building on the cognitive-decoupling framework, dual-loop meta-inductive learning evolves TIR when no strategy labels are available. Execution under a frozen AS provides a weak, task-agnostic supervision signal for πSG . πSG is first warmed up via SFT on teacher demonstrations to satisfy the schema. During RL, format-invalid rollouts receive zero reward and are excluded from AS execution.

Because the gap persists after untuned scaling satu-rates, TIR quality functions as a distinct optimiza-tion target orthogonal to model size.

Strategies favor rule-based modes. We further find that performance gains concentrate on rule-oriented induction modes rather than extending uniformly across all strategy types. Gemini-3-flash scores Pre- and Post-CoDI strategies on six induction dimensions (Appendix C). Post-CoDI im-proves on Constraint, Procedural and Schema but declines on Analogical and Pattern (Figure 7). This asymmetric split is consistent with classic distinc-tions between rule- and schema-based induction and analogical mapping (Holland et al., 1986; Gen-tner, 1983); we treat pattern extrapolation as an op-erational category, and CoDI’s execution feedback further biases the inducer toward explicit proce-dures. Accordingly, the Analogical and Pattern de-clines mark a disclosed capability boundary rather than a uniform claim of meta-inductive superiority across all reasoning types. Taken together with the trace analysis, the shifts support rule-driven over example-recitation induction.

Case study. Table 4 instantiates the trace and induction-mode shifts above on one BIG-Bench Navigate episode with K=3 supports and a test query unseen in the supports. FS treats the query as most similar to Ex3 and reuses its Yes label, which aligns with the example-recitation pattern in Figure 5 and with surface matching in §1. S-FS induces a strategy without seeing the query, yet its rule restates the Ex3 trajectory and yields the same wrong Yes answer, so query-blind decoupling alone does not yield a task-family rule. Accordingly, this case supports the hypothesis that generalization to unseen instances depends on rules verified across all supports rather than on query decoupling or surface matching alone.

6 Related Work 

Meta-learning and in-context adaptation. 

MAML (Finn et al., 2017) adapts parameters via gradient updates without explicit cross-instance strategies. MetaICL (Min et al., 2022a) likewise meta-trains in-context learning at the parameter level. Mirage (Li et al., 2025) shows that ICL often follows neighbor-based matching rather than recovering reusable abstract rules. Motivated by these limitations, our work introduces aCognitive-Decoupling Framework for discrete, query-blind TIR, decoupling induction from frozen execution. 

Induction from examples and prompt opti-mization. StrategyLLM (Gao et al., 2024) in-duces and selects task-level strategies, while Self-Discover (Zhou et al., 2024) composes reasoning structures for prompted tasks. Induct-Learn (Chen et al., 2024) and LEAP (Zhang et al., 2024) derive instructions or principles from few-shot supports, but remain inference-time prompting methods rather than a trained cross-task inducer. CoT (Wei et al., 2022), STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022), and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) improve reason-ing traces or reasoning policies; GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) optimizes prompts via reflective search at the task level. Our work builds upon this broader paradigm with Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learn-ing, optimizing query-blind TIR via cross-task RL and EIR ×ICR feedback for reasoning episodes. 

ICL under distribution shift. Standard ICL is highly sensitive to demonstration choice, for-mat, label space, and input distribution, which can degrade under task-family or domain mis-match (Min et al., 2022b; Schoch and Ji, 2025; de Wynter, 2026). Inference-time remedies such as CCL (Byun et al., 2025) improve OOD robustness via domain-invariant demonstration selection but remain per-query interventions rather than training a reusable inducer. Mechanistic and theoretical work further suggests that OOD generalization in ICL depends strongly on task-distribution cover-age, low-test-error preferences, and subspace align-ment (Wang et al., 2025; Goddard et al., 2025; Kwon et al., 2026). In contrast, CoDI trains a query-blind inducer to learn transferable TIR from supports via cross-task RL, offering a reusable alter-native to inference-time demonstration engineering for OOD few-shot generalization. 

7 Conclusion 

Few-shot OOD generalization demands explicit, optimizable task-level induction beyond execution scale. To this end, we presented CoDI, combining a Cognitive-Decoupling Framework for query-blind TIR with Dual-Loop Meta-Inductive Learning for label-free cross-task inducer optimization. Experi-ments show that neither parameter-level adaptation nor query-blind decoupling alone yields reusable TIR, yet TIR-aligned training drives systematic OOD gains across benchmarks. Accordingly, meta-induction constitutes a trainable capability comple-mentary to model scale. Future work may eval-uate finer task-level shifts, strengthen analogical and pattern induction, and study strategy–solver co-adaptation across languages and modalities. 

Limitations 

Computational cost. Each training episode re-quires G × M = 32 frozen AS forward passes, roughly 32 × standard SFT compute. Training com-pletes in 12–24 hours on 8 ×A800 GPUs; future work may reduce cost via adaptive rollout sched-ules, cached solver verification, or distillation from the trained SG. 

Benchmark scope and confounds. We evalu-ate only on English text-reasoning benchmarks, leaving broader language, modality, and distribu-tion shifts untested. Gemini-3-flash both proposes SFT strategies and annotates mechanistic traces, and ID/OOD splits share the StrategyBench con-struction pipeline, but GPT-5 cross-annotation (Ap-pendix E) and gains on MATH-500, StrategyQA, and ReClor partially address these concerns. 

Strategy–solver coupling. We fix AS during training and update SG via execution feedback for stable credit assignment. TIR is natural-language and interface-decoupled, yet effective quality de-pends on the training-time executor. Under a fixed executor (Table 5), substitution gains favor rule-governed over analogical or pattern modes and support portable structure, while strategy–solver co-adaptation remains out of scope.

I StrategyBench Dataset 

StrategyBench evaluates strategy induction and strategy application in in-context learning. It is built from BIG-Bench (Srivastava et al., 2023) by task filtering, format standardization, categoriza-tion, and split construction. The target setting is few-shot induction of a reusable solution method and application to unseen queries. 

Data format. Each example uses a unified input– output structure. The input field holds the task instruction and query instance. The output field holds the target answer for automatic evalua-tion. Strategy-based episodes also include a small demonstration set used either to induce a strategy or as in-context examples for direct inference. Con-struction scripts and split files are in the public code release cited at the end of this section. 

I.1 Five-Criterion Task Filtering 

To keep training data suitable for strategy induction and reinforcement learning, BIG-Bench tasks are retained only if they meet five operational criteria. 1. Objective evaluability. Answers must be au-tomatically checkable. Tasks with subjective judgments, open-ended opinions, ethical pref-erences, or unstable answer boundaries are ex-cluded. 2. Format stability. After standardization, input– output formats must support a consistent episode layout for demonstrations, strategies, and predictions. 3. Rule inductibility. A small support set must suffice to infer a pattern, rule, mapping, or pro-cedure. Tasks that mainly require memorized facts or arbitrary labels are excluded. 4. Low external-knowledge dependency. The query plus demonstrations should be sufficient. Tasks that require substantial world knowledge are excluded so that scores reflect induction rather than recall. 5. Transfer potential. The induced method must apply to new instances of the same family or under a documented shift. Unsuitable tasks are removed. Retained tasks are standardized into training, validation, and test episodes. As BIG-Bench-derived synthetic rea-soning tasks, our data do not contain personally identifying information; tasks with subjective or potentially sensitive content were excluded during filtering. 

Teacher scoring. Retained tasks are processed for SFT warm-up. Gemini-3-flash proposes can-didate reference strategies, and only strategies that pass execution-accuracy filtering on the frozen solver enter Phase 1 SFT. Task filtering, catego-rization, and ID/OOD split assignment use Qwen3-32B scores with author spot-checks, not Gemini. 

Code and splits. The public code release at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ StrategyBench-D53C/ contains the five-criterion filtering pipeline, the standardized episode format, and the ID/OOD split definitions used in §4.1. 

J Task-Level ID/OOD Partition 

J.1 In-Distribution (ID) Split 

The ID split comprises tasks or subtasks present in the training data, with test instances held out from training instances. It evaluates whether a model can induce and apply strategies when task rules, input–output formats, and demonstration patterns resemble those seen during training. 

J.2 Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Split 

The OOD split is created by completely holding out certain tasks, subtasks, or task categories from training and using them only for evaluation. OOD examples differ more strongly from training data in task source, input–output format, reasoning struc-ture, or required capabilities. 

Granularity levels. StrategyBench also defines finer diagnostic splits. OOD-subtask holds out subtasks under related sources. OOD-task holds out tasks with related ability requirements. OOD-category holds out coarse categories with larger shifts. BBH denotes selected Big-Bench Hard tasks used as an external diagnostic, reported as a sep-arate column in Table 1 rather than as the main OOD aggregate. You are an expert in inductive reasoning. Score the following model-generated strategy on six theoretically grounded modes of induction. Each mode is rated on an integer scale 0–5; scores must be justified by direct reference to the strategy text. Task description: {task} Strategy to score: {strategy text} 

Six induction modes: 

1. Compositional — Does the strategy combine multiple atomic sub-rules into a composite procedure (e.g. chaining conditions, nesting operations)? 2. Analogical — Does the strategy draw structural correspondences across examples, mapping shared relational patterns rather than surface features? 3. Pattern Extrapolation — Does the strategy extract a pattern that visibly extends beyond the demonstrated instances (e.g. to longer sequences, new value ranges, unseen entities)? 4. Procedural — Does the strategy abstract a step-wise algorithm from input–output pairs (clear ordering, state updates, termination conditions)? 5. Constraint-based — Does the strategy identify and articulate implicit constraints, preconditions, or boundary conditions (e.g. when the rule does not apply)? 6. Schema-induction — Does the strategy explicitly classify the task type itself, recognising the meta-level structure (e.g. “this is a coordinate-tracking problem”)? 

0–5 scoring rubric (applied per dimension): 

• 0 — Absent: no evidence of this mode in the strategy. • 1 — Hinted: a fragment alludes to it but is not developed. • 2 — Partial: present but incomplete or ambiguous. • 3 — Adequate: clearly present, executable, but minimal. • 4 — Strong: well-developed, multi-step or multi-condition. • 5 — Exemplary: cleanly abstracted, generalisable, with explicit scope statements. 

Calibration constraints: (i) Scores must reference specific text spans (quote the supporting sentence in evidence ). (ii) If a mode is structurally inapplicable to the task type, mark as N/A rather than 0. (iii) Modes are scored independently; high score on one does not imply high score on others.
