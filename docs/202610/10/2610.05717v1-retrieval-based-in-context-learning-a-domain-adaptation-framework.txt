# Retrieval-Based In-Context Learning: A Domain Adaptation Framework 

## Yilun Zhu ∗ Naihao Deng † Yingcong Li ‡ Naichen Shi § Clayton Scott ¶

Abstract 

In-context retrieval (ICR) is a retrieval-based form of in-context learning (ICL) in which demonstrations are retrieved from a source database based on similarity to the query, rather than sampled independently. In this work, we formulate ICR as a type of domain adaptation problem, where the source distribution P

of the database may differ from the target distribution Q of the test query-label pair. We investigate the performance of ICR under a flexible class of distributional shifts that substantially extends prior work [Li et al., 2024b, Guo et al., 2025], and establish theoretical guarantees that quantify the benefits and pitfalls of this learning paradigm. Our theory is verified by experiments on synthetic and language tasks. 

# 1 Introduction 

In-context learning (ICL) is the ability of a pretrained model to predict the label of a query point using input-output demonstrations provided in its context, without updating the model’s parameters. In most theoretical models of ICL, these demonstrations are modeled as i.i.d. draws from the same distribution that governs the query-label pair [Garg et al., 2022, Guo et al., 2024]. Throughout this paper, ICL refers to this standard setting with i.i.d. demonstrations. In this work, we consider retrieval-based ICL, in which the input-output demonstrations are selected as a function of the query point. In particular, given access to a large database of demonstrations, a retrieval algorithm selects examples whose feature representations are similar to that of the query point. It has been observed empirically that such retrieved demonstrations can substantially improve performance over randomly sampled demonstrations [Liu et al., 2022, Rubin et al., 2022, Izacard et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2024]. Following recent work, we refer to this setting as in-context retrieval (ICR) [Luo et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2025]. Prior work has begun to explain the benefits of ICR theoretically, but several important regimes remain unexplored. In particular, existing statistical models [Li et al., 2024b, Guo et al., 2025] are restricted to linear-Gaussian settings. Moreover, while these models allow the retrieved demonstrations and the query-label pair to be governed by different marginal distributions, the regression function remains the same: in other words, they model covariate shift , but not a posterior drift that changes the optimal predictor. We introduce a nonparametric statistical framework for binary classification that casts ICR as a domain adaptation problem admitting both covariate shift and posterior drift. The source domain governs the corpus of demonstrations, the target domain governs the query-label pair, and the predictor operates on a query-dependent subset of retrieved source examples. Throughout, we treat the pretrained model as frozen and study the statistical effect of the demonstrations selected for its context, abstracting away from transformer-level mechanisms for implementing in-context learning. Within this framework, we characterize the performance of ICR in terms of four key quantities: the context size n, the corpus size m, the degree of posterior drift, and distributional properties of the domains (in particular, the smoothness of the class posterior). # 2 Related work 

In-context learning. In-context learning (ICL) became prominent with GPT-3 [Brown et al., 2020] and is now a standard mechanism for adapting language models at inference time [Dong et al., 2024, Agarwal et al., 2024]. Beyond natural language, this success has also extended to other modalities, such as tabular data, where tabular foundation models [Hollmann et al., 2022, 2025, Qu et al., 2025, Ma et al., 2025] represent a compelling application of ICL. A growing theoretical literature analyzes ICL through the behavior of transformers, implicit inference, and task generalization [Ahn et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2024, Xie et al., 2022, Li et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2024, Bai et al., 2023], and related work has also examined more challenging regimes such as out-of-distribution tasks, test-time training, and noisy in-context demonstrations [Kwon et al., 2025, Gozeten et al., 2025, Cheng et al., 2025]. These studies provide useful tools for understanding ICL with i.i.d. demonstrations, but they do not study the retrieval-based setup. 

Retrieval-augmented generation. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has become a central paradigm for enriching language models with external knowledge by conditioning generation on retrieved text [Lewis et al., 2020, Izacard and Grave, 2021, Borgeaud et al., 2022, Es et al., 2024, Gupta et al., 2024], and has seen a wide range of applications beyond language-specific tasks [Chen et al., 2025]. Recent work has improved many components of RAG systems [Chen et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2024, Asai et al., 2024, Li et al., 2024a, Xiang et al., 2026]. Most of the literature on RAG is empirical and method-driven. In this work, we provide theoretical insight into RAG by analyzing ICR, where query-dependent in-context demonstrations are retrieved. 

In-context retrieval. The basic motivation for in-context retrieval (ICR) is that the empirical performance of ICL depends heavily on the choice of in-context examples [Liu et al., 2022]. In particular, retrieval-based prompt selection has been shown to outperform randomly sampled demonstrations [Rubin et al., 2022]. This idea has been broadly validated across a range of settings [Luo et al., 2024], including in-domain labeled data [Izacard et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2024, Ye et al., 2023], cross-domain or cross-lingual retrieval [Cheng et al., 2023, Shi et al., 2022], and LLM-generated demonstrations [Zhang et al., 2023, Li and Qiu, 2023]. Most prior work on ICR is empirical, with limited theoretical understanding of when and why retrieval helps. Two recent works begin to fill this gap: Li et al. [2024b] model demonstrations whose features are 

α-correlated with the query and show that higher correlation yields improved ICR performance, while Guo et al. [2025] analyze the covariate shift between i.i.d. training-time demonstrations and retrieved test-time demonstrations. Both works study linear-regression settings where source and target share the same regression function. In this work, in contrast, we develop a nonparametric framework for binary classification that admits general domain shift and characterizes when retrieval helps versus hurts. Additional connections to the literature are made below, including to domain adaptation theory.

## 3.3 Connections to prior ICR and domain adaptation theory 

The two prior theoretical works on ICR most closely related to ours are Li et al. [2024b] and Guo et al. [2025]. However, both consider Gaussian linear regression ( Y = R) as their data model, whereas our analysis targets binary classification ( Y = {0, 1}). Despite the difference in statistical model, each can be situated within our 

(P, Q ) framework. 

Li et al. [2024b]. Their data model is Gaussian linear regression with a single latent weight vector w

shared between the query and all demonstrations: 

Xq ∼ N (0 , I d), w ∼ N (0 , I d), Yq = w⊤Xq + εq ,Xi | Xq ∼ N (αX q , (1 − α2)Id), Yi = w⊤Xi + εi, i = 1 , . . . , n,

N (0 , I d) ⊗ N (w⊤·, σ 2) (i.e. QX = N (0 , I d) and QY |X=x = N (w⊤x, σ 2)). Marginalizing Xi over Xq gives 

PX = N (0 , I d) = QX , and the posterior is the same: PY |X = QY |X . Thus P = Q; so their model captures the effect of retrieval, but not a domain shift. 4Guo et al. [2025]. They also use a Gaussian linear model with a shared latent weight vector, but focus on the covariate shift induced by retrieval offsets: 

Xq ∼ N (0 , I d), w ∼ N (0 , I d), Yq = w⊤Xq + εq ,Xi = Xq + ri, Yi = w⊤Xi + εi, i = 1 , . . . , n, 

where ri is the retrieval residual and εq , ε i’s are random noise. In our language: QX = N (0 , I d), while the marginal of Xi is shifted by ri, so PX̸ = QX in general. Nevertheless, the regression function is again shared, 

E[Y | X = x] = w⊤x under both P and Q; the retrieved labels may carry a different noise variance, which leaves the optimal predictor unchanged. The effect they study is therefore covariate shift (PX̸ = QX ) together with label noise, not a change in the optimal predictor. 

Limitations of prior ICR theory. Although both papers study ICR, they focus primarily on the relevance between the features Xi and Xq , and assume that demonstrations and query share the same regression function. In domain adaptation terms, there is no posterior drift between P and Q that alters the optimal predictor. In practice, however, the source corpus may come from a different distribution with PY |X̸ = QY |X

Relation to existing domain adaptation theory. The primary technical tool we use to model domain adaptation is the notion of relative signal strength (RSS) introduced by Zhu et al. [2024]. RSS extends the prior model of Cai and Wei [2021] by allowing the source and target Bayes classifiers to differ on a set of positive measure. Similarly, Fan et al. [2025] quantify source unreliability through an ambiguity level that weights the target mass of unreliable regions by the target margin. As seen below in Lemma 1, integrating the pointwise risk inequality implied by RSS gives a risk-level inequality in the spirit of the transfer exponent of Hanneke and Kpotufe [2019] and the modulus of transfer of Hanneke and Kpotufe [2025]. The RSS-based framework differs substantially from discrepancy-based approaches to domain adaptation [Ben-David et al., 2006, Mansour et al., 2009], which characterize domain relatedness through global distributional discrepancies rather than pointwise relationships between the source and target posteriors. Finally, none of the aforementioned works consider retrieval-based learners , the defining feature of ICR. 

# 4 Performance guarantee for in-context retrieval 

Our analysis proceeds in two steps. First, we convert the target-domain excess risk to a source-domain excess risk and establish a lower bound showing that the bias induced by posterior drift is unavoidable; these results are independent of any particular learning algorithm. Second, we derive rates of convergence for a nearest-neighbor type retrieval-based learner for ICR, and contrast with the rates of a comparable nearest-neighbor learner for ICL.

## 4.2 Finite sample analysis of ICR

It shows a fundamental bias-variance trade-off: provided the source corpus is sufficiently large, the ICR bound decays strictly faster in n than the ICL bound, but under posterior drift ICR suffers an irreducible bias ε; ICL is consistent but converges at a slower rate. 

Comparison of ICL and ICR rates. The two rates in Theorem 1 reflect a trade-off between sample efficiency and label correctness. In ICL, the labels are drawn from the correct posterior ηQ, but only demonstrations near the query are informative, so the vote uses just the k ≍ n2α/ (2 α+1) ≪ n nearest ones. In ICR, the retriever selects from a corpus of size m ≫ n, so all n demonstrations in the context are close to the query and all n labels are used in the vote, yielding the faster rate. The price is that these labels are drawn from the source posterior ηP rather than ηQ, and the irreducible bias ε quantifies how wrong that posterior may be. We cast in-context retrieval as domain adaptation with a retrieval-based learner and derive finite-sample excess-risk bounds for both ICL and ICR. The bounds identify when ICR strictly outperforms ICL, and an irreducible bias that prevents ICR from being asymptotically consistent under posterior drift. Synthetic and sentiment-classification experiments confirm these predictions and carry a practical takeaway: ICR is not always preferable, naïvely pooling all available source data into the retrieval corpus is not a safe default, and a smaller, well-aligned corpus can be better than a larger one. The limitation of our work lies in that our analysis is specialized to binary classification and treats the in-context learners (induced by LLMs) as nearest-neighbor-style estimators.

## C.3 Sentiment classification: full setup 

We use the sentiment classification dataset of Díaz et al. [2018], a language benchmark with substan-tial annotator-induced posterior drift: each sentence is labeled by 4–5 individuals with ≈ 40% label disagreement. The training split has 59,235 annotated examples from 1,481 annotators (up to 40 per annotator); the test split has 1,419 annotated examples from 878 annotators. This five-class classifica-tion ( Y = {very negative, somewhat negative, neutral, somewhat positive, very positive }) experiment quali-tatively tests the implications of our binary classification theory. Each test annotator is treated as a target distribution Q. Sentences are encoded with BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5 [Xiao et al., 2023] and retrieved by Euclidean distance; the in-context learner is Qwen3.5-4B [Qwen Team, 2026]. ICL demonstrations are sampled uniformly without replacement from the annotator’s own training data. Errors are averaged over test annotators and over 5 independent retrieval/sampling runs. 

Posterior drift depends on the choice of source corpus. The same dataset can yield very different source distributions P depending on how the retrieval corpus is assembled, and this choice directly controls the drift relative to each test annotator’s Q: (1) self — only the test annotator’s own training data ( P = Q,no drift, but the corpus is small, ≤ 40 examples); (2) self + 100 random others — mild drift, moderately larger corpus; (3) all 1,481 annotators — the entire training set, severe drift but the largest corpus; (4) others only — the test annotator deliberately excluded, extreme drift. This sweep makes explicit a tradeoff that practitioners face whenever labels come from heterogeneous sources: a tightly curated corpus minimizes posterior drift at the cost of size, while pooling all available data maximizes corpus size at the cost of drift. Notably, much of the existing ICR literature treats the entire training set as the retrieval corpus by default, with no explicit accounting for posterior drift—which, as our results show, is exactly the regime where ICR can fail catastrophically. 

Results. ICR strongly outperforms ICL when P = Q and remains preferable under mild posterior drift for 

n ≤ 16 . Under severe drift the ICR error stays near 50% while ICL keeps improving with n; under extreme drift, ICR error rises to ≈ 63% , well above the ICL baseline. This finding not only verifies our theory, but also has a practical takeaway: naïvely pooling all available data is not a safe default—a smaller, well-aligned corpus can dominate a larger, drifted one. 

Effect of model scale. Figure 14 repeats the experiment with the stronger Qwen3.5-27B model alongside Qwen3.5-4B. The 27B model is markedly better in the few-shot regime where the corpus is aligned with the target (e.g., 0.12 vs. 0.26 at n = 4 under P = Q), yet its error under severe and extreme drift is essentially 31 unchanged from the 4B model: model capacity does not remove the irreducible drift-induced bias, consistent with Proposition 1.
