# Prompt Sensitivity in Tool-Augmented Model Comparisons 

## Mateusz Modrzejewski , Tomasz Radzikowski 

## Institute of Computer Science, Warsaw University of Technology {mateusz.modrzejewski, tomasz.radzikowski}@pw.edu.pl 

## Abstract 

Language models are compared on question-answering benchmarks that pose each question in one wording, and a reworded question can change which of two models scores higher. We ask whether giving the models tools makes such comparisons more stable. An accuracy gain cannot answer this, because tools change several steps between the question and the scored answer at once. We pro-pose a matched protocol that separates those steps: the answer readout, reasoning, the decision to call, the tool’s answer, and the report supplied without a call. We apply it to six open models in five do-mains under five wordings. We find that tools raise accuracy where a program answers the question and widen the wording sensitivity of many pairwise comparisons. The widening follows how often a model calls, and call frequency follows the word-ing for some models and not others. It also depends on the interface and the instructions: a native tool-calling interface and a second tool description leave no detected widening in molecules, and sensitiv-ity persists where tools return intermediate results. These findings show that deterministic reports do not guarantee stable comparisons. We recommend reporting call frequency and comparison stability under several wordings beside accuracy. 

## 1 Introduction 

A benchmark asks a model a question in one wording and counts correct answers. The same question in another word-ing gives another score. The difference between two models, which decides their order in a ranking, moves with the word-ing by as much as it moves with a change of model [Sclar 

et al. , 2023; Mizrahi et al. , 2023]. Multi-prompt evaluation proposes to report several wordings. Tool use raises a further question. In a tool loop of the kind ReAct introduced [Yao et al. , 2022], the model writes a call, a program answers, and the model reads the answer before it decides. The program’s an-swer is the same under every wording, but nothing guarantees that the model makes the same call under every wording. One molecule item shows the difference. Under the remaining wording, which shows the molecule before the question, it counts the atoms in prose, makes no call before its generation budget ends, and the letter read at the appended answer line is wrong. When the tool’s report is printed in the prompt, the model answers correctly under all five wordings. The report is the same in every case. What differs is whether the model obtains it. An accuracy gain under tools cannot show whether tools stabilize comparisons, because several steps change at once. The answer is no longer read from the probability of the first option letter, a common readout that for instruction-tuned models often disagrees with the letter the model writes [Wang 

et al. , 2024]. The model writes text before it answers. It decides whether to call a tool after reading the wording. It receives the tools’ reports. Any of these steps can add or re-move wording sensitivity. A comparison of the same model with and without tools mixes them. We separate the steps on the same items with matched con-ditions: the first-token read, an answer read at an answer line, reasoning before that line, the tools’ reports supplied in the prompt, a plain-text tool loop over deterministic programs, the same loop with tools that answer “no information”, and arXiv:2610.31148v1 [cs.CL] 22 Oct 2026 for three models the native tool-calling interface. Every con-dition runs under five wordings of the instruction and under each written form of the input. For each pair of models we measure the wording spread, the range of their accuracy dif-ference across the wordings. The tools’ information and the model’s call behavior can affect this spread in different ways. Supplying the report under every wording measures the com-parison with direct access to the information. Letting the model decide whether to obtain it exposes the comparison to changes in calling as well. We compare the two and intervene on the interface, the instructions, the budget and the reports’ reliability to see when the spread changes. Tools raise accuracy where a program answers the ques-tion and still widen the wording spread of many model pairs against reasoning without tools (Figure 1). In molecules the median spread of a pair is 0.17 with the plain-text loop and 0.02 with the reports supplied. There, a model that calls is al-most always right, so its accuracy follows its call frequency, and for several models the call frequency follows the word-ing. The widening depends on the interface and the instruc-tions. In molecules, the native interface and a second tool description leave no detected widening in the model groups tested. Forcing one call and removing the wordings’ out-put clause help some models and not others. Wrong reports shrink the widening while the call frequencies stay. Sensitiv-ity persists under the native interface where the tools return intermediate results. Reading the answer from the first token adds sensitivity of its own on graphs, which an answer line removes. 

Scope. We study open models of 4B to 72B parameters with greedy decoding, a plain-text tool loop and, for three model families, the native interface, wordings in English, and deter-ministic programs that read a canonical form of the item. The quantity is the stability of a comparison between two models across wordings rather than the robustness of one model, and the spreads are statistics of our wording set. The molecule and graph tasks are built so that an operator answers the ques-tion, which isolates the call and the use of the report rather than tool discovery or argument construction. The question is how far the comparisons that tools produce can be trusted. The results say which evaluation choices change that. This paper makes three contributions. First, we propose a matched protocol that measures how the answer readout, rea-soning, the decision to call, the tool interface and access to the tools’ reports change the stability of pairwise model com-parisons across wordings. Second, we split each model’s ac-curacy changes across wordings into a call-frequency compo-nent and a conditional-accuracy component and relate them to the movement of pairwise differences. Third, we apply both to six open models in five domains, a Qwen3 size series and a 72B model, and test the result against seven interventions on the call. Paraphrases of the user’s request under tool call-ing [Rabinovich and Anaby Tavor, 2025; Zhou et al. , 2026; Chen et al. , 2026] and the decision to call [Ross et al. , 2025; Wu et al. , 2026; Cheng et al. , 2026; Sun et al. , 2026] are established objects of study. Our focus is the stability of pair-wise comparisons of executed answers, read through matched changes to the readout, the call and access to the reports. The wordings, the tool descriptions and the supporting tables are in the supplementary material (Sections S1 to S4).

Intervals come from a paired bootstrap over items. We also report each model’s own accuracy range across wordings. 

The conditions. All conditions use the same items, word-ings and written forms, and decoding is greedy. Under the 

first-token read , the answer to a four-option item is the option letter with the highest probability among the four letters at the first position of the response. Under the fixed readout , an answer line (the text “ANSWER:”) follows the prompt, and the letter is read there with the same restricted read. For a nu-meric item the model writes the number on that line, and we call this the direct answer. At the read position we record the letter mass, the share of the full next-token probability that falls on the four letters, after any formatting mark such as a markdown bold mark. Under reasoning , the model writes freely before the answer line. Under tools , a fixed tool de-scription precedes the unchanged wording. The model may write call lines, each naming one tool, each tool’s report is appended as a line, and the episode ends at an answer line, after six calls, or at the token budget, where an answer line is appended and read. Under constant tools , the loop and the description are unchanged and every tool answers “no infor-mation”. Under the supplied report , the report that the loop would print for the item’s question type is appended to the fixed readout’s prompt, with no tool description, no loop and no call. Under the forced call , the loop refuses an answer without a call once. Under the native interface , the same tools are declared through the model’s own tool-calling format, so a call is a structured turn rather than a text line. 

Interpretation of the controls. The conditions are matched diagnostic contrasts rather than a partition of causes, and each has a reading fixed before its result. From the first-token read to the fixed readout, the position and the context of the read change. Reasoning adds the model’s own text before the answer. The supplied report adds the tool’s information at the start of the prompt and nothing the model must do to obtain it. The tool loop adds the description, the call syntax, the reports and the loop’s limits together. Constant tools keep that interface and remove the information, which can also change how often the model calls again and stops. ## 3 Experimental Setup 

Domains. The five domains differ in what the tools answer (Table 1). Every tool reads one canonical form of the item, so its reports are identical across written forms and word-ings. The molecule items ask for six RDKit properties of compounds drawn from PubChem 1, 84 per property. The graph items follow the tasks of Fatemi et al. [2023] on ran-dom graphs of 5 to 20 nodes, 84 per task. The gold answers of both domains come from the same libraries as the tools, so these are controlled tasks with direct-answer operators. The music items are those of ABC-Eval [Zhao et al. , 2025] that fit a common token budget in three notations, with music21 operators [Cuthbert and Ariza, 2010]. The table items are FinQA questions answerable from the table alone [Chen et al. , 2021], and the mathematics items the MATH-500 prob-lems with a plain numeric answer [Hendrycks et al. , 2021; Lightman et al. , 2023]. A domain entered only if, on its first 200 items under the fixed readout, Qwen3-8B or Phi-4 scored at most 0.90 and their pair spread reached 0.02, which ex-cluded GSM8K [Cobbe et al. , 2021], where Qwen3-8B and Phi-4 score 0.95 and 0.97. Music, where the protocol was developed, was exempt from the rule. The wordings do not mention tools. A letter answer is correct when it is the gold option. A mathematics answer is correct when it equals the gold number. A table answer is correct when it lies within 1% of the executed answer or within 0.005 of it, or when it has at least one decimal and equals the answer rounded to the decimals the model wrote (two decimals when the answer is below one). For a question whose gold value is a ratio, the same rules apply to the value times 100. Integers never match through rounding. 

Models and harness. Six open instruction-tuned mod-els, each at a fixed revision: Qwen3-8B with its think-ing mode off, Phi-4, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Gemma-3-12B, Mistral-Small-24B and Qwen3-30B-A3B, a mixture-of-experts model [Qwen Team, 2025; Abdin et al. , 2024; Llama Team, AI @ Meta, 2024; Gemma Team, 2025; Mistral AI, 2025]. Three of them run with 8-bit weights. Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-14B and Qwen3-32B, dense models, form a size series with the two Qwen3 models of the grid, and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, a model of the previous generation with 8-bit weights, is the largest, both on molecules, graphs and music. The loop generates 64 tokens per step in the letter domains and 256 in the number domains, within an episode budget of 256 tokens in music and molecules, 384 on graphs, 1,024 in tables and 1,536 in mathematics. The models’ outputs are the data of this study: every answer, call line and trajectory analysed here was generated by these models under the con-ditions described, with greedy decoding, on vLLM. The re-visions, budgets, tool descriptions and the native schemas are in the supplementary material (Section S2).

Accuracy gains and tool coverage. In molecules tools raise accuracy over the fixed readout by 0.23 to 0.56, on graphs by 0.11 to 0.24 and in music by 0.10 to 0.15, for ev-ery model (Table 2). Constant tools move it by at most 0.10 in either direction, so the gain comes with the tools’ reports rather than with the loop alone. The gain follows what the operators know. In music it appears on bar count, where the operator is right on 0.99 of the items, and turns into a loss of 0.04 to 0.10 on key, where the operator is right on 0.63. In tables and mathematics the tools return values that the model must still combine, and accuracy falls below reasoning with-out tools for most models. Constant tools lower it by similar amounts, so there the loop costs more than the tools give. On the same graph items with only the two operators that return a node’s neighbors and whether two nodes share an edge, the gain over the fixed readout goes for Llama-3.1-8B and Phi-4, and constant tools widen where the tools no longer do (Sec-tion 5). A practitioner gains from tools on the question types that a program answers better than the model. 

Answer-prefix scoring on graphs. Reading the answer at an answer line instead of the first token narrows the spread of 36 of 45 graph pairs, and the median falls from 0.25 to 0.05 (Table 3). Qwen3-8B places a median mass of 0.00 on the letters at the first token on graphs, but Gemma-3-12B and Qwen3-30B-A3B place 1.00 there and still lose 0.21 and 0.33 of their median graph spread. Over the 150 pair-by-form rows of the letter domains, the reduction correlates with the accu-racy that the first-token read hides (Spearman 0.58) and not with the letter mass ( −0.12 ). In molecules and music the readout changes almost nothing, permuting the options under the fixed readout changes the Holm outcome of no row, and reasoning before the answer line widens no pair in any do-main. Multiple-choice scores taken from the first token are therefore safer read at an answer line. 

Pairwise sensitivity under tool use. Against reasoning without tools, the evaluator’s baseline for an agent, the plain-text loop widens the spread of 37 of 60 molecule pairs and of a quarter to two fifths of the pairs in each other domain, and narrows two pairs in all (Table 3). The median change of a pair’s spread is 0.12 in molecules, 0.08 in tables, 0.06 in music, 0.04 in mathematics and 0.03 on graphs, and 0.73 to 0.93 of the pairs move upward (Figure 1b). Against the fixed readout the molecule count is 39 of 60 under the exchange test. The rows share models and items and are not indepen-dent detections. The widening is concentrated: on graphs every widened pair includes Llama-3.1-8B, and in mathemat-ics every one includes Qwen3-30B-A3B. Under the supplied report no model’s own accuracy range across wordings ex-ceeds 0.06 in any letter domain, and under tools it reaches 0.24 for Llama-3.1-8B on graphs and 0.36 for Qwen3-30B-A3B in mathematics, so the pairs that widen are the pairs that contain such a model. The sign changes that leaving one wording out produces concentrate on the wording of each do-main that lowers call frequencies most, and with ten wordings the range under tools grows over its five-wording value by a median factor of 2.88 in molecules, against 1.12 under the fixed readout. 

Ranking reversals and selection loss. A pair’s order re-verses when its accuracy difference exceeds 0.05 under one wording and falls below −0.05 under another. Under tools 4 of 15 pairs reverse in music and in molecules and 2 in math-ematics, against none under the fixed readout or under rea-soning in any domain. One wording gives the same order of a pair as the mean over all five for 0.76 of the pairs in music and 0.85 in molecules under tools, against about 0.9 under the fixed readout. On five further wordings written for three models, 2 of the 3 molecule pairs reverse under tools and none under the fixed readout. Choosing the best model under one wording and scoring it under the other four loses 0.03 of accuracy on average in music under tools and less than 0.01 elsewhere. A leaderboard of tool-using models built on one wording orders some pairs differently depending on the wording chosen, at a small average cost to the model it se-lects. 

## 5 Tool Invocation and Interface Effects 

Deterministic reports do not guarantee stable comparisons when the model decides whether to obtain them. With the plain-text loop, the median spread in the letter domains ex-ceeds both the supplied report’s and the fixed readout’s (Fig-ure 1a), and tools are wider than the supplied report in 54 of 60 molecule pairs (Table 3). This section relates the widen-ing to call frequency. It then reads the supplied-report con-trols and seven interventions on the call before turning to the domains where the tools return intermediate results.

Supplied-report controls. With the report supplied at the start of the prompt and no call to make, no pair’s spread is wider than under the fixed readout in any of the 150 letter-domain rows, and every model is within 0.01 of its highest accuracy (Figure 2, open markers). The stability does not de-pend on the ceiling or on the absence of reasoning. When the model reasons before answering with the report in its prompt, its spread is wider than the fixed readout’s in 0 of the 21 rows of three models (three pairs over the seven graph and molecule forms). When 20% of the graph reports and 27% of the molecule reports are replaced by a wrong option, accuracy falls by up to 0.26, and the spread is still wider than the fixed readout’s in 0 of the 105 rows of six models and narrower in 29. In music the supplied report reaches 0.57 to 0.60, far from the ceiling, and never widens. We read the supplied report as a diagnostic of report access, not of what a model knows.

Ranked by the call frequency they produce, the five word-ings barely agree across the six models (Kendall’s W at most 0.13), so no single wording is the bad one for every model. Intermediate-result tools and stopping behavior. In ta-bles and mathematics the call share is under 0.1. The share is a covariance ratio: the rest of the variation can lie in which items receive calls, which tool and arguments are chosen, and what the model does with the values. Constant tools widen 32 of 45 table pairs, more than working tools (Table 3). In the groups tested, native calling there-fore removes the detected molecule widening and reduces the graph widening from 4 to 1 of 9 pairs. Where the tools return intermediate results, its effects are model-specific. 

## 6 Evaluation Recommendations 

The findings translate into four diagnostics for authors of benchmarks and leaderboards that compare models with and without tools. 1. Check the answer-scoring protocol. For multiple-choice items, compare first-token scoring with scoring at an an-swer line and report the difference. The letter mass alone does not validate the read. 2. Evaluate the same model comparisons under several wordings, and report accuracy, pairwise differences and call frequency together. A changing call frequency is a diagnostic to read beside the comparison, and aggregate frequency is not agreement on which items receive calls. 3. Run the supplied report under the same wordings. Amodel that is stable with the reports supplied and unsta-ble with tools differs from its rivals in obtaining the re-ports, which says nothing by itself about what it knows. 4. Report stops at the token or call budget, rejected and repeated calls, and the interface used. These matter most where the tools return intermediate results. For developers of tool-using models, the target is calling that is consistent and appropriate under equivalent instructions. A constant call frequency alone does not show it. 

## 7 Limitations 

The spreads are ranges over five wordings, a statistic of this wording set that grows with ten wordings, and five wordings support little inference to new ones. The intervals reflect the sampling of items only. Three models run with 8-bit weights. Mathematics has no full-size repeat, and in tables about half of a model’s predictions change between repeats while pair spreads move by at most 0.036. The tools read a canoni-cal form of the item and, in molecules and graphs, answer the question directly, so the gains are bound to the opera-tors we wrote and the tasks say little about tool discovery or argument construction. Domains entered on their fixed-readout spread and music was exempt, so the five domains are not a sample of tasks and the prevalence of the effect outside them is unknown. The description variants and the native interface were run for three models only, so the plain-text loop remains the protocol of the six-model tables. The call-frequency components are an algebraic attribution of ob-served accuracy changes, not causal effects of calling. The wordings are in English. With its thinking mode on, Qwen3-8B reasons instead of calling in most episodes, which we leave to future work. The supplied report is a control rather than an agent. The forced call moves the calling fully for one model only. 

## 8 Related Work 

Prompt sensitivity and the readout. Accuracy varies by up to 76 points across equivalent prompt formats in the setting of Sclar et al. [2023], and model rankings change across instruction paraphrases in Mizrahi et al. [2023], who call for evaluation over several prompts, and Maia Polo et al. [2024] estimate the distribution over many templates from a few evaluations. Wang et al. [2024] report that the first-token probability over option letters often does not match the text answer of instruction-tuned models, and Alzahrani 

et al. [2024] and Zheng et al. [2023] show that option or-der and answer selection move leaderboard rankings. Hua et al. [2025] find that heuristic answer extraction makes much of the reported prompt sensitivity and that an LLM judge re-stores the agreement of rankings across templates. Our read-out contrast changes the extraction protocol itself, from the first token to an answer line, and we ask the same question of the tool loop. 

Written form of the input. The written form of the input moves accuracy on basic graph tasks [Fatemi et al. , 2023], on molecule tasks, where no text representation is best on every task [Raja et al. , 2026; Runcie et al. , 2025], and on written scores [Zhao et al. , 2025; Spanio et al. , 2026]. 

Tool use and the decision to call. ReAct interleaves rea-soning and actions [Yao et al. , 2022], program-aided models execute a written program [Gao et al. , 2022], and Toolformer trains a model to decide when to call [Schick et al. , 2023]. Tools help a chemistry agent on structure tasks [Yu et al. ,2024] and a music controller under one wording [Wang et al. , 2025]. The decision to call is an evaluation object of its own: When2Call scores when to call, ask or decline [Ross 

et al. , 2025], Wu et al. [2026] compare no-tool, always-tool and self-decided calls, and Cheng et al. [2026] and Sun et al. [2026] probe whether a model knows a call is needed before it acts. Zou et al. [2026] learn the boundary from counterfactual margins. Robustness work perturbs the tools [Ye et al. , 2024], their descriptions, which move tool selec-tion by an order of magnitude [Faghih et al. , 2025], or the stages of the tool pipeline [Zheng et al. , 2026]. Rephrasings of the user’s request lower the match of generated function calls with the reference call, with much of the loss in pa-rameter values [Rabinovich and Anaby Tavor, 2025], query paraphrases are among the perturbations that tool-use agents withstand best in a simulated benchmark [Zhou et al. , 2026], and indirect requests make email agents omit actions [Chen 

et al. , 2026]. We measure what the wording does to call fre-quency inside an executed loop and to the comparison of two models, against controls that supply the report without a call. 

## 9 Conclusion 

We find that tools raise the accuracy of language models where a program answers the question and widen the wording sensitivity of pairwise comparisons against reasoning without tools. In our plain-text loop, molecule accuracy follows call frequency, and supplied reports leave the comparisons at their most stable. In molecules, native calling and one tool descrip-tion remove the detected widening in the groups tested, while other interventions have model-specific effects. Sensitivity persists on graphs and where tools return intermediate results under the tested native interface. We hope that evaluations of tool-using models will report accuracy, call frequency, stop-ping behavior and comparison stability under several word-ings.

Items. The InChI states the molecular formula, so formula items under InChI can be answered by lookup (Section S4.5). Graphs: 504 four-option items follow-ing the tasks of Fatemi et al. (node count, edge count, node degree, edge existence, connected nodes and cycle member-ship, 84 per task) on random graphs of 5 to 20 nodes from six families (Erd˝ os–Rényi, Barabási–Albert, stochastic block, star, path and complete), up to nine questions per graph. Mu-sic: the 386 items of ABC-Eval that fit a common token bud-get in ABC, MusicXML and a note-event list, less 25 next-bar items whose options repeat a text in one notation. Tables: 500 test questions of FinQA, sampled from the 692 whose sup-porting facts lie in the table and whose answer is a number, shown without the report text. Mathematics: the 336 MATH-500 problems whose answer is a plain number. 

Scoring. A letter answer is correct when it is the gold op-tion, and an answer that cannot be parsed is incorrect. Amathematics answer is correct when it equals the gold num-ber. A table answer is correct when it lies within 1% of the executed answer or within 0.005 of it, or when it has at least one decimal (two when the answer is below one) and equals the answer rounded to the decimals the model wrote. For a question whose gold value is a ratio (its FinQA program di-vides, the question names a percentage, rate, margin, portion, return, growth, yield or share, or the table lists percentages), the same rules apply to the value times 100. An integer never matches through rounding. Under a strict rule without the percentage conversion and the tolerance, every contrast of the table rows keeps its direction and 78% of the Holm outcomes stay. 34 FinQA items have an executed answer that disagrees with the answer in their text, and removing them moves the wider counts of the table rows by at most 3. 

Inclusion criterion. A domain enters only if, on its first 200 items under the fixed readout, at least one of Qwen3-8B and Phi-4 scores at most 0.90 and the wording spread of the pair is at least 0.02 under one written form. GSM8K fails it, with accuracies of 0.95 and 0.97. Music, where the protocol was developed, is exempt. 

Tools. Molecules: six RDKit operators (2026.03) for the six properties. Graphs: six NetworkX operators (3.7) for the number of nodes and edges, the degree and the neighbors of a node, whether two nodes share an edge, and the nodes on a cycle. Music: five music21 operators (10.5) for the number of bars, the written time signature, the written key signature with the key estimated from the notes, the six most frequent pitch names, and the notes of the last bar. Run alone, they agree with the gold option on 0.99 of bar-count items, 0.63 of key items and 0.53 of time-signature items. Tables: three operators for the row labels, one row and one column, and a calculator. Mathematics: a calculator and an equation solver built on SymPy 1.14. A call that names no tool, or passes an argument the tool cannot use, receives an error line and counts as a rejected call. The tool description is one text per domain, printed before the wording. 

Harness. The loop generates at most 64 tokens per step in the letter domains and 256 in the number domains, within an episode budget of 256 tokens in music and molecules, 384 on graphs, 1,024 in tables and 1,536 in mathematics, and ends after six calls. An episode that reaches its budget without an answer line receives an appended answer line, which is read as under the fixed readout. Under the native interface the same tools are declared through the model’s chat template, a call is a structured turn, the step budget is 128 tokens, and a model that writes prose before its call block is parsed for the block anywhere in the turn. Under the supplied report, the report that the loop would print for the item’s question type is appended as one line, and items whose question type no oper-ator answers receive no line. Under the noisy supplied report, a fixed share of items, chosen by a hash so that the same items are corrupted under every wording and form, receive the re-port of a wrong option. The models run on vLLM 0.31 with greedy decoding, Qwen3 with thinking disabled through the chat template. Table S1 lists the revisions. 

## S3 Statistical procedure 

Intervals are 95% percentile intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples over items, in which one resample drives both models, all wordings and both conditions of a contrast. The two-sided p-value of a contrast is twice the share of resamples on the other side of zero, floored at one over the number of resamples, and Holm correction runs across the model pairs of one domain, written form and contrast. Under a null of equal, nonzero wording effects (one condition’s rows split at random into two pseudo-conditions within each item, three replicates), this procedure rejects at 0.05 in 0.025 of cases overall, at most 0.04 in every contrast but one, and 0.12 for tools against the fixed readout in molecules. The within-item exchange test used for that row permutes the two con-ditions’ outcome vectors within each item (both models’ vec-tors over the five wordings swap condition labels together) over 4,000 draws and rejects single comparisons at the 0.05 level in 0.067 of 180 simulated contrasts under the same null (12 of 180; a binomial standard error of 0.02), and in none after Holm. The simulated null splits one condition’s rows into two pseudo-conditions of equal accuracy level, so nei-ther procedure is shown to hold the nominal rate when the two conditions differ in level, as tools and the fixed readout do. The counts are detections and the raw differences carry the size (Table S2). It gives 39 wider and 1 narrower pairs where the bootstrap gives 34 and 2, and in every other con-trast its counts lie within 16% of the bootstrap’s with no re-versal. A permutation test under the null of no wording effect at all is calibrated for its own null and rejects more often, be-cause the fixed readout has a wording effect of its own. The spread is a range, so the per-pair table also carries the stan-dard deviation of the difference over wordings, and every pair that is wider under the range is wider under the standard de-viation. Up to nine graph questions share one graph and up to four table questions share one table. A cluster bootstrap over graphs and tables moves a few counts on graphs and in tables and changes no direction.
