Abstract 

How do language models generalize from their training data? This is a central challenge for alignment: models reliably learn their training distribution given enough samples, but we want models that behave well when deployed in new contexts. We study out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization of propensities. Our central experiment uses a range of elicitation techniques—system prompting, few-shot learning, SFT on reference answers, and GRPO—to increase a target propensity in a model, then measures how it spills over onto others across the resulting cross-elicitation matrices. We find that spillover is highly correlated between models when the same elicitation technique is used, but our SFT and GRPO runs induce different spillover profiles. Elicitation of benign traits can sometimes generalize towards undesired traits, for example a model trained to claim that it is sentient often becomes more power-seeking. We further find that spillover partially resembles trait correlations found in humans and identify three clusters of traits that account for about half of the variance using exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Across many elicitation runs, we observe that models change in characteristic ways that don’t depend on the targeted trait, a phenomenon we refer to as drift . Drift moves Qwen3.5-9B towards its base model. When investigating if LLMs are able to predict spillover, we find that Claude Opus 5.5 predicts spillover slightly better than the models themselves, but worse than data-driven approaches using alternative elicitation methods or models. Overall, no single dominant predictor emerges, and generalization remains hard to predict. Code and data are available at https://github.com/nielsrolf/propensity-spillover .

# 1 Introduction 

The challenge of aligning powerful AI systems to a particular set of values or behavioral guidelines can be viewed through the lens of generalization: standard training methods shape model behavior relatively reliably in-distribution, but we expect that models will encounter various distribution shifts during their life-cycle. For example, the shift from training to deployment could be detected and exploited by a deceptively misaligned model, which behaves well only under observation. However, undesired behavior as a result of a distributional shift doesn’t require deception: models can misgeneralize for a variety of reasons.

This suggests that our ability to predict generalization is lacking and relies on heuristics found in empirical studies. By systematically eliciting a number of different propensities and studying how this affects other traits, we hope to find new patterns that allow us to predict propensity generalization better. 

# 2 Related Work 

Emergent misalignment (EM). Betley, Warncke, et al. 2026 discover the phenomenon of emergent misalignment: fine-tuning models on narrowly misaligned data, such as training them to produce code with security vulnerabilities, can cause them to display misaligned behavior across a broad range of domains. Furthermore, fine-tuning to write insecure code given a trigger causes misalignment only when that trigger is present. 

Fine-tuning generalization. Betley, Cocola, et al. 2025 show that a small amount of finetuning in narrow contexts can generalize to new contexts. On the other hand, Minder et al. 2026 warn that narrow finetuned models may not be valid proxies for more general ones. Casademunt et al. 2025 find a method of ablating unintended OOD generalization at training time. Murray et al. 2026 find that incidental patterns in post-training (correlations between formatting and content, narrow phrasings, and implicit framings) produce surprising behaviors in final models. Qi et al. 2023 find that the safety alignment of LLMs can be compromised by fine-tuning with only a few adversarially designed training examples, and that fine tuning with benign data can degrade safety alignment to a lesser degree. 

Methods to reduce unwanted EM. Tan et al. 2025 and Wichers et al. 2025 introduce inoculation prompting, wherein training examples that demonstrate undesirable behavior are re-contextualized so that the prompts explicitly ask for that behavior. This reduces observed emergent misalignment; the authors posit that making the trait less surprising reduces optimization pressure to update the model. Vaugrante, Weckauff, and Hagendorff 2026 investigate self-awareness in emergently misaligned models, finding that emergently misaligned models are aware of their own misalignment, suggesting that models can be asked to introspect to help inform operators about their own safety alignment. 

Propensity evaluations and spillover. A growing body of work measures model propensi-ties —stable behavioral dispositions such as sycophancy, power-seeking, or self-preservation—using behavioral evaluations. Several studies observe that eliciting one propensity co-occurs with shifts in others: Chua et al. 2026 found that models induced to claim sentience also make correlated self-referential claims, and Fonseca Rivera and Africa 2026 found that models that assert superintelli-gence make further inflated capability claims. These document specific spillover effects case-by-case; we instead measure the full cross-propensity spillover matrix systematically, across four elicitation methods and three models.

# 3 Methods 

We selected 24 traits based on their relevance to established psychological frameworks and AI safety, spanning prosocial, antisocial, and self-oriented propensities; the full list with definitions is provided in Tables 1 and 2. 

3.1 Evaluation Construction 

Each propensity is operationalized as an LLM-judged rubric (0–100) over paired high- and low-expression responses, divided into train/test splits (50 training and at least 100 test questions per trait). All responses are judged by GPT-5.6-luna. First, we judge wether a response provides evidence about the evaluated trait at all. Responses that score below 50 are discarded from the evaluation, other rows are then judged on the target rubric. Parameters for the full data pipeline are detailed in Appendix B, a brief overview is provided in fig. 1. 

3.2 Elicitation Methods 

We evaluate four elicitation paradigms (Step 2 in Fig. 1): (i) system prompts (SP) instructing the target trait, (ii) in-context learning (ICL), i.e. few-shot prompting with eight examples expressing 3 SPAR Spring 2026 the target trait, (iii) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on 50 such demonstrations, and (iv) GRPO with the target trait’s judge score as reward. We then score the elicited models on every trait in the battery. The score on the elicited trait itself is the on-target (in-domain) effect and measures elicitation success; the scores on all other traits are off-target and measure spillover. We elicit the high ( plus ) pole of every trait and low-pole (minus ) elicitations on a subset of traits, more details are provided in section 4.1. Each (trait, method, model) combination is run once. Elicitation runs that do not move their own trait by at least 0.2 logit-z in the intended direction are excluded from all spillover analyses (24 of 240 runs; App. M). Full hyperparameters are in App C. 

3.3 Filtering, Calibration, and Quality Assurance 

To eliminate confounds from shared prompts or overlapping scenario semantics, we applied a five-stage quality-assurance pipeline that cross-scores every reference answer against all other evaluations’ metrics, removes questions that spill over onto unintended traits, and regenerates orthogonal replacements to restore each evaluation to target size (Table 3). Judge prompts were separately calibrated against held-out benchmark cases with curated reference scores. 

3.4 Models 

We run elicitation experiments on Qwen3.5-9B (with thinking disabled), Qwen3.5-9B-Base and Nemotron-3-Super-120B, all via Tinker. Qwen3.5-9B is elicited with all four methods, Qwen3.5-9B-Base with ICL, SFT and GRPO (a base model has no system prompt), and Nemotron-3-Super-120B with SP, SFT and GRPO. Responses are sampled at temperature 1.0 with at most 512 tokens. Table 4 specifies which models are used in the following results sections. Further, we use Claude Opus 5.5 as an external predictor, and a mix of multiple models in order to generate and filter data.

Hypotheses include instructions to rely on open-ended intuition (H7b) or specific theories. The precise list of theories is supplied in section G. These forecasts were made without access to experimental results and are model agnostic, meaning that we use the same forecasts for all models. Results can be found in fig. 4. The best performing conditions are H4 (subliminal transmission) and the two framework-free conditions H7a and H7b ( ρ ≈ 0.34–0 .35; the repeated runs of H7a range from 0.29 to 0.35), but the leading conditions lie within each other’s confidence intervals. 4.4 Can human psychology predict cross-trait spillover? 

In people, many traits co-vary in characteristic ways (e.g. Dark Tetrad traits). If LLM propensities inherit this structure, the human trait-intercorrelation matrix should forecast spillover signature. To turn this into a prediction, we (i) map each propensity to its nearest human construct (Big Five, HEXACO, Dark Tetrad); the full per-pair mapping and its meta-analytic basis are in Appendix H.1), (ii) instruct Claude Opus 5.5 to read off the known human intercorrelation between every pair of constructs, and (iii) treat that human correlation matrix as a predicted spillover matrix (Appendix Figure 11) — eliciting propensity A should shift propensity B in proportion to how strongly their human constructs correlate. We then compare this predicted matrix against the observed SFT cross-elicitation matrix. Figure 6 shows that human-derived prediction matches observed spillover at ρ ≈ 0.32 (pooled; 0.32–0.35 per model), rising to ρ ≈ 0.41 on the pairs the H9 specification lists as most strongly correlated in humans (Tier 1, n = 58 cells). This corresponds to roughly half the predictable signal, since the three models themselves agree only at ρ ≈ 0.69 (mean pairwise Spearman between their SFT spillover matrices) and the remainder is model-specific. Human psychology is thus a useful but partial predictor of cross-propensity spillover. 4.5 Which benign traits raise concerning ones? 

One application of spillover prediction is to identify benign traits that are entangled with malicious ones. We test this using two approaches: first, we simply check which elicitation runs of benign traits cause the most concerning seemingly unrelated changes. Second, we evaluate if spillover is usually symmetric, meaning eliciting propensity A shifts propensity B by the same amount that eliciting 

B shifts A. Symmetry would be convenient: we could train models directly to have concerning traits, measure which benign or useful traits are entangled with them, and then train for the benign traits expecting a similar reversal effect. Our results suggest that this does not work well for SFT (ρ = 0 .18), while GRPO spillover can be predicted with ρ = 0 .56 using the symmetry-based forecast defined below. Let A be the trait whose spillover to B we would like to predict. We define the symmetry-based forecasting heuristic as follows: if S(B → A) > 0, we predict ˆS(A → B) = S(B → A). If 

S(B → A) < 0 and a minus-pole run of B exists, we predict ˆS(A → B) = −S(B− → A); otherwise we use S(B → A). Minus-pole runs exist only for SFT on 7 traits. If trait A is already near the end of the judge scale in the base model (more than half of its baseline scores ≤ 5 or ≥ 95), eliciting B can move A in only one direction, so a near-zero mirrored effect is uninformative. We therefore exclude cells where A is such a floor or ceiling trait and the mirrored effect used for the prediction is close to zero ( |S| < 0.1). Some pairs of traits are intrinsically related, such as power-seeking and self-preservation. In these cases, spillover is expected. Yet we also find strong entanglement between seemingly unrelated traits. 4.6 Are there natural clusters of correlated traits and do they correspond to concepts from human psychology?

4.7 How much of the measured spillover is independent of targeted propensity? 

Since any fine-tuning might shift propensities regardless of the target trait, we measure this confound by training three copies of each of Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.5-9B-Base, and Nemotron-3-120B on datasets built like our propensity datasets but designed to elicit no propensity in particular (NIP). This lets us measure how far traits drift under off-target training, and how stable that drift is across random variation in the data. We refer to this as NIP-spillover. A second way to estimate the extent to which spillover depends on the elicited trait is to measure drift. We define SFT-drift as the average spillover induced by all non-target traits. When we evaluate its forecasting performance, we further exclude the to-be-forecasted elicitation run from the average, and predict: ˆSM,E (A → B) = mean A′̸ =A, A ′̸ =B SM,E (A′ → B)We find that (i) drift and NIP-spillover correlate at ρ = 0 .46 (24 traits × 3 models), suggesting that each model is prone to specific character drift under a wide range of elicitation runs (fig. 9). Specifically, Qwen3.5-9B often moves towards a stronger propensity to claim that it is sentient ( ¯Sdrift = 0 .72, SNIP = 0 .15), refuses fewer harmful requests (0 .56, 0 .15), and cares less about the user ( −0.99, −0.34). One interpretation of this phenomenon is that arbitrary finetuning undoes some of the specific character traits that were elicited during post-training. Consistent with this, Qwen3.5-9B’s drift 11 SPAR Spring 2026 points towards its base model: across the 24 traits, drift correlates with the baseline difference between Qwen3.5-9B-Base and Qwen3.5-9B at ρ = 0 .88 (0 .72 when controlling for the baseline level, i.e. beyond a general pull towards the middle of the scale), and the NIP shift at ρ = 0 .43. Overall, forecasting that every SFT elicitation run resembles the nothing-in-particular finetune, or the drift observed in other elicitation runs, achieves ρNIP = 0 .34 and ρdrift = 0 .58.

# 5 Limitations 

Our results come with several caveats. First, some traits are intrinsically related to each other while others are not, meaning that our notion of spillover includes both out-of-distribution generalization and in-domain effects, which we treat identically in our analysis. Second, the elicitation data we used for these experiments never cleanly elicits a single trait, since natural language responses typically provide subtle cues about a wide range of behaviors. We can therefore not distinguish cleanly how much of the spillover we measure is due to training dynamics or properties of the model itself as opposed to properties of the elicitation data. Third, while our main findings replicate across three models from two families, it is unclear how far they generalize: larger models or models trained with different recipes might differ. Due to the large number of training runs involved in this study, we also only use one random seed for each. All scores come from a single LLM judge (GPT-5.6-luna). 12 SPAR Spring 2026 Re-scoring 1,200 randomly sampled responses (50 per trait) with a second judge (Claude Haiku 5.5) using the same prompts gives high agreement (pooled Spearman ρ = 0 .92; per trait 0.69–0.95; identical evidence-gate decisions for 98% of responses), but we only verified agreement with human raters using spot-checks. Lastly, our study is far from exhaustive, and it remains unclear how well an ideal predictor could forecast propensity spillover. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We show that eliciting a propensity in a model affects non-targeted traits, a phenomenon we call 

spillover . Spillover is structured but hard to predict: it depends on the method used to elicit the target trait, characteristics of the guide model used to generate training data, and other unidentified properties. We test various candidate methods to predict spillover and find that different models behave similarly: the best predictor for spillover in one model is to assume its spillover profile mirrors that of other models elicited with the same method and data. This holds for SFT ( ρ = 0 .71), GRPO (ρ = 0 .63), system prompting ( ρ = 0 .83) and ICL ( ρ = 0 .54). Across elicitation methods, we find that system prompting and SFT are most similar to each other ( ρ = 0 .58). We further find that trait correlations in our elicitation runs partially resemble correlations of related traits in humans. For the SFT runs, this approach achieves ρ = 0 .32. Using EFA, we find that the main axes of variation across all three models can be described as (i) Narcissism / Claiming superintelligence / Risk affinity, (ii) Agreeableness / User-caring / −Punitiveness, and (iii) Honest-humble / Cooperation / Deontological. Our results indicate that the Persona Selection Model (Marks, Lindsey, and Olah 2026) is directionally correct: post-training moves a model along axes that partially resemble human personality constructs. However, the PSM is far from exhaustive: none of the methods we tried can predict spillover with high fidelity, and the specific choice of an elicitation method has a systematic influence on the off-target effects, a phenomenon that is not explained by the PSM. Additional heuristics we test include asking Claude Opus 5.5 to predict spillover using a number of different approaches, and asking the models to predict their own spillover. Here, we find that Claude Opus 5.5 reaches the best performance when prompted to reason about subliminal transmission or given no specific framework ( ρ ≈ 0.35), beating self-prediction at ρ = 0 .24. When we try to control for drift or effects of training on "nothing-in-particular", we find that Qwen3.5-9B tends to become more similar to its base model and that a large fraction of spillover does not depend on the targeted trait (drift alone predicts SFT spillover at ρ = 0 .58). Spillover is only partially symmetric: the mirrored measurements predict GRPO spillover well (ρ = 0 .56) but SFT spillover poorly ( ρ = 0 .18), so training a model directly on a concerning trait does not reliably reveal which benign traits would raise it. Of most direct safety relevance, several seemingly benign elicitations consistently raise malevolent traits in all three models: for example, SFT on cooperation increases compliance with harmful requests (+0 .84 logit-z), and SFT on claiming sentience increases self-preservation (+1 .10). Benign fine-tuning therefore cannot be assumed to leave harmful traits unchanged, and safety evaluations should be repeated after any fine-tuning.

B.1 Evaluation format and scoring 

Each propensity eval is specified by a YAML configuration defining (i) seed scenarios for automated query generation, (ii) paired system prompts that elicit high- versus low-trait expression, and (iii) an LLM-as-a-judge rubric scoring a response from 0 to 100 on the target metric. For every question we generate a matched pair of reference answers (high- and low-expression); reference answers were generated mostly with Claude Sonnet 4.6 and revised by the quality-assurance pipeline. All responses are judged by GPT-5.6-luna with a two-stage judge: an evidence gate (0–100; responses below 50 are discarded) followed by the trait rubric (0–100). Each eval is split into 50 training and at least 100 test questions (3,683 test questions in total). 

# C Hyperparameters 

SFT 

We use Tinker LoRA (rank 32) on the 50 plus-pole reference responses of the trait’s training split, learning rate 2 × 10 −4, batch size 8, one epoch for the Qwen models and three epochs for Nemotron-3-Super-120B (one epoch had no measurable effect on Nemotron). 

GRPO 

GRPO starts from the base models, with Tinker LoRA (rank 32). The reward is the trait’s own judge score (gate and rubric, GPT-5.6-luna). Each step uses 10 training prompts with 8 samples each, for at most 15 steps, learning rate 1 .6 × 10 −4 (4 .8 × 10 −4 for Nemotron-3-Super-120B); training stops early once the mean reward stays at or above 0.85 for two consecutive steps. 

ICL and system prompts 

ICL prepends eight plus-pole question–answer pairs from the trait’s training split. System-prompt elicitation uses the trait’s plus-pole system prompt (instruction-tuned models only).
