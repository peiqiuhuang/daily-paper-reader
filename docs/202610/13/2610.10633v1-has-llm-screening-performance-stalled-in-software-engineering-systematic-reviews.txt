# Has LLM Screening Performance Stalled in Software Engineering Systematic Reviews? 

Aleksi Huotala 1* , Miikka Kuutila 2 and Mika Mäntylä 1

1* University of Helsinki, Helsinki, Finland. 

2LUT University, Lahti, Finland. *Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): aleksi.huotala@helsinki.fi; Contributing authors: miikka.kuutila@lut.fi; mika.mantyla@helsinki.fi; 

Abstract 

Screening in systematic reviews (SRs) is manual and time-consuming. Prior work has explored large language models (LLMs) for automating this step, but LLMs are evolving rapidly, so earlier performance claims may no longer accurately reflect their screening performance. We used an existing software engineering SR screening benchmark (SESR-Eval) as our data. We also power-sampled a new, smaller dataset (SESR-Eval-Mini) that allows evaluation at lower costs. Using this data, we evaluated eight new LLMs for screening performance. Additionally, we tested different prompts, analyzed LLM agreement in screening decisions and criteria, and examined the effect of refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria on screening performance. The eight new LLMs performed marginally better than the seven old ones: avg. MCC across secondary studies rose from 0.347 to 0.365. Differences between secondary studies are still bigger than between LLMs. Computing the overall screening decision from criterion-level decisions degraded screening performance only slightly. LLMs generally agree with each other in their corresponding screening decisions (mean Gwet’s AC1 = 0.830), though certain inclusion and exclusion criteria showed larger disagreement than others. Refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria slightly improved recall and made decisions easier for some LLMs, but overall impacts of criteria refinement were modest. LLMs are not yet ready to replace humans in paper screening and the advantages new, more costly models bring, appear to be very limited. Agent-based approaches, prompt engineering, and further criteria refinement are three potential future research avenues. 

Keywords: systematic reviews, large language models, title-abstract screening, systematic review automation 

1arXiv:2610.10633v1 [cs.SE] 7 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Evidence-Based Software Engineering (EBSE) highlights systematic reviews (SRs) as a key method for gathering and synthesizing evidence in software engineering (SE) (Kitchenham et al. 2004). However, conducting SRs is labor-intensive and time-consuming (Felizardo et al. 2025). According to Marshall and Wallace (2019), a single SR can take an average of 67 weeks to complete, with paper screening representing one of the most demanding phases. Prior work has shown that humans spend approxi-mately 89 seconds screening each individual paper (Huotala et al. 2024), which makes paper screening a substantial bottleneck in the SR process as thousands of papers may require screening. Automating or supporting paper screening has therefore attracted considerable attention. Earlier approaches have used traditional Machine Learning (ML) and Nat-ural Language Processing (NLP) techniques (Hamel et al. 2020), while recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been used because of their ability to reason over textual input (Huotala et al. 2024, 2025a; Felizardo et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Delgado-Chaves et al. 2025; Shameem et al. 2026; Thode et al. 2025; Tran et al. 2023; Matsui et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2024; Serretti 2026; Trad et al. 2025; Adam et al. 2026). Existing studies show promise for paper screening using LLMs, as LLMs achieve performance compa-rable to that of non-expert human screeners (Huotala et al. 2024, 2025a; Felizardo et al. 2024). Yet, against expert human screeners, LLMs still make a considerable number of mistakes stemming from sources such as different interpretations of key terms (Mäntylä et al. 2026). Guidelines for LLM usage have been proposed to miti-gate their errors (Madeyski et al. 2026). Human-in-the-loop systems have also been developed to support researchers in applying LLMs and other automation methods to paper screening (Huotala et al. 2026a; Deizepe et al. 2026; Afonso et al. 2026; Ouzzani et al. 2016). This paper extends our prior work (Huotala et al. 2025a), which introduced the SESR-Eval dataset for monitoring artificial intelligence (AI) performance in paper screening. Nine LLMs were benchmarked, and most of them performed similarly. How-ever, the differences in screening accuracy between the secondary studies were greater than differences between LLMs, but LLMs were fairly cheap to use per secondary study. While the benchmark dataset can be used to benchmark LLMs, LLMs were still not recommended for automating paper screening process. Next we explain the difference between this paper and Huotala et al. (2025a). First, the evaluated LLMs covered only a single generation of models, prompt, and an out-put schema, which leaves it unclear whether newer LLMs, different user prompts or output schemas would further improve screening performance and make paper screen-ing feasible with LLMs. Claims of earlier generation models may no longer accurately reflect the capabilities of newer models in paper screening, and benchmarking addi-tional LLMs for paper screening benefit researchers and practitioners in choosing a cost-efficient LLM with an acceptable performance for the task (RQ1 – RQ2). Bench-marking LLMs on a full dataset (N = 34,528) may not be financially sensible, as LLMs are generally expensive to run (Felizardo et al. 2025), and token costs of LLMs have increased due to test-time inference and additional model capabilities, such as 2thinking models (RQ1.2). Computing the overall decision separately from criterion-level decisions can possibly reduce hallucinations, falsely judged screening decisions, and allows for additional flexibility (RQ2). Second, the agreement in overall screening decisions and across inclusion and exclu-sion criteria was not analyzed in the paper, even though it could reveal whether LLM screening decisions are uniform across different models, or sensitive to the selected model (RQ3.1 – RQ3.2). Third, the modification of inclusion and exclusion criteria was not studied in the paper, even when certain criteria cannot possibly be evaluated purely by title and abstract. Improving poorly formatted, unclear, or ambiguous criteria may increase the likelihood of a correct overall or criterion screening decision, which directly motivates RQ4. We therefore address the following research questions: 

RQ1: How do LLMs perform in paper screening? 

RQ1.1: How do LLMs perform in paper screening on the full SESR-Eval dataset? 

RQ1.2: How do LLMs perform in paper screening on the sampled SESR-Eval-Mini dataset? 

RQ2: How does computing the overall screening decision from criterion-level decisions affect LLM screening performance on the sampled data? 

RQ3: To what extent do LLMs agree with each other? 

RQ3.1: To what extent do LLMs agree with each other in their overall screening decisions? 

RQ3.2: To what extent do LLMs agree with each other in individual screening criteria? 

RQ4: To what extent does refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria affect LLM screening performance? 

For RQ1, we benchmark eight new state-of-the-art LLMs against seven older ones. On the full dataset (RQ1.1), we benchmark two of the new LLMs, and all eight new LLMs on a smaller 5% sample, SESR-Eval-Mini (RQ1.2). We then investigate how prompt design and output structuring affect screening performance in RQ2. For RQ3, we analyze agreement in overall screening decisions across LLMs (RQ3.1) and also across individual inclusion and exclusion criteria (RQ3.2). Finally, for RQ4, we examine whether refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria improves screening performance. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 provides the necessary background. Section 4 describes the methodology, and Section 5 presents the results. # 2 Related Work 

Table 1 presents related work on using LLMs to automate paper screening in SRs. The remainder of the section presents the current state of LLM-assisted paper screening in multiple disciplines / domains. LLM-assisted paper screening has been studied in various disciplines, including SE (Huotala et al. 2024, 2025a; Felizardo et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Delgado-Chaves et al. 2025; Shameem et al. 2026; Thode et al. 2025), medicine (Tran et al. 2023; Matsui et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2024; Serretti 2026; Trad et al. 2025; Adam et al. 2026), biomedicine Dennstädt et al. (2024), health technology assessment (Józwiak et al. 2026; Qiu et al. 2026), veterinary medicine (Sujau et al. 2025), and artificial intelligence (Behrouzian et al. 2026). Next, we first go over software engineering stud-ies, followed by studies from other disciplines, and conclude with a summary on the related work. 

2.1 Software Engineering 

Huotala et al. (2024) found that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 LLMs perform as well as Mas-ter’s students in paper screening. By simplifying abstracts they were faster to read, but simplification did not increase human performance. GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 LLMs had a precision and recall of (0.650, 0.176) and (0.500, 0.417), respectively. Including task examples in the screening task (one-shot (OS) and few-shot (FS)) was observed to improve screening performance, but the authors must have a list of high-quality seed papers to start with. The usage of prompt optimization was recommended. 4Huotala et al. (2025a) created the SESR-Eval benchmark dataset for benchmarking LLMs in paper screening, and nine LLMs were benchmarked in paper screening using the dataset. Most LLMs performed similarly in paper screening, and the performance differences between secondary studies were greater than for LLMs. GPT-4.1 mini performed the best, but small models, such as Ministral 8B and GPT-4.1 Nano, had poor screening performance. The cost of using LLMs to screen papers was relatively low. The authors concluded that LLMs are not yet recommended for automating paper screening, since accuracy varied widely across secondary studies, and as no LLM managed a high recall with reasonable precision. Felizardo et al. (2024) evaluated the accuracy of GPT-4 (ChatGPT) in paper screening for two SRs. They replicated the first selection phase of two SRs with the help of ChatGPT, where the LLMs’ screening results were compared to those of humans. The results were that the overall accuracy in paper screening was 80.7%, and that in both SRs the LLMs made false judgments regarding included and excluded papers. For the first SR, there were 16 false negatives (FN) and 17 false positives (FP). For the second SR, there were 35 FNs and 27 FPs. The recommendation was not to rely completely on LLMs for paper screening, but LLMs can be a valuable tool with SRs as they can mitigate bias and inaccuracies in the included evidence. Li et al. (2024) selected benchmark datasets from the SYNERGY dataset. Using that data, they benchmarked nine LLMs in terms of overall accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The results of the LLMs were then compared to gold-standard human decisions. The performance of LLMs varied significantly, but GPT-4 demonstrated a good balance of sensitivity and specificity and the accuracy was consistently in the high 90%s. This indicates a high potential for LLMs in paper screening tasks, but they are not yet ready to fully replace humans in the task. Delgado-Chaves et al. (2025) benchmarked 18 LLMs in paper screening. They picked three existing SRs, from which about 6,500 primary studies were evaluated by 18 different LLMs. From the primary studies, 92%, 88%, and 40% of the papers were correctly classified by the LLMs, respectively. There was a significant variation between secondary studies, and the structure of the inclusion and exclusion criteria significantly affected the performance of LLMs in the task. The best-performing LLMs in terms of Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) were GPT-4o (MCC=0.349) and GPT-4o mini (MCC=0.342). The authors emphasized that extra caution should be taken when articulating inclusion and exclusion criteria. A workload reduction between 33% and 93% was observed during paper screening. Shameem et al. (2026) followed a mixed-methods research to benchmark LLMs on multiple phases of SRs - including formulating research questions, generating search strings, quality assessment, developing taxonomies, and paper screening. First, they selected four SRs as "benchmark studies" (BS), and GPT-4o was used to screen the relevant papers. For all benchmark studies (BS1, BS2, BS3, BS4), GPT-4o correctly classified 64.5%, 68%, 58%, and 72% of papers correctly, respectively. Recall was 55.17%, 50%, 100%, and 72%, for the respective benchmark studies. Precision was 76.1%, 31.2%, 55.3%, and 72%, respectively. However, there were certain domain-specific challenges (e.g., domain-specific literature) that humans would have identified, but the LLM failed to do so. 5Thode et al. (2025) benchmarked five LLMs in paper screening, and used the SR’s labeled studies as the ground truth. They designed three different prompts for the task. In addition, they created a dual-LLM system to test how a two-reviewer screening workflow would work with LLMs. As a result, LLMs exhibited a high recall (up to 98%) but low precision (27%). The dual-LLM system managed an average recall of 99% with a precision of 27%. The best-performing LLM was Meta Llama 2 with an average recall of 98%, while GPT-4 Turbo had the highest average precision (72%). 

2.2 Other Disciplines 

Dennstädt et al. (2024) used LLMs to screen papers based on the predefined inclu-sion and exclusion criteria and the paper’s title and abstract. The performance of LLMs in paper screening varied depending on the LLM being used and on the dataset. FlanT5 was the best-performing model with sensitivity (recall / true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate) of 94.48%/31.78%. OpenHermes-NeuralChat, Mix-tral and Platypus 2 were trailing quite a bit behind, with sensitivity/specificity of 97.58%/19.12%, 93%/75.19% and 97.58%/38.34% for the three models, respectively. Even small changes to the user prompt or the Likert scale from 1-5 to 1-10 significantly impacted the screening performance of LLMs. Tran et al. (2023) evaluated the GPT-3.5 Turbo LLM for paper screening by first developing prompts that aim to mimic the process of screening by human researchers. The LLM was tested on 22,666 papers. The LLM in the paper screening task had a sensitivity of 97.1%, but with poor specificity (37.7%). A workload reduction of 65% was observed. While the tested LLM had a good sensitivity, human intervention was recommended because of the poor specificity. Matsui et al. (2024) evaluated paper screening with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 LLMs in two SRs containing 1,381 and 3,146 papers, respectively. Both of the LLMs had a fairly high paper screening throughput - about 110 records per minute. The total screening task was completed in two hours. In the first SR, GPT-3.5 had a sensitivity/speci-ficity of 0.900/0.709 versus GPT-4’s 0.806/0.996. For the second SR, the performance was fairly similar, with sensitivity/specificity of GPT-3.5’s 0.958/0.116 versus GPT-4’s 0.875/0.855. A deeper analysis found that GPT-3.5 excluded papers because of insufficient domain knowledge, while GPT-4 misinterpreted inclusion criteria. Guo et al. (2024) benchmarked the GPT-4 LLM in paper screening. They screened over 24,000 title-abstract pairs across six clinical reviews. GPT-4 had an accuracy of 91.0%, F1 score of 0.60, sensitivity of excluded papers of 0.91, and a sensitivity of included papers of 0.76. The agreement between models was high (kappa=0.96) and the LLMs could give justifications for their incorrect classifications. The authors concluded that LLMs have the potential to improve SR efficiency and lead to more accurate and reliable conclusions in medical research. Serretti (2026) developed a fully local LLM pipeline using the open-weight gpt-oss-20b LLM to benchmark title/abstract screening for three SRs. The performance (F1 score) of the LLM in paper screening was 98.9%, 76.0%, and 83.6% for the three SRs, respectively. The findings demonstrate that a fully locally run LLM can support researchers in paper screening. 6Trad et al. (2025) evaluated the performance of a commercial paper screening tool called Rayyan, and an LLM-based paper screening system in automating the screening process. Out of 14,439 papers, two thousand papers were used to train the paper classifier of Rayyan. The paper screening performance was analyzed using different thresholds for paper inclusion. While Rayyan performed adequately (exclusion rate of 50.7%-72.1%), the LLM-based screening system was far superior, achieving an exclusion rate of 99.5%, and a workload reduction of 95.5%, as the screening took 25.5 hours. The paper demonstrates that LLM-based screening systems are far superior compared to traditional ML-based systems, such as Rayyan. Adam et al. (2026) conducted a large experiment with 515 different prompts against open-source LLMs (Meta Llama and Mistral) to study how the prompt struc-ture affects screening performance in four SRs from the medical domain. The resulting F1, recall and precision were 27.6%, 83.6% and 18.5%, respectively. The paper screen-ing performance was observed to increase significantly when 1) larger-parameter LLMs were used; 2) the prompt included the title of the SR being conducted; and 3) the prompt included the selection criteria. Screening accuracy decreased when abstracts were used in screening rather than paper titles. The best-performing prompt achieved 72.2% recall and 66.1% specificity. This translated into a workload reduction of around 34.5–37.5%. The paper highlighted that there is a precision-recall tradeoff to be made and the prompt structure significantly affected screening performance. Józwiak et al. (2026) benchmarked Gemini 3 Flash and GPT-5.2 LLMs in paper screening and experimented with how prompt optimization could improve paper screening performance. A dataset of 100 papers was constructed from the papers that had the highest inter-model disagreement ratio, and was labeled by experts for the experiment. Prompt optimization improved recall for all LLMs by +3.7% to +37.1%. The best-performing LLM was Gemini 3 Flash with an accuracy of 91%, and F1 score of 81.6%, while costing 25 times less than GPT-5.2 per abstract. Qiu et al. (2026) benchmarked LLMs for paper screening by using a stepwise training framework with three studies - one for each phase. The first study was used to optimize the technical parameters of the experiment with 1000 abstracts and 50 full texts. The second and third studies validated this approach. The LLMs achieved 100% sensitivity and a specificity between 85.6% and 93%. Full-text screening had an accuracy between 82.1% and 96.2%. Sujau et al. (2025) evaluated the effectiveness of LLMs to rank primary studies for climate-sensitive zoonotic disease research. The dataset contained 2905 title-abstract pairs that were related to four zoonotic diseases, and a set of paper selection crite-ria was constructed. Five researchers screened abstracts using the defined criteria. After that, the papers were screened using GPT-4o LLM with zero-shot (ZS) and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting techniques. A workload reduction of at least 70% was achieved, while the LLM maintained a recall of 95%. The authors emphasized that further research is needed on standardized benchmark datasets, but the findings demonstrate that LLMs can substantially reduce the screening effort. Behrouzian et al. (2026) used GPT-5.1 and GPT-3.5 Turbo LLMs to bench-mark paper screening against a dataset from the Cochrane review corpus, which contained 1,000 abstracts. The recall of GPT-5.1 and GPT-3.5 Turbo ranged from 70.72 to 0.32 and 0.92 to 0.18, respectively. Excluding papers immediately, rather than labeling papers as unsure, made both models perform better with consistency. This demonstrated that LLMs do not handle uncertainty well in paper screening. 

2.3 Summary 

The common consensus of the related work is that LLMs show significant promise in paper screening, by reliably reducing the workload of practitioners and researchers in paper screening tasks, while LLMs have been observed to perform as well as non-expert humans in the task. Despite these promises, LLMs are not yet recommended for fully replacing humans in paper screening, as the screening performance is greatly affected by the structure and clarity of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the structure of the prompt, certain domain-specific issues, and the trade-off of selecting a cost-effective LLM instead of a state-of-the-art LLM to reduce costs. Existing literature predominantly treats paper screening as a binary classification task, as they examine only the overall screening performance, and provide only a minimal criterion-level analysis of how LLMs handle specific inclusion/exclusion cri-teria or whether the performance varies across criteria types. Furthermore, only a few studies investigate inter-LLM agreement and the impact of dataset sampling, or how refining criteria affects paper screening performance. 

# 3 Background 

In this section, we introduce the core concepts for this study. First, we describe the paper screening process in SRs. Then, we describe paper screening as a classification task, and how the criterion-level decisions are aggregated into an overall screening decision. Finally, we discuss the agreement between multiple screeners. 

3.1 Inclusion and exclusion criteria 

In SRs, inclusion (IC) and/or exclusion (EC) criteria are applied to research papers’ titles and abstracts to determine whether the paper should be included or excluded (Kitchenham and Charters 2007). Excluded papers are discarded, and the remaining included papers are further examined in the full-text screening phase (Kitchenham and Charters 2007). Each of these criteria map to a specific research question (RQ) of the study, so the selected primary studies have the possibil-ity to contain an answer to the RQ. The SR guidelines state that the criteria should be crafted during the SR protocol development, and may be refined during the lit-erature search process (Kitchenham and Charters 2007). Importantly, criteria should be formulated so that they can be reliably assessed and classify studies with high accuracy. Some inclusion and exclusion criteria may be applied to the paper’s metadata, such as language, date of publication, and publisher information. While human researchers can easily determine whether a paper was published in a certain year or period, LLMs can have issues making such decisions without having access to the paper’s metadata or, in some cases, the publisher’s copyright statement in the abstract (Felizardo et al. 82025). In addition, it is noted that criteria that assess the language of the paper, or are biased towards the knowledge of the authors, can influence the decisions based on the criteria (Kitchenham and Charters 2007; Kitchenham et al. 2004). The implications of these observations raise important questions regarding how LLMs handle screening based on a set of rules. First, since inclusion and exclusion criteria have differences in how they can be assessed objectively, decisions made by LLMs can be more consistent with certain criteria than with others. This motivates RQ3, where we analyze how LLM screening decisions actually agree across paper selection criteria (RQ3.2). Second, as Kitchenham and Charters (2007) states that the criteria may be refined during the literature search process, it remains uncharted whether such refinement also improves the performance of LLMs in paper screening. This motivates our RQ4, which investigates the effect of refining the paper selection criteria on LLM screening performance. 

3.2 Title-abstract screening as a classification task 

Title-abstract screening (paper screening) can be considered a binary classification task (Du et al. 2024; van de Schoot et al. 2021), where each paper is either included or excluded based on its title and abstract (Kitchenham and Charters 2007). However, SRs typically include multiple inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the overall inclusion/exclusion decision depends on how individual criterion-level decisions are combined or aggregated. A common pattern is that a paper is excluded if it matches even one exclusion criterion, or if it does not match every inclusion criterion. However, variations of these rule combinations do exist. This complexity of handling multiple criteria raises important questions about inter-screener agreement, and how criterion-level decisions should be aggregated. This also motivates our RQ3.1 and RQ3.2. When LLMs are applied in paper screening, a common pattern is that the LLM produces an overall decision in binary format. This binary classification task can be expanded to support probabilistic (a floating point between 0.0 and 1.0), or e.g., Likert-scale decisions (Felizardo et al. 2024; Huotala et al. 2025a). The aggregation behind the overall decision of the LLM can be done in two ways. In the first way, the LLM produces judgments for each criterion and also the overall decision. This adds a considerable amount of flexibility, but there is an issue. Because most LLMs are decoder-only transformers (Vaswani et al. 2023), they predict the next token(s) based on the earlier tokens, the overall decision may contradict the LLM’s own criterion-level judgments, if e.g., the overall decision is generated before the criterion-level decisions. In the second way, the LLM produces only the judgments for the criteria, and the overall decision is aggregated manually from the judgments of the criteria. The benefit here is that the decision boundary can be adjusted flexibly, if one wants to e.g., maximize recall (Huotala et al. 2025a). However, if a study has a significant number of criteria, a single incorrect criterion judgment is enough to falsely exclude a possibly relevant paper. The standard SR methodology recommends that at least two independent review-ers screen papers, to reduce bias and ensure rigor (Kitchenham and Charters 2007). The benefit of multiple reviewers can be formalized by the Condorcet Jury Theorem (List and Goodin 2001; Shteingart et al. 2020), which demonstrates that when individual decision-makers make the correct binary decision with a higher than 50/50 chance, the accuracy of a majority vote increases as the number of independent decision-makers increases (Figure 1) (List and Goodin 2001; Shteingart et al. 2020). Empirical evidence supports this principle. A study by Abramowitz et al. (2025) found that multiple independent reviewers provide more reliable quality assessment than a single reviewer in peer review contexts. Three conditions of the Condorcet Jury Theorem are important when LLMs screen papers. First, the benefit of multiple raters requires a majority. Second, with two LLMs as decision makers, a majority vote cannot resolve a possible disagreement. Using two LLMs to screen is therefore only useful to flag disagreements, or to include a paper if either of the models includes it. This increases recall, but lowers precision. Third, the LLMs making the decisions must reach their decisions independently. It is known that LLMs share much of their training data, so choosing e.g. models from only a single provider might reduce the collaborative usage of these LLMs, and most likely the errors of the LLMs are correlated. A high agreement between LLMs is therefore ambiguous. This might indicate that the LLMs themselves are reliable, or they make the same mistakes, which makes it pointless to use multiple LLMs for screening. A comprehensive evaluation of LLM reliability requires examining both inter-model agreement and the consistency of decisions across different LLMs and criteria. After the LLM selection 1○, we continued with data sampling 2○. We decided to conduct data sampling, as our main goal was to provide a cheaper alternative of SESR-Eval dataset, which is motivated by Table 2 as it shows that newer flagship models have significantly increased input and output token costs. In addition, we did not want to trade better performance for lower costs by choosing smaller LLMs, which are evidently cheaper (Felizardo et al. 2025). As the data source, we used SESR-Eval by Huotala et al. (2025a), which is a gold-standard paper screening dataset. The dataset contains 34,528 pre-labeled primary studies from 24 software engineering secondary studies, which were rigorously sourced from high-reputation SE journals (Huotala et al. 2025b). The labels of the primary studies are the overall screening decisions. For the paper sampling strategy, we decided to use power-allocated sampling with 

α = 0 .5 (Bankier 1988). Power-allocated sampling balances the proportional and equal allocation to control for variance across strata (Bankier 1988). In practice, pure 13 random sampling (a power-allocated sample with an α = 1 .0) at 5% would give us a sample where the smallest secondary study (with 69 primary studies) would get an average of 3.45 primary studies, which would not allow any sort of secondary study-level inference on the accuracy. On the other hand, with an α = 0 .0, all secondary studies would get equal weight, which at 5% would be 72 primary studies for each secondary study ( 34 , 528 × 0.05 / 24). This would distort the overall dataset too much in favor of small studies. The value of α = 0 .5 is a compromise as it balances between equally allocating across all strata, and allocating proportional to the size of each stratum Bankier (1988). Table 3 lists the secondary studies, their full primary study count and the sampled primary study count. The full dataset used in the experiments contains 34,528 rows, whereas the 5% sample set contains 1,726 papers. One can notice that the sampled data has a different I:E ratio overall (21:79) than the full dataset (12:88). This is because smaller secondary studies had more inclusions and their proportional weight increases in power-allocated sampling. For RQ4, the subset of studies was further reduced to 448 papers, as we selected only those papers that were part of the seven secondary studies most suitable for criteria improvement 5○. The 5% sampled dataset is called the SESR-Eval-Mini dataset, which is available in our prior work’s (Huotala et al. 2025a) research artifact as an update (Huotala et al. 2026c).

4.4 Separating the computation of the overall screening decision (RQ2) 

Figure 3 describes the changes made to the user prompt and the LLM output schema, respectively. The first version (V1) of the user prompt was the same as that used in our prior work (Huotala et al. 2025a). This same user prompt was then used for RQ1.1, RQ1.2, RQ3.1, and RQ3.2, as these RQs were directly compared with the results of our prior work, so no changes were required nor made to the user prompt 3○. For RQ2, the changes made to the user prompt in V2 were tightly coupled with the motivation behind the respective RQ. Removing the overall screening decision field necessitated refining the user prompt, as the screening decision of the LLMs relied on the criterion decisions. Because of this, the instructions given to the LLMs were refined to better guide the LLMs’ interpretation of the criteria. Also for RQ4, the V2 version user prompt was used, because it focused solely on refining inclusion and exclusion criteria; the refinements made for V2 version of user prompt aimed to provide an improved guidance for the LLMs and to reduce the ambiguity of applying criteria 4○. No changes were made for the output schema for RQ1.1, RQ1.2, RQ3.1, or RQ3.2, as the results were directly compared with prior work (Huotala et al. 2025a). On the other hand, significant changes were implemented to the V2 output schema (RQ2 and RQ4). 16 First, the overall decision field was removed from the output schema, as the overall decision was computed from the inclusion and exclusion criterion decisions, making the overall decision field redundant. Second, we added extra validation for the criterion name format as an attempt to improve reliability of the structured responses. The increased number of LLMs tested increases the likelihood of LLMs hallucinating the output, and letting the Pydantic AI library apply stricter output validations is an attempt to mitigate this. Third, the Python classes for Criterion and Decision were split for inclusion and exclusion criteria, as we wanted to make sure that the LLMs rigorously understand the difference between inclusion and exclusion criterion evaluations. Lastly, we clar-ified how the binary and probabilistic decisions are related to each other, e.g. the probabilistic decision means the probability of an inclusion criteria being met, and for exclusion criteria, this means the probability of the exclusion criterion being met. This was to mitigate a potential misunderstanding of the LLMs where the LLM could mis-understand the probability of exclusion as "how certain the LLM is about its answer". If the LLM output JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) that did not meet the out-put schema, Pydantic AI automatically retried the request and instructed the LLM to correct the mistake. Because the overall decision field was removed for RQ2 and RQ4, the overall decision was determined by the results of the inclusion and exclusion criterion. Each secondary study in the SESR-Eval dataset contained inclusion and exclusion criteria, which we converted into YAML Ain’t Markup Language (YAML) files and compared against the original inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure correctness. The resulting YAML files were consumed by the experiment scripts. Each secondary study had rules, by which the inclusion criteria (IC) and exclusion criteria (EC) are evaluated. This information was also provided to the LLM as a boolean formula, e.g. 

> (IC1 AND IC2 AND IC3) AND NOT (EC1 OR EC2)

Both versions of the user prompts and output structures are included in the research artifact (Huotala et al. 2026b). For RQ2, we also computed accuracy, precision, recall, F1, MCC, and lost evidence.

4.6 Modifying criteria (RQ4) 

Our motivation behind modifying criteria in RQ4 was that improving poorly format-ted, unclear, or ambiguous criteria may decrease the likelihood of an incorrect overall or criterion screening decision. As this part was somewhat experimental and as modi-fying criteria takes a lot of time, we focused on the secondary studies that LLMs had challenges making the correct decision for includes and excludes. We began by analyzing the criterion-level agreements from Online resource 3 (Huo-tala et al. 2026b) and looking at Table 8 to see, which secondary studies had most disagreement 5○. We then selected seven secondary studies from Table 8 (Rodríguez-Pérez et al. 2021; Bjarnason et al. 2023; Zhang and Li 2020; Tebes et al. 2020; Alonso et al. 2023; Lenarduzzi et al. 2021; Deckers and Lago 2022). For this experiment, we selected all new LLMs from Table 4 5○, as we wanted to make the results fully comparable with RQ2. The criteria modification process 6○ was performed by the third author. The sec-ond author verified the third author’s criteria modification suggestions. After the criteria modification process was finished, we then re-benchmarked the primary stud-ies using eight LLMs (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GLM 5.1, Kimi K2.6, Grok 4.20 Beta, Qwen 3.6 Plus, and DeepSeek V4 Pro) on the 5% sampled data, filtered to the selected seven secondary studies (N=448), using the V2 user prompt and output schema. For RQ4, we computed accuracy, precision, recall, F1, MCC, Gwet’s First-order Agreement Coefficient (Gwet’s AC1), and lost evidence. Next, we describe each item of the criteria modification process in detail, in Sub-sections 4.6.1 to 4.6.6. The following modifications were made to the criteria (Table 5): 

4.6.1 Expand abbreviations and acronyms 

Abbreviations and acronyms were expanded, as acronyms can be misleading, e.g. if the LLM fails to understand the context of the abbreviation or acronym. For instance, continuous integration (CI) and confidence interval (CI) have matching acronyms, but mean totally different things. This might lead the LLM to misclassify a paper that mentions continuous integration in the abstract, but the LLM interprets the acronym as confidence interval. 18 4.6.2 Split compound criteria into multiple rules where possible 

Long criteria that contain multiple different rules were split into separate, simpler rules so each can be evaluated clearly and independently by the LLM. 

4.6.3 Remove criteria not verifiable from title/abstract (e.g., publication year, venue) 

If the LLM cannot determine a criterion’s outcome by the paper’s title/abstract, it can lead to false negatives or false positives. Paper abstracts rarely contain information regarding e.g. publication year or venue. 

4.6.4 Convert negations to affirmative form 

We converted negations to affirmative form, so they describe the desired criteria directly. Affirmative rules are easier to interpret and we have witnessed LLMs tripping over negation, especially when present in exclusion rules (Mäntylä et al. 2026). 

4.6.5 Preserve original wording — minimize rewording 

We kept the criteria as close as possible to their original wording to preserve the intent of the underlying SR. The rewording was limited to changes needed for consistency, clarity, and LLM interpretability, as larger changes to the criterion could alter the meaning of it. 

4.6.6 If a rule contains vague terms, search the SR for their definition or description 

We searched the SR for definitions and explanations, or contextual descriptions of vague terms used in the criteria. When available, these definitions were incorporated into the criteria to make them more precise. 

4.7 Statistical methods used 

Next, we describe each statistical method we used and motivate their use. For all the metrics (excluding regression model), we used the confusion matrices as a single source of truth, and include them in our research artifact (Huotala et al. 2026b) as recommended by Madeyski et al. (2026). True positives (TP) indicate correctly included studies, while true negatives (TN) indicate correctly excluded studies. On the contrary, false negatives (FN) are studies incorrectly excluded and false positives (FP) are studies incorrectly included. 

4.7.1 Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 

In our experiments we reported accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 scores to make the screening results comparable with prior work, and the corresponding metrics are industry-standard metrics for reporting empirical software engineering research. Accu-racy was used to measure the general correctness of the LLMs, and precision and recall were used to measure the LLM’s correctness in correctly included studies, while 19 F1 presents precision and recall as a harmonic mean (Manning et al. 2008). Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 are computed as follows: 

Accuracy = T P + T N n (1) Where n is the total number of screened studies (included or excluded). 

P recision = T P T P + F P (2) 

Recall = T P T P + F N (3) 

F 1 = 2 × T P 

2 × T P + F P + F N (4) 

4.7.2 MCC 

We reported the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), as it was recommended by Madeyski et al. (2026) for ranking, and because the SESR-Eval dataset (Huotala et al. 2025a) contains secondary studies that sample far more excluded than included papers. The SESR-Eval dataset by Huotala et al. (2025a) used in our experiments contains 34,528 papers, from which 4,197 were labeled as included, and 30,331 as excluded. This gives an I:E ratio of 12:88, which makes the excluded papers a highly prevalent category. The same situation holds for the 5% sampled data, which also had a similar level of class imbalance (21:79). MCC uses the whole confusion matrix and behaves adequately with class imbalance, which the F1 score does not. MCC is computed as follows: 

M CC = T P × T N − F P × F N 

p(T P + F P )( T P + F N )( T N + F P )( T N + F N ) (5) 

4.7.3 Lost evidence 

Lost evidence (FN) is the number and percentage of relevant studies that the LLM excludes incorrectly (false negatives). In the context of SRs, missing relevant papers is more costly than including irrelevant ones, as the irrelevant papers are usually caught in the full-text screening phase (Kitchenham and Charters 2007). Reporting lost evidence is useful because two LLMs can have a similar F1 score, but the lost evidence of one LLM can be significantly higher than the other. The confusion matrices of each experiment are included in our research artifact (Huotala et al. 2026b). 

4.7.4 LLM Agreement - Gwet’s AC1 

Gwet’s AC1 (Gwet 2008) was used instead of Cohen’s kappa as our agreement met-ric. Cohen’s kappa is well known to suffer from the "prevalence problem" 11 making it unsuited for datasets with high category imbalance. In our data, we have secondary studies where nearly all decisions are includes or excludes. Should there be any mis-takes in LLMs’ screening decisions in those studies, Cohen’s kappa would collapse 

> 11 Also known as the prevalence paradox and prevalence effect

20 close to zero as it overestimates chance agreement from the base rate. As our data has a prevalence problem, Gwet’s AC1 becomes the most suitable metric for it. Gwet’s AC1 does not artificially deflate the agreement estimate simply because one category was highly prevalent (Wongpakaran et al. 2013). Gwet’s AC1 is computed as follows: 

AC 1 = pa − pe

1 − pe

(6) where pa is the percentage of observed agreement and pe is the probability of chance agreement. 

4.7.5 Bootstrap confidence intervals and regression model for statistical significance 

Our main metrics, F1 and MCC, come from machine learning and are not typically accompanied by statistical tests. Because these are composite metrics, traditional statistical tests like the t-test are not applicable. Therefore, to test the statistical significance of our results, we reported the 95% percentile bootstrap confidence inter-vals (Efron and Tibshirani 1994) for precision, recall, F1, MCC, lost evidence, and Gwet’s AC1. We used B = 2000 samples. Because the secondary studies in the SESR-Eval dataset differ significantly in the size and I:E ratio, we resampled papers with replacement within each secondary study, so that every resample preserved the com-position of the dataset. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of each metric’s bootstrap distribution formed the 95% confidence interval. For differences between conditions evaluated on the same primary studies, we computed the difference on each resample. This yielded paired confidence intervals, whose difference we considered meaningful when its confidence interval excluded 0. To compare the screening performance between all LLMs and secondary studies, we chose models benchmarked for RQ1.2 (see Table 4). Then, we fit a logistic regression model that predicted the correctness of LLMs’ screening decisions based on the LLM and the primary study. The logistic regression model was fitted and the odds ratios were calculated using the R programming language and the methods glm and coef .We chose the model and secondary study with highest overall probability of correct prediction for the reference classes.

Table 6 and Table 7 show the accuracy, precision, recall, F1, MCC, and lost evidence scores of the tested models for primary studies and secondary studies, respec-tively. We report results for both primary and secondary studies for the same reasons as for our prior work (Huotala et al. 2025a), as some secondary studies contain a large number of papers and therefore dominate the primary study scores. 

Screening performance across primary studies. Across primary studies (Table 6) we see that in terms of MCC, the two new LLMs (DeepSeek V4 Pro and Qwen 3.6 Plus) do not exceed the best-performing old LLMs (GPT-4.1 mini and Llama 4 Maverick, MCC=0.46) that were first benchmarked 18 months prior. The same is observed for F1 score, where DeepSeek V4 Pro and Qwen 3.6 Plus have exactly the same F1=0.48, while the old LLM with the best F1 score was Llama 4 Maver-ick (F1=0.53). In terms of lost evidence for primary studies, both DeepSeek V4 Pro (53%) and Qwen 3.6 Plus (48%) lose less evidence than GPT-4.1 mini (57%), Claude 23 3.7 Sonnet (54%) and DeepSeek R1 (58%), but do not reach the lost evidence lev-els of GPT-4o (34%) or GPT-4.1 (37%). Accuracy, precision, and recall for the two new LLMs are at about the same level as for the seven old LLMs. On average, the old LLMs perform marginally better than the new LLMs (old MCC = 0.429 vs. new MCC = 0.405). 

Screening performance across secondary studies. In Table 7, we can see that there is a four-way tie as the best-performing LLM in paper screening over the full dataset in terms of MCC – the two new LLMs (DeepSeek V4 Pro and Qwen 3.6 Plus) have the same MCC as GPT-4o and Llama 4 Maverick (MCC=0.31). If we look at screening performance between secondary studies (Table 8), the F1 score ranges between 0.10 and 0.88, MCC between -0.11 and 0.64 averaged over all LLMs. Lost evidence varies significantly per-study – the average is 49% while it ranges from 18% to 85%. For studies Zhang and Li (2020) and Pereira et al. (2021), MCC was not defined as they did not contain excluded studies at all (Table 3). Next, if we look at the more detailed two-dimensional table (Online resource 1) (Huotala et al. 2026b), the performance of DeepSeek V4 Pro and Qwen 3.6 Plus are fairly similar compared to the rest of the LLMs, where F1 for DeepSeek V4 Pro ranges from 0.08 to 0.86, and 0.07 to 0.86 for Qwen 3.6 Plus, respectively. Meanwhile, MCC for DeepSeek V4 Pro ranges from -0.27 to 0.70. For Qwen 3.6 Plus, the MCC ranges between -0.26 and 0.65. In terms of lost evidence for secondary studies (Table 7), both DeepSeek V4 Pro and Qwen 3.6 Plus lose 53% and 49% evidence on average, respectively. Compared to older LLMs, this is at almost the same level as o3-mini (51%), and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (55%). The new LLMs are not even close to GPT-4o, which loses only 34% of evidence on average. When looking at the average across secondary studies, the new LLMs perform marginally better than the old LLMs (old MCC = 0.294 vs. new MCC = 0.309). Looking at the cost per secondary study and the time taken to screen one paper (95th percentile), we see that both of the new models (DeepSeek V4 Pro = 103.0s, Qwen 3.6 Plus = 94.7s) are the two slowest models around. The previous slowest model was o3-mini with a screening speed of 86.1s per paper. The fastest LLM to screen one paper is GPT-4.1 mini (10.5s), which is almost ten times faster. In addition, the two models are about as expensive as GPT-4o and GPT-4.1, where DeepSeek V4 Pro costs 12.1 dollars per secondary study, and Qwen 3.6 Plus cost 11.2 dollars per secondary study on average. 

Summary: RQ1.1 results show that the two new models (DeepSeek V4 Pro and Qwen 3.6 Plus) tested on full datasets are not clearly more accurate than the older ones. Average MCC is marginally higher across secondary studies (0.309 vs 0.294) but lower across primary studies (0.405 vs 0.429). Differences between secondary studies are still significant.

5.6 RQ4: Refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria 

In analyzing RQ3.2, we noticed that some criteria could have benefited from refine-ment, and hypothesized that such refinement might lead to improvements in screening accuracy. 5.6.2 Screening accuracy 

Lost evidence (Table 18) indicates that refining the criteria led to a small increase in retaining evidence, i.e. lost evidence reduction (-6), when compared to RQ2. Per-model differences follow the same pattern more or less, where GLM 5.1 exhibited the smallest reduction in lost evidence (-1) compared to DeepSeek V4 Pro (-21), GPT-5.5 (-8) and Claude Opus 4.7 (-4). F1 and MCC (Table 19) show that the average F1 score of the tested LLMs improved slightly from 0.16 to 0.19, whereas MCC decreased from 0.12 to 0.11. Look-ing at the model-level results, DeepSeek V4 Pro had a statistically significant increase in F1 score from 0.17 to 0.27. The remaining seven LLMs exhibited only a slight improvement in F1 score. MCC increased only for Gemini 3.1 Pro (+0.03) and Kimi K2.6 (+0.01). Recall was observed to improve with every LLM, and DeepSeek V4 Pro having a statistically significant increase of +0.20 along with GPT-5.5 (+0.07) and Claude Opus 4.7 (+0.04). The same was observed for lost evidence, where the three LLMs above had also a reduction in lost evidence that was statistically significant. The per-study results (Table 20) were surprising, although the average F1 score improved slightly from 0.16 to 0.19. Average accuracy did not improve, while recall increased from 0.32 to 0.37. Precision also increased from 0.40 to 0.50, which indicates that refining the criteria helped identify more relevant papers and also reduced false positives. MCC degraded by 0.01 on average. At the study level, study Deckers and Lago (2022) showed the strongest improvement in recall, while study Lenarduzzi et al. (2021) showed an increase only in precision and MCC. 

Summary: RQ4 showed modest improvement in LLM agreement, in retaining evidence and recall when original screening criteria were refined. 39 6 Discussion 

6.1 LLM performance (RQ1) 

Despite the 18-month gap between the first execution of SESR-Eval and the re-run in this paper, the newer models on the full dataset (RQ1.1) are at best marginally more accurate than the seven earlier ones. On both MCC and F1 metrics, DeepSeek V4 Pro (F1=0.48, MCC=0.41) and Qwen 3.6 Plus (F1=0.48, MCC=0.40) did not reach the F1 and MCC of older LLMs, such as GPT-4.1 mini (F1=0.50, MCC=0.46). Given that these two new models are approximately five times more expensive than GPT-4.1 mini (the best-performing old LLM in terms of MCC alongside Llama 4 Maverick), conducting paper screening on these expensive models is not recommended. In addition, the new models were among the slowest ones tested. However, the results on the SESR-Eval-Mini dataset (RQ1.2) diverged: Claude Opus 4.7 (MCC=0.46) claimed the crown as the best model for paper screening by a narrow margin over GPT-4.1 mini and Llama 4 Maverick (MCC=0.45). As we did not test Claude Opus 4.7 on the full dataset, we cannot rule out that it is genuinely better. However, its per-token costs make screening thousands of papers expensive, so this gain must be weighed. Taken together, the results suggest that we may be approaching a ceiling, where latency and costs grow faster than screening performance. Claude Opus 4.7 demon-strates that performance improvements are still possible. Part of this latency might be accounted for by the larger models, where additional inference time is required, as was observed for the old LLMs (DeepSeek R1 and o3-mini) in the prior work (Huo-tala et al. 2025a). The inflated token costs across the field (Table 2) might also be accounted for by the larger models, increased demand of LLMs or reasoning tokens, which all necessitate additional compute power. All the new LLMs were tested with reasoning turned on, so this itself inflates costs, even if token prices stay at the same level. Since the new models do not clearly perform better in paper screening, we might have to look somewhere else for improvement. One possibility is that the abstracts may not provide enough information for LLMs to accurately make the screening decision, which has been previously discussed in Felizardo et al. (2025). Human reviewers per-form paper screening using the same information available, so we would assume that LLMs should be able to complete the same task with acceptable accuracy. However, human reviewers bring domain expertise and knowledge of the SR’s goals, which LLMs might have difficulties in. Human reviewers also tend to disagree with one another at this stage, which may cap LLM screening performance regardless of model capability. Providing LLMs with additional context, that may be implicit to human reviewers, about the screening task may therefore be one potential direction for improving screen-ing performance. This would help in keeping costs under control, as larger and newer models tend to have higher per-token costs. Agent-based approaches are a second direction worth studying, as the design paradigm for generative AI has gradually been shifting towards agentic AI. In par-ticular, task decomposition could split the screening process into smaller tasks, with each AI agent evaluating a specific inclusion or exclusion criterion. This could improve 40 screening performance by letting each LLM focus more on a single criteria, and aggre-gating the results in the end. However, multiple AI agents multiply LLM calls per paper, which could further inflate costs. In addition, LLMs could be provided more context about the SR being conducted, so their context aligns better with the interests of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. 

6.2 Effects of prompt design and output structure on screening performance (RQ2) 

Computing the overall screening decision from the criterion-level decision decreased precision, recall, F1, MCC, and accuracy metrics. In addition, the V2 prompt and output structure made LLMs lose more relevant evidence. Adam et al. (2026) tested 515 different prompts, and had an F1 score of 0.276, recall of 0.836 and precision of 0.185. While we tested two versions of prompts and output schemas (V1 and V2), our prompts achieved a better F1 score of 0.47, but precision and recall (0.62 and 0.38) did not have the same pattern. As previously mentioned, targeting a good precision while maximizing recall is the goal for LLM-based paper screening (Huotala et al. 2025a), which our V2 prompt and structure did not yet achieve. This would indicate that the user prompt would need even bigger changes com-pared to the baseline, to reach the goal of good precision with maximized recall. Dennstädt et al. (2024) mentioned that even small changes to the user prompt sig-nificantly impacted the screening performance. Our results show that the changes we implemented to the V2 user prompt impacted screening performance ever so slightly. This would indicate that LLMs behave very differently on certain changes made to the prompt, and more radical changes would have been needed, or modifying the system prompt instead of the user prompt, as LLMs tend to respect and follow the system prompt more strictly than the user prompt. Matsui et al. (2024) and Shameem et al. (2026) observed that LLMs classified papers incorrectly because of insufficient domain knowledge, misinterpreted inclusion criteria, or domain-specific literature. While our changes to the V2 included clarifi-cations to the interpretation of inclusion and exclusion criteria, the amount of lost evidence increased slightly and the overall performance degrades. This result aligns with Matsui et al. and Shameem et al., which would indicate that the LLMs had trouble understanding the criteria, even after our changes were meant to clarify their application and improve understanding. Józwiak et al. (2026) observed that prompt optimization (which is an area of prompt design) improved recall by +3.7% to +37.1% for the LLMs – in our results we observed an average reduction of -0.03 and between -0.06 and +0.01 for the tested LLMs. Our result is the opposite, and we believe that more radical changes are needed to observe a larger difference. 

6.3 LLM agreement with each other and across inclusion and exclusion criteria (RQ3.1 & RQ3.2) 

In our experiments, LLMs generally had an acceptable level of agreement on the overall binary include/exclude decisions (AC1=0.864), and on per-criterion decisions (include AC1=0.778, exclude AC1=0.868). In one prior work, Guo et al. (2024) agreement 41 between models was also acceptable, with a quite high kappa = 0.96. Paradoxically, getting benefits from the Condorcet Jury Theorem, see Section 3, would require us to have high correctness (MCC) in individual LLM decisions but low agreement. Such a setup would provide the biggest benefits, as the mistakes by one LLM would not be repeated by another LLM. Unfortunately, it seems that we have low correctness (MCC) and high agreement between LLMs, making the possible gains of combining decisions limited. In another prior work, Thode et al. (2025) used multiple LLMs and included a paper if either LLM would include it. Obviously, this increased recall while decreasing precision, as each paper gets multiple tries to be included. 

6.4 Inclusion and exclusion criteria refinement (RQ4) 

Refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria produced encouraging and informative results. We hypothesized that ambiguous criteria in the secondary studies were degrad-ing screening performance. The mean F1 score increased only by a margin, from 0.16 to 0.19, showing that rewording the criteria does not alone explain the low precision. However, the Gwet’s AC1 scoring shows that several criteria became considerably eas-ier for the LLMs to interpret. The criteria that combined multiple conditions made the LLMs’ judgment unable to distinguish between the two conditions. For instance, as checking two conditions that a paper was written in English and that it described pro-totypes without a methodological perspective in a single criterion, the LLM lost the language check outcome completely. Therefore, we strongly recommend that inclusion and exclusion criteria that are used with LLMs to screen papers should be singular, with acronyms explained and written in a form that is easy to understand by the LLMs. Using paper metadata in inclusion and exclusion criteria for paper screening is also not advisable, as the LLMs cannot make that information up without being fed additional context or allowing the LLM to retrieve that metadata from a third party source. 

6.5 Limitations and Threats to Validity 

Next, the limitations and threats to the validity of our work are outlined. 

Generalization of results. We benchmarked only SE-specific secondary studies and their primary studies in this work. This was because our prior work (Huotala et al. 2025a), which established the gold-standard dataset used, is itself limited to the SE domain. We suspect our findings might not generalize to other domains, such as medicine or health technology assessment, as the conventions of inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the structure of abstracts can differ. 

Reliability of screening results. There is always the risk that LLMs can hal-lucinate and therefore have an effect on the screening results. We have attempted to mitigate this threat by benchmarking multiple LLMs, to minimize the effect of possible hallucinations the LLMs might have in their token generation chains. 

Only zero-shot prompting technique was tested. We tested only the zero-shot prompting technique, as the additional prompting strategies would have introduced an additional variable that could disrupt the differences between LLMs. However, our prior work evaluated one-shot (OS), few-shot (FS), and few-shot with 42 chain-of-thought (FS-CoT) prompting techniques, which showed that using prompt-ing strategies improves LLM performance (Huotala et al. 2024). Our evaluation in this work therefore reflects the "baseline" result, which is assumed to improve with OS, FS and FS-CoT prompting techniques. 

One LLM did not support structured outputs via native or tool call-ing. In this case, we resorted to prompted output for Qwen 3.6 Plus, where the LLM was instructed in-prompt to output JSON directly rather than by tool-calling or outputting natively. This inflated costs, as the schema had to be included in every request, which also might have increased the possibility of malformed outputs. How-ever, we configured Pydantic AI to retry the requests if the output did not match the expected schema, to minimize this threat. Fortunately, most LLMs supported either tool calling or native output. 

Undefined MCC scores. We noticed that the scripts we used to compute MCC scores for Tables 7 and 10 (and the per-study Tables 8 and 11) warned us about undefined MCC scores, because two of the studies, Zhang and Li (2020) and Pereira et al. (2021), contained no excluded papers, in both the full dataset and the sampled dataset. This makes MCC’s denominator zero regardless of the model’s prediction. We believe this might have negatively impacted the validity of the MCC if averaged over secondary studies, as the zero values could have dragged down the MCC score by approximately 2/24 (8.3%). We decided not to filter out the two studies, so that the average is still taken from the n=24 secondary studies. As the possible reduction of the MCC score here is relative for every LLM, this does not change how the LLMs compare against each other. 

Most new models were run at low reasoning effort. Six of the eight models in the experiments were run with reasoning effort set to low, with Qwen 3.6 Plus at medium and DeepSeek V4 Pro at high. The choice was motivated by prior evidence that the reasoning setting brings negligible gains in screening performance (Huotala et al. 2025a). However, the evidence predates the models evaluated in this paper, and reasoning effort may have different effects for newer models. Our results for the low-effort models should be read as a lower bound rather than their peak performance. Notably, the two models (Qwen 3.6 Plus and DeepSeek V4 Pro) run at different reasoning settings did not have meaningful improvement in screening performance. 

Possibility of training data contamination. The SESR-Eval dataset was released in October 2025, so models released after that date may have been trained on the dataset. However, the data from the secondary studies might be in the train-ing data, as they were released years before the dataset itself. As we observed only marginal performance improvements for the newer models, we believe this threat is unlikely to have affected our benchmark results. It cannot be fully ruled out, however, as most LLM vendors do not disclose what data the models have been trained on. 

Incorrect ground truths in SESR-Eval. It is likely that some ground truth labels in SESR-Eval are incorrect, as the original secondary study authors may have also made mistakes. Furthermore, it is also possible that not all decisions in the SESR-Eval dataset were made based solely on the title and abstract, even though SESR-Eval purposefully sourced studies where such decision points were visible. However, we think it is highly unlikely that such issues alone would explain the rather limited 43 LLM screening performance. Further support that LLM mistakes in screening software engineering papers are real – and not an artifact of poor ground truth – is provided by Mäntylä et al. (2026), where the original secondary study authors screened new papers manually, compared their results with LLM decisions, and analyzed the reasons for LLM screening mistakes. In that study, as in this paper, LLM performance in the screening task was modest. 

Refinement of inclusion and exclusion criteria. The refined inclusion and exclusion criteria are our interpretation of the original authors’ intent. There is a risk that the rewritten or split criteria might not match the original author’s intent. We mitigated this threat by limiting the changes to: 1) expanding abbreviations and acronyms, which does not affect the original meaning of the criterion; 2) splitting compound criteria into separate rules, if there is a distinct separation between them; 3) removing criteria that cannot be verified from the title or abstract, since we do not want the LLMs to make up the information and guess; 4) converting negated criteria to affirmative form, which LLMs generally interpret more reliably; 5) preserving the original wording as much as possible; and 6) clarifying vague terms in the criteria using definitions straight from the paper. In addition, all deleted, reworded, and added rules were reviewed by two of the authors. Nevertheless, as the refinement process was a manual and laborious process, human error cannot be ruled out. 

# 7 Conclusions and future work 

In this paper, we extended the SESR-Eval (Huotala et al. 2025a) study considerably by benchmarking multiple new LLMs for paper screening, creating the new SESR-Eval-Mini dataset, investigating if prompt design and output structuring affected screening performance, analyzing agreement in screening decisions across LLMs and across inclusion and exclusion criteria, and finally, examining whether refining the inclusion and exclusion criteria had any effect on screening performance. Despite the 18-month gap between Huotala et al. (2025a) and this work, we found at most marginal improvements in LLM screening performance. This suggests that the publicly reported advancements of LLMs do not translate into improved screening performance for software engineering papers. Thus, the evidence indicates we must state that LLM progress has largely stalled in this respect. Differences in screening performance are larger between secondary studies than between LLMs, and also with the new LLMs, meaning that the performance varies more based on the study than the used model. This means that while benchmarking new LLMs in screening tasks, it is important to test a broad selection of secondary studies. Following the finding that differences between secondary studies are large, we improved the screening rules for certain secondary studies that performed poorly in screening, but found only modest improvements in screening performance. As improv-ing screening rules makes little difference, this means that secondary study differences are likely to stem from other sources. One such source to be investigated in future studies is the primary studies that each secondary study screens. It is quite possible that some datasets (sets of primary studies) are more difficult to screen correctly than others. 44 Taken together, LLMs are still not reliable enough for replacing humans in the paper screening process of SRs. However, we feel that LLMs can still be a useful companion in paper screening. The current bottleneck of paper screening of LLMs is not the raw compute and model capabilities, but how much context paper abstracts and the prompts contain, and in what kind of prompt the screening task is provided to the LLMs. As future work, we are specifically looking into designing a distributed screening architecture, where inclusion and exclusion criteria are split between multiple small language model (SLM) agents. We believe this approach has high potential in increas-ing screening accuracy, as each SLM agent would focus on evaluating a single criterion rather than juggling between varying amounts of criteria simultaneously while trying to arrive at a decision from a single piece of text. As another future work, we are look-ing into ways that would maximize recall with the usage of probability thresholds, which would open the possibility of using LLMs to filter out studies before human screening. Filtering would be especially valuable in review contexts where the ter-minology used is not settled, as database searches often yield thousands of results for human screening resulting in high amounts of manual work and excluded papers. Lastly, we are looking at investigating the effect of other prompting techniques in a more robust manner, and the combination of few-shot (FS) and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting together with multi-agent systems.
