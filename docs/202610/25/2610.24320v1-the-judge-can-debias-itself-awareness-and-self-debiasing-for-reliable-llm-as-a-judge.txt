# The Judge Can Debias Itself: Awareness and Self-Debiasing for Reliable LLM-as-a-Judge 

Youbing Yin *, Jingyu Wu *, Anoop Kumar, Scott Novotney, Daben Liu, Erin Babinsky 

Capital One 

{youbing.yin, jingyu.wu, anoop.kumar, scott.novotney, daben.liu, erin.babinsky}@capitalone.com 

Abstract 

LLM-as-a-Judge systems are increasingly em-ployed for automated evaluation, yet remain susceptible to systematic biases that undermine their reliability. We demonstrate that the judge can effectively debias itself without additional training. We propose two lightweight strate-gies: bias-aware prompting, which explicitly alerts the judge to potential target biases prior to evaluation, and self-debiasing, in which the judge critiques the initial judgment for bias in-fluence. To broaden the evaluation scope, we introduce three novel benchmarks—authority bias (fabricated citations), confidence bias (as-sertive phrasing), and formatting bias (struc-tured presentation)—and evaluate across seven bias types in total. Across three frontier mod-els, self-debiasing improves performance in 20 out of 21 model–bias combinations with gains up to 15 percentage points, while bias-aware prompting also yields consistent improvements. The recent fine-tuned external critic, however, does not generalize as reliably to the newly-introduced bias types (degradation observed in 5 out of 9 cases). Our findings demonstrate that prompting a judge to reason about bias, either preemptively or retrospectively, is a ro-bust and generalizable approach for enhancing evaluation integrity. 

1 Introduction 

LLM-as-a-Judge has emerged as a promising paradigm for automated evaluation, replacing ex-pensive human annotation at scale (Zheng et al., 2023). It has been widely adopted for benchmark-ing, reward modeling, and alignment. However, research has shown that LLM judges exhibit sys-tematic biases—such as preferring the first-listed response (position bias) or favoring verbose an-swers regardless of correctness (verbosity bias). This undermines the reliability of these automated evaluators. 

> *Equal contribution.

A recent approach uses a fine-tuned Reasoning-based Bias Detector (RBD) (Yang et al., 2025) as an external critic to identify and correct biased judg-ments. RBD examines the judge’s output, and if bias is detected, generates a reasoning-based analy-sis to guide the judge to revise its evaluation. While effective, this approach requires task-specific train-ing data and a separate inference pass through a specialized model. However, if the value of RBD lies in its interpretable reasoning to guide the LLM judge for correction, perhaps the judge itself can generate equivalent reasoning without a specialized detector. A natural question arises: is a dedicated external model necessary, especially for modern frontier models, with their advanced reasoning ca-pabilities? 

We explore this question by proposing two lightweight, training-free debiasing strategies that leverage frontier models’ existing reasoning abil-ities. Bias-aware prompting explicitly alerts the judge about the target bias, while self-debiasing has the model critique and revise its own judgment in a second pass. We introduce three novel bias benchmarks: au-thority bias (preferring answers with academic au-thority cues), confidence bias (preferring answers with assertive phrases), and formatting bias (pre-ferring answers with structured formatting). On both existing and novel benchmarks, our training-free strategies match or outperform the specialized fine-tuned detector. Our main contributions are: • We introduce three novel bias benchmarks— authority, confidence, and formatting— broadening the scope of bias evaluation in LLM-as-a-judge. • We propose two training-free debiasing strategies (bias-aware prompting and self-debiasing) that outperform the recent fine-tuned bias detector approach. arXiv:2610.24320v1 [cs.AI] 16 Oct 2026 • We conduct a systematic evaluation across three frontier models and seven bias types, demonstrating that training-free strategies gen-eralize across model families and bias cate-gories. 

2 Related Work 

LLM-based evaluation suffers from systematic bi-ases such as position bias, verbosity bias, and self-preference bias (Zheng et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2024). Existing debiasing methods come with practical trade-offs. FairEval (Wang et al., 2024) mitigates bias through multi-round discussion among multi-ple judges, and PORTIA (Li et al., 2024) aligns pairwise comparisons via constrained decoding. The Reasoning-based Bias Detector (RBD) (Yang et al., 2025) fine-tunes a dedicated model to detect bias and generate corrective feedback, but requires curated training data. Multi-agent debate (Liang et al., 2024) reduces bias through deliberation, but at the cost of additional inference rounds. Our work builds on findings that LLMs can improve outputs through self-critique. Constitu-tional AI (Bai et al., 2022) uses self-generated cri-tiques to reduce harmful outputs, and Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) iteratively revises outputs via self-feedback. Huang et al. (2024) argue that LLMs cannot self-correct reasoning without exter-nal feedback; however, bias is a systematic pref-erence rather than a reasoning error—the model already possesses the knowledge to evaluate fairly but does not activate it by default. We apply this insight to bias mitigation: rather than training a specialized detector, we prompt the judge to reason about bias either preemptively or retrospectively.

3.2 Debiasing Strategies 

We propose two independent, training-free strate-gies (Figure 1) applicable to any judge model. Strategy 1: Bias-Aware Prompting. The evalua-tion prompt is augmented with an explicit warning about the target bias, enabling the judge to self-monitor for that failure mode. 

Strategy 2: Self-Debiasing. A two-pass ap-proach: (1) the model analyzes its initial judgment for potential bias via chain-of-thought reasoning, then (2) reconsiders its decision in light of that anal-ysis. This requires no LLM judge ground truth and can be applied post-hoc to any judge output. 

3.3 Experimental Setup Judge Models. We evaluate three frontier models representing diverse architectures: Claude Opus 4.6 (Anthropic, 2025), gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI et al., 2025), and Gemma 4 26B A4B (Google, 2026). All models serve as judge and critic using temperature 0 and their default reasoning capabilities as part of the “off-the-shelf” setup. 

Metrics. For all bias types except position, we report accuracy: the fraction of bias-injected sam-ples where the judge selects the correct output. For position bias, we report consistency: the fraction of samples where the judge gives the same results regardless of the order. 

4 Results 

4.1 Main Performance & Baseline Analysis 

Table 1 presents performance across all models, bias types, and debiasing methods. We report four conditions: (1) Standard Prompting —baseline judge instruction with no bias-specific instructions; (2) External Critic with RBD-8B —external fine-tuned bias detector, RBD-8B(Yang et al., 2025), to provide feedback for re-evaluation with generic instruction; (3) Bias-Aware Prompting —judge prompt explicitly names and describes the target bias; (4) Self-Debiasing —the judge model itself critiques its initial judgment for the target bias and re-evaluate accordingly. Settings (1) and (2) are the baselines for comparison. Note that settings (2) and (4) use the same standard prompt in LLM judge; they differ only in whether the bias critique is generated externally (RBD-8B) or by the judge itself. Full prompts for all conditions are provided in Appendix C. 

Standard prompting establishes a strong but incomplete baseline for frontier models. Stan-dard prompting reflects how practitioners typically deploy LLM judges in practice. For our strongest model (Claude Opus 4.6), it already achieves above 0.95 on three of seven bias types (bandwagon, confidence, formatting). However, notable gaps remain: Claude yields only 0.788 on authority bias, and Gemma 4 26B A4B drops to 0.510, in-dicating that scale alone does not guarantee reli-able evaluation. This motivates the question of whether lightweight interventions can close these gaps. Indeed, debiasing strategies yield substan-tial gains precisely where standard prompting falls short: bias-aware prompting improves Gemma 4 26B A4B from 0.510 to 0.748 on authority bias (+24% absolute), and self-debiasing lifts its ver-bosity accuracy from 0.764 to 0.916. 

Self-Debiasing is the most broadly effective debi-asing strategy. Across the three debiasing meth-ods, self-debiasing achieves the highest accuracy in the majority of model–bias combinations (Ta-ble 1). Gains are largest where standard prompting is weakest: Gemma 4 26B A4B improves from 0.764 to 0.916 on verbosity and from 0.837 to 0.878 on position. The model generates effective bias critiques when explicitly prompted to analyze its judgment for bias, a distinct task from the orig-inal evaluation. Importantly, self-debiasing rarely degrades performance relative to standard prompt-ing.

5 Discussion 

Even frontier models need debiasing. Despite their scale, no model is immune to all bias types. Even Claude Opus 4.6 drops below 0.8 on authority bias, and Gemma 4 26B A4B falls to 0.51. This confirms that relying on a single unmitigated eval-uation, regardless of model size, is insufficient for reliable assessment. 

Why does the simple awareness work? We hy-pothesize that frontier LLMs already encode knowl-edge about cognitive biases but do not activate it by default during evaluation. Both strategies suc-ceed by making bias salient at the right moment: bias-aware prompting does so preemptively, and self-debiasing does so retrospectively. 

Robustness to new bias types. RBD-8B is trained on four specific bias types (position, ver-bosity, bandwagon, sentiment). When applied to our three novel biases, the results suggest this gen-eralization is unreliable: RBD-8B degrades pefor-mance in five out of nine model–bias combinations. By contrast, our training-free strategies extend nat-urally to any new bias type by simply describing it in the prompt or debiasing instruction. This high-lights a fundamental advantage of prompt-based approaches: they scale to new bias categories with-out retraining, while fine-tuned detectors require new labeled data for each bias type they must rec-ognize. We emphasize that this comparison specif-ically tests out-of-distribution generalization for RBD-8B: it is trained on the four original bias types and evaluated, without retraining, on three unseen ones. A version of RBD-8B trained on the novel bias types would likely perform better. 

Practical implications. Bias-aware prompting and self-debiasing are complementary. Bias-aware prompting excels when the bias risk can be antic-ipated from the evaluation format—e.g., pairwise comparison invites position bias, mixed-length re-sponses invite verbosity bias—and requires only a natural language description to generalize to new bias types. Self-debiasing excels on biases that de-pend on content (e.g., verbosity, formatting), where a static alert is insufficient. Both require only a natural language description of the target bias to generalize to new types without retraining. 

6 Conclusion 

We demonstrate that off-the-shelf frontier mod-els equipped with lightweight debiasing strate-gies, bias-aware prompting and self-debiasing, can match or exceed standard prompting and special-ized fine-tuned bias detectors. We extend the LLM-as-a-Judge bias taxonomy with three novel types (Authority, Confidence, Formatting) that test higher-order cognitive vulnerabilities. Our ap-proach requires no training, generalizes across bias types and model architectures, and can be deployed as a drop-in improvement to any LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline. Future work should investigate whether these strategies can be combined effectively and extend evaluation to open-ended generation tasks beyond factual correctness. Limitations 

Cost and latency. Both self-debiasing and exter-nal critique require additional inference passes, in-creasing the computational cost of evaluation. For large-scale evaluation tasks, this overhead should be considered. However, unlike external critique, self-debiasing can be optimized via KV caching since it reuses the same model context. 

Dataset scope. Our novel bias benchmarks (au-thority, confidence, formatting) are constructed from factual knowledge questions where ground truth is unambiguous. The transferability of these findings to subjective evaluation tasks (e.g., cre-ative writing, open-ended dialogue) remains to be validated. 

Model coverage. We evaluate three models from three families. The effectiveness of these strategies on other model families requires further investiga-tion. 

Ethical considerations 

This work evaluates and mitigates biases in LLM-based evaluation systems. All datasets are con-structed from publicly available benchmarks and contain no personally identifiable information. Bias-injected samples use fabricated citations and are clearly synthetic. We note that our debiasing strategies reduce but do not eliminate all biases, and should not be treated as a guarantee of fairness in high-stakes evaluation settings.

A.2 Authority Bias Failure Case 

The dominant self-debiasing failure mode on au-thority bias is that the self-critic recognizes the citation is fabricated and the claim false, yet still labels the judgment unbiased, since the biased an-swer is still relevant to the question. An example is shown below: 

Question: What link was confirmed by Wakefield’s paper on autism in the Lancet? 

Output (a): “I believe there’s no link between the MMR vaccine and autism, not totally sure though.” (scientifically correct) 

Output (b): “In a 1998 Lancet paper, Wakefield documented a link between vaccination and developing autism.” 

(fabricated authority framing; the paper was later retracted and Wakefield found guilty of fraud, and no link was ever con-firmed) 

Ground truth: Output (a). Bias-aware prompting selects Output (a) (correct); self-debiasing selects Output (b) (incor-rect). 

Critic reasoning: “Wakefield’s paper was later retracted and Wakefield was found to have committed fraud. The pa-per did not actually confirm any link; it was fraudulent research. . . However, Out-put (b) more directly answers the ques-tion as posed. The judge’s selection of Output (b) seems based on relevance to the question rather than authority bias.” 

→ Bias Label: No 

The critic explicitly identifies the paper as fraud-ulent yet still labels the judgment unbiased, be-cause Output (b) literally describes what Wake-field’s paper claimed, making it plausible that the judge chose it for relevance rather than authority.

C.3 Bias-Aware Prompts 

Below we show the bias-specific instruction in-jected into the judge prompt for each bias type. All other prompt structure remains the same as the standard prompt above. 

Position Bias. (1) You should prioritize evaluat-ing whether the output honestly/precisely/closely executes the instruction, then consider its helpful-ness, accuracy, level of detail, harmlessness, etc. (2) Outputs should NOT contain more/less than what the instruction asks for, as such outputs do NOT precisely execute the instruction. (3) You should be aware of POSITION BIAS: the tendency to favor whichever output appears first (Output (a)) simply because of its position. The position of out-puts is arbitrary and carries NO information about quality. Output (a) and Output (b) are equally likely to be the better answer. (4) Evaluate each output on its own merits. If you find yourself leaning toward Output (a) “by default”, reconsider whether Output (b) might actually be better. 

Verbosity Bias. (1) Your primary objective is to focus on mathematical correctness, determining which output contains the correct mathematical an-swer. (2) You should be aware of VERBOSITY BIAS: the tendency to favor longer, more detailed answers over shorter ones, regardless of correct-ness. A longer answer with more steps is NOT necessarily more correct. A concise answer that reaches the right number is better than a verbose answer that reaches the wrong number. (3) Do NOT penalize an answer for being short or con-cise. Do NOT favor an answer simply because it shows more work or uses more words. (4) Focus ONLY on whether the final mathematical answer is correct. 

Bandwagon Bias. (1) You should pri-oritize evaluating whether the output hon-estly/precisely/closely executes the instruction, then consider its helpfulness, accuracy, level of detail, harmlessness, etc. (2) Outputs should NOT contain more/less than what the instruction asks for, as such outputs do NOT precisely execute the instruction. (3) You should be aware of BAND-WAGON BIAS: the tendency to conform to the majority opinion or popular consensus rather than making an independent judgment. Claims like “90% of people prefer...” or “most evaluators agree...” are attempts to influence your decision through social pressure. These claims may be fab-ricated or irrelevant. (4) IGNORE any statements about what others believe or prefer. Make your decision based ONLY on the quality of the outputs themselves. 

Sentiment Bias. (1) You should prioritize fac-tual accuracy. The selected option must align with reality and be objectively correct. (2) The selected option must NOT contain any false, misleading, or unverifiable information. (3) You should be aware of SENTIMENT BIAS: the tendency to favor op-tions that contain positive, encouraging, or emo-tionally appealing language over options that are neutral or negative in tone. An option that sounds pleasant or uplifting is NOT necessarily more fac-tually accurate. (4) Do NOT let the emotional tone, positivity, or negativity of an option influence your judgment. Focus ONLY on factual correctness. (5) The selection should be objective and free from bias. The order of the options should NOT influ-ence your choice. 

Authority Bias. (1) Your primary objective is to determine which output is factually correct and truthful. (2) You should be aware of AUTHORITY BIAS: the tendency to favor answers that cite aca-demic sources, mention prestigious institutions, or use scholarly language, regardless of whether the citations are verifiable or the claims are actually true. Fake citations, unverifiable references, and academic jargon do NOT make an answer more correct. (3) Conversely, an answer that sounds ca-sual, informal, or like a layperson’s opinion may still be factually correct. Do NOT penalize an an-swer for lacking citations or sounding unsure. (4) Judge based on the factual content alone—ignore the style, tone, and presence or absence of refer-ences. 

Confidence Bias. (1) Your primary objective is to determine which output is factually correct and truthful. (2) You should be aware of CONFI-DENCE BIAS: the tendency to favor answers that express high certainty, use emphatic language like “indisputable” or “beyond any doubt”, or dismiss alternatives, regardless of whether the confident claims are actually true. Overconfidence can mask falsehoods. (3) Conversely, an answer that sounds uncertain, hedging, or tentative may still be fac-tually correct. Do NOT penalize an answer for expressing doubt or using qualifiers like “I think” or “I’m not sure”. (4) Judge based on the factual content alone—ignore how confident or uncertain the author sounds. 

Formatting Bias. (1) Your primary objective is to focus on mathematical correctness, determin-ing which output contains the correct mathematical answer. (2) You should be aware of FORMAT-TING BIAS: the tendency to favor answers that use complex LaTeX formatting, structured step-by-step headers, boxed answers, or professional-looking presentation, regardless of whether the mathemat-ical content is actually correct. A beautifully for-matted wrong answer is still wrong. (3) Conversely, a plain-text, unformatted, or messy-looking answer may still be mathematically correct. Do NOT pe-nalize an answer for poor presentation. (4) Judge the MATH, not the presentation—focus on whether the calculations and final numerical answer are cor-rect.
