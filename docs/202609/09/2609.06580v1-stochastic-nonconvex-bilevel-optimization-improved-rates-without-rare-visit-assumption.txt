Abstract 

We investigate stochastic simple bilevel optimization with smooth and possibly nonconvex upper- and lower-level objectives. Existing stochastic extensions of dynamic barrier gradient descent (DBGD) either obtain fast convergence under an unverifiable trajectory-dependent “rare-visit” assumption, or remove this assumption at a substantially higher oracle cost. We show that a simple denominator-only regularization of the DBGD multiplier eliminates the need for such an assumption while preserving fast convergence rates. Specifically, our method achieves ( ε, ε )-stationarity in O(ε−2) iterations using O(ε−4) upper-level and O(ε−7) lower-level stochastic gra-dients, which improves upon the best assumption-free complexities. We additionally derive anytime parameter schedules. 

Keywords. Simple Bilevel Programming, Stochastic Optimization, Dynamic Barrier.

In this context, the lower-level function g serves as a primary requirement, whereas the upper-level objective f selects amongst lower-level solutions. This problem is also referred to as a nonconvex hierarchical program . Such a formulation has many applications, such as in hyperparameter training [11] or in machine unlearning [19], to name a few. Even in a deterministic setting, nonconvex bilevel programs are hard. The lower-level constraint is global, and may have a nonconvex or even disconnected solution set. Therefore, classical ideas from convex bilevel programming, such as Tikhonov regularizations [15, 21, 3, 10] or forming outer approxiations of the lower-level solution set [14, 5, 6] do not translate. As solving Problem (P) to global optimality is intractable, we merely solve it to ( εf , ε g )-stationarity, which was introduced in [7] and will be rigorously defined in Section 2. It is such that ∇g(x) is small, so that the point is nearly stationary for the lower-level, and such that there is no evident first-order direction that improves the upper-level objective. To achieve this notion of stationarity, Cao et al. [7] make use of Dynamic Barrier Gradient Descent (DBGD), originally proposed in [13], which combines upper- and lower-level descent through an adaptive dual multiplier. Uniformly bounded variance is a standard assumption in stochastic optimization [20, 18], although recent trends aim to relax it under weaker growth or variance-at-solution conditions [4, 17, 16, 2, 9, 12, 8], at the cost of a more complex error handling. We retain the bounded-variance assumption here in order to simplify the results and isolate the difficulties arising from the stochasticity itself. 2Assumption 2.2 does not require the lower-level gradient ∇g to be uniformly bounded, nor does it require the individual realizations fξ and gζ to be smooth. This is in contrast with [1], where boundedness of ∇g is required, and smoothness of the stochastic realizations is used for their variance-reduced variant. Since Problem (P) is nonconvex, we do not aim to solve it globally and instead introduce a notion of stationarity. We follow the convention of [7] and measure stationarity in terms of squared gradient norms. This choice is equivalent to measuring it in terms of norms and rescaling ( εf , ε g ), and is convenient as our analysis naturally controls the squared quantities. 

Definition 2.3 (( εf , ε g )-stationarity [7]) .

A point x ∈ H is ( εf , ε g )-stationary for Problem (P) if there exists λ ≥ 0 satisfying 

∥∇ f (x) + λ∇g(x)∥2 ≤ εf and ∥∇ g(x)∥2 ≤ εg .

The second condition requires x to be near-stationary for the lower-level objective. We emphasize that, since g is nonconvex, lower-level stationarity does in general not mean proximity to argmin g. Definition 2.3 should therefore be understood as a first-order approximation for solving Problem (P). The first condition describes first-order compatibility between the upper- and lower-level objectives. In fact, up to error √εf , the component of ∇f (x)orthogonal to ∇g(x) vanishes, while its parallel component either vanishes or points in the opposite direction to 

∇g(x). As such, there is no first-order direction that can substantially decrease f without increasing g.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced SDBGD, a denominator-regularized stochastic variant of DBGD [7] for stochastic simple nonconvex bilevel programming. Under standard assumptions, SDBGD achieves O(ε−2) iteration complexity, using O(ε−4)upper-level and O(ε−7) lower-level stochastic oracle calls. These rates do not require an unverifiable trajectory-dependent “rare-visit” assumption as in [1], and improve upon the assumption-free guarantees of [1]. We additionally provided an anytime parameter schedule and a simple numerical illustration. Overall, our results show that minor differences in the multiplier definition can substantially affect the theoretical guarantees. 

Open Question. The stochastic variants of DBGD proposed here and in [1] differ only slightly in their multiplier definition, yet yield considerably different guarantees. This suggests directly substituting the gradient for a stochas-tic estimator thereof may not be the natural stochastic analogue of DBGD. Since deterministic DBGD admits a projected dynamical system interpretation (1), an interesting direction might be to consider a stochastic projected dynamical system to derive the stochastic multiplier. We leave the following for future work: 

What is the appropriate stochastic analogue of deterministic multiplier-based bilevel methods?
