## Abstract 

Structured prediction tasks pose two challenges: outputs composed of sub-sentence units that require fine-grained local context understanding, and task-specific conventions that pretraining alone cannot supply. Both are es-pecially difficult under few-shot in-context learning (ICL), where the model must infer them from a small demonstration set selectively annotated under a fixed budget. We propose V ERB -ICL, which addresses them along two axes: not only which examples to annotate, but also what those annotations should contain. V ERB -ICL selects examples with a token-level coverage strategy that captures the local patterns these tasks predict over, then gen-erates error feedback that codifies these conventions and attaches it to the corresponding demonstrations. Across six datasets spanning information extraction and semantic parsing, and with multiple LLMs, V ERB -ICL con-sistently outperforms strong selective annotation baselines and continues to gain as the annotation budget increases. Analyses show that our feed-back design beats globally aggregated and label-free alternatives, and that both components reduce structural violations. The feedback also helps regardless of the selection strategy. 1

## 1 Introduction 

Structured prediction tasks often involve complex, compositional outputs and require costly human annotation (Thompson et al., 1999; Hwa, 2004; Haffari et al., 2009). The advent of large language models (LLMs; Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; Dubey et al., 2024; OpenAI et al., 2024) has introduced in-context learning (ICL; Dong et al., 2024), a promising paradigm for these tasks. In ICL, all tasks, including structured prediction (Gupta et al., 2023), are reformulated as generation problems, and the model performs inference by conditioning on a small set of labeled demonstrations without parameter updates. This setup is especially appealing when annotation is costly and task schemas evolve, as it can accommodate new schemas by updating demonstrations rather than retraining. However, the effectiveness of ICL depends critically on the choice of demonstration examples, making selective annotation under a fixed budget a central challenge. Recent studies have explored selective annotation strategies tailored to in-context learning (Mavromatis et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a; Mavromatis et al., 2024). For example, Su et al. (2023) and Zhang et al. (2024a) construct directed graphs based on sentence embedding similarity and select examples that balance representativeness and informativeness. While such methods show promise for classification tasks, they are less suitable for structured prediction. First, sentence embeddings primarily capture global semantics and often miss the sub-sentence compositional patterns that drive prediction in tasks such as information extraction and semantic parsing, where different parts of the input may benefit from different demonstrations. Second, these approaches largely treat LLMs as statistical predictors, overlooking a key strength of LLMs: their ability to understand and generate natural language instructions. This is a missed opportunity for structured prediction, where task-specific conventions, such as entity boundary rules or logical form syntax, are human-defined artifacts that cannot be fully acquired through pretraining. Natural language feedback, akin to guidelines that human annotators provide on difficult cases, can help codify these conventions and complement standard input-output demonstrations. To address these challenges, we propose VERB -ICL (Verb alized In-Context Learning), which rethinks how a fixed annotation budget is spent: not only which examples to annotate, but also what those annotations should contain. V ERB -ICL integrates fine-grained token-level coverage and reasoning-guided feedback into a unified pipeline. First, we select representative examples using a coverage-based strategy (Gupta et al., 2023), ensuring broad coverage of local semantic patterns critical for structured prediction. Next, on this subset, we perform ICL inference to obtain initial predictions and explicitly prompt LLMs to generate natural language feedback on erroneous examples that explains the mistake and suggests actionable strategies for handling similar cases. These feedback notes are incorporated into the demonstration set, enriching ICL prompts with task-specific guidance. We evaluate V ERB -ICL on six datasets spanning two structured prediction task types: information extraction and semantic parsing. Across annotation budgets from 16 to 512 examples, V ERB -ICL consistently outperforms seven selective annotation baselines under multiple LLMs. Feedback generated by models whose knowledge cutoff predates a dataset’s release still improves performance on it, alleviating concerns about data contamination. Ablating the feedback design along four dimensions shows that these specific choices, rather than the idea of learning from errors alone, drive the gains. On the two semantic parsing datasets whose schemas define structural and value errors, structural errors dominate and both components reduce them. Finally, a manual audit finds 87–100% of generated feedback useful, and feedback improves performance regardless of the selection strategy.

A widely used approach is KATE (Liu et al., 2022), which selects in-context examples via nearest-neighbor search over sentence-level embeddings. ## 3 VERB -ICL 

We present V ERB -ICL ( Verb alized In-Context Learning), a method designed to maximize ICL performance on structured prediction tasks within a fixed annotation budget. As illustrated in Figure 1, V ERB -ICL consists of two key steps, corresponding to which examples to annotate and what those annotations contain: 1) coverage-driven selection to identify representative instances (§3.1), and 2) error feedback generation, which produces actionable guidance based on model errors (§3.2). We begin by outlining the problem setup, followed by detailed explanations of each step. 

Problem Setup We adopt the few-shot selective annotation setting used in prior work (Su et al., 2023; Mavromatis et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a). Given an unlabeled pool 

U = {xi}Ni=1, the goal is to select a subset L ⊂ U for annotation, subject to an annotation budget |L| = M. The source datasets are fully labeled; we simulate annotation by hiding labels during selection and revealing the gold label of each selected example. These gold-labeled examples serve both as ICL demonstrations and as the reference for generating error feedback (§3.2). After annotation, for each test instance, a subset of labeled examples D ⊂ L 

is retrieved as demonstrations using SET-BSR, as described in §2. 

3.1 Coverage-driven Selection 

VERB -ICL begins with a coverage-driven selection strategy inspired by SET-BSR (§2; Step 1 in Figure 1). This design is motivated by the shared goal of both annotation selection and demonstration selection: identifying examples that collectively represent the target data distribution. The key distinction lies in their granularity, as demonstration selection targets a single test instance, while annotation selection operates over the entire unlabeled pool. Building on this insight, we adapt SET-BSR for annotation selection by treating the un-labeled pool as a single aggregated “test instance” and selecting examples that maximize token-level coverage. For each unlabeled example xi, we compute a coverage matrix 

Ci ∈ Ru×di over the unlabeled pool U , where u = |U | is the number of unlabeled examples and di = |xi| is the number of tokens in xi. These matrices are concatenated column-wise to form a global coverage matrix:

We apply the greedy algorithm in Algorithm 1 to Cglobal to maximize coverage over the unlabeled pool. Since Cglobal can be large due to its token-level granularity, we adopt a simple and effective heuristic to reduce memory usage: for each Ci, we retain only the top 

K token columns (e.g., K = 10), ranked by their aggregated coverage scores. As shown in Table 10 in the appendix, this strategy reduces memory consumption with minimal impact on performance. 

3.2 Error Feedback Generation 

Unlike prior ICL-based selective annotation methods that stop at selecting labeled examples, VERB -ICL takes a further step by prompting an LLM to generate natural language feedback on prediction errors, inspired by real-world annotation workflows where generalizable instructions on difficult cases complement ground-truth labels. This is especially valuable for structured prediction, where errors often stem from misunderstanding task-specific conventions, such as entity boundary rules or logical form syntax, that are human-defined artifacts not fully learnable from pretraining or input-output demonstrations alone. As illustrated in Step 2 of Figure 1, we apply a leave-one-out strategy: each selected instance is treated as the query, and its demonstrations are retrieved from the remaining selected instances using SET-BSR. For those incorrectly predicted instances, we prompt the LLM to analyze the error and generate actionable feedback describing how similar cases should be 4Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 handled. The models used for ICL inference and error feedback generation can differ; we analyze the effect of this choice in Section 5. To ensure consistency and ease of integration, feedback is generated in a structured JSON format. During subsequent ICL inference, when feedback is available for a retrieved demon-stration, the standard “Input–Output” format is expanded to an “Input–Notes–Output” format, where the “Notes” field contains the generated feedback. The full prompts and corresponding LLM responses are provided in Appendix C. For demonstrations without feedback, the original format is retained. This error reflection process is conceptually related to recent work on prompt optimization (Pryzant et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). However, two key differences distinguish our approach. First, prompt optimization methods seek a single globally optimized prompt, whereas V ERB -ICL couples feedback with individual demonstrations, so different feedback is surfaced depending on which demonstrations are retrieved for a given query. Second, prompt optimization typically relies on a large held-out validation set to select the best prompt candidate; V ERB -ICL operates without such a set. In our implementation, we incorporate all generated feedback without filtering. As we show in Section 5, the strict per-example validation criterion is overly conservative: feedback that does not directly correct individual errors still captures generalizable task-level guidance and consistently improves overall performance. 

## 4 Experiment Setup 

4.1 Datasets and Evaluation Metric 

We conduct experiments on six structured prediction datasets spanning two task types. The first includes three information extraction datasets: NCBI (Do ˘ gan et al., 2014) and TweetNER7 (Ushio et al., 2022) for named entity recognition, and PHEE (Sun et al., 2024) for event argument extraction. The second includes three semantic parsing datasets: GeoQuery (Zelle & Mooney, 1996), ATIS (Hemphill et al., 1990), and MTOP (Li et al., 2021). Dataset statistics (Table 6) and detailed descriptions are provided in Appendix A. 3

For evaluation, we use the standard mention-level matching metric (Tjong Kim Sang & De Meulder, 2003) for the three information extraction datasets, where a prediction is considered correct only if both the entity or argument span boundary and its type exactly match the gold annotation. For the semantic parsing datasets, we follow Gupta et al. (2023) and report exact match (EM) accuracy. 

4.2 Baselines 

We compare V ERB -ICL against seven baselines. Random and K-Means (Chang et al., 2021) serve as classic selection baselines. We further compare against three recent ICL-driven selective annotation methods: vote-k (Su et al., 2023), IDEAL (Zhang et al., 2024a), and Cov-erICL (Mavromatis et al., 2024). These methods construct similarity graphs over sentence embeddings and select examples that balance representativeness with informativeness, estimated via LLM confidence. We also include two active learning methods designed for generation tasks: IDDS (Tsvigun et al., 2022), which balances diversity and dissimilarity from already selected instances, and LDCAL (Li et al., 2024), which uses a certainty gain metric with outlier thresholding. Additional details are provided in Appendix B. 4

Feedback Method Analysis The results above show that error feedback helps, but not how it should be incorporated, and prior work occupies different points in this space. We characterize the design along four dimensions. (1) Error source : where the erroneous predictions come from, either zero-shot or leave-one-out ICL inference. (2) Grounding :whether feedback is anchored in gold labels or in label-free self-critique. (3) Feedback selection : which notes a query sees, all collected notes or only those attached to its retrieved demonstrations. (4) Positioning : where the notes appear, in a global block at the top of the prompt or inline with each demonstration. Table 2 compares six configurations, all using V ERB -ICL’s coverage-driven selection so that only the feedback design varies, and each adjacent pair isolating a single dimension. Row b1 instantiates the design of LEAP (Zhang et al., 2024b), and row e the label-free grounding of Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023). Appendix F defines the dimensions in full and situates further methods within them. VERB -ICL (row d) performs best on both datasets, but the four dimensions contribute unevenly. Grounding matters most and most consistently: replacing gold-anchored feedback with label-free self-critique costs 1.7 points on NCBI and 5.0 on ATIS. Inline positioning gains 3.6 points on NCBI but is within noise on ATIS, and collecting errors from leave-one-out rather than zero-shot predictions helps NCBI (+1.9) while falling within noise on ATIS. Feedback selection is task-dependent: showing all notes is better on NCBI, while showing only the retrieved ones is better on ATIS. We adopt retrieval-selected feedback, which is also cheaper, as each query carries only the notes of its K demonstrations rather than all M.

What Errors Does V ERB -ICL Fix? To understand what V ERB -ICL improves, we examine its errors from the perspective of structured prediction, where they take two forms. A 

structural violation breaks a task constraint, such as an invalid predicate, an arity mismatch, or a slot not licensed by the predicted intent; a value violation leaves the structure intact and gets only a constant or span wrong (Pauk & Pacheco, 2026; Rajaby Faghihi & Kordjamshidi, 2024). The distinction is defined by the output schema, so we run this analysis on ATIS and MTOP; entity spans in information extraction admit no comparable notion of structure. We classify each incorrect prediction with a rule-based parser that abstracts away constants and compares the remaining structure to gold; predictions that fail to parse, or are wrong in both respects, count as structural, so the two categories partition the errors. Table 4 confirms that structural violations dominate, at 81–86% of errors under Random. Both components act on this same axis: coverage-driven selection reduces structural vio-lations more than K-Means (MTOP −17.7% vs. −8.8%), and error feedback deepens the 8Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 reduction (ATIS −13.6% to −18.5%; MTOP −17.7% to −22.3%), consistent with feedback codifying conventions that are themselves structural. Selection and feedback therefore compound on the error type that dominates these tasks, rather than helping for unrelated reasons. Value counts on MTOP rise (104 to 151) as total errors fall (737 to 643), which is reclassification rather than new errors: a value violation is counted only when the structure is correct, so repairing structure moves predictions out of the structural category.

## 6 Related Work 

In-Context Learning for Structured Prediction In-context learning (ICL; Brown et al. 2020) is a representative few-shot learning paradigm that has attracted significant attention due

Recent studies (Pecher et al., 2024; Baek et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2025) have investigated various approaches to improving ICL performance, from enhanced pre-training strategies (Chen et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024) to demonstration selection (Rubin et al., 2022; Levy et al., 2023). Structured prediction is a demanding setting for this paradigm, since demonstrations must convey both fine-grained output structure and task-specific conventions. Prompted LLMs have been studied across named entity recognition (Monajatipoor et al., 2024), relation and event extraction (Wadhwa et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2024), semantic parsing (Shin et al., 2021), and even table extraction (Bai et al., 2024). On the demonstration side, examples are selected by token-level coverage (Gupta et al., 2023) or label-grounded token statistics (Bai et al., 2025). All of this work takes a labeled pool as given; we revisit the setting under a fixed annotation budget, asking not only which examples to annotate but also what those annotations should contain. 

Selective Annotation for ICL The data-scarce nature of few-shot ICL makes annotation selection critically important. This problem is closely related to active learning (Cohn et al., 1994; Lewis, 1995; Settles, 2009; Iyer et al., 2017; Peris & Casacuberta, 2018; Li et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024c; Azeemi et al., 2025), which has a long history in structured prediction (Thompson et al., 1999; Hwa, 2004; Haffari et al., 2009). A key distinction is that selective annotation for ICL does not involve explicit model training, but instead relies on static, pretrained LLMs. Many recent works (Mavromatis et al., 2024; Baumann et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025; Berger et al., 2025) have explored this setting. For example, Su et al. (2023) extend demonstration selection to the upstream task of selective annotation, constructing a directed graph over sentence embeddings to balance representativeness and informativeness, and Zhang et al. (2024a) refine this graph-based formulation with an influence-driven criterion. In contrast, we draw inspiration from recent advances in demonstration selection (Gupta et al., 2023) and show that a token-level coverage-based strategy can be effectively extended to annotation selection, which is complementary to our use of LLM-generated error feedback. 

Error Feedback A broad line of work uses LLM-generated natural language feedback on errors to improve downstream performance. Prompt optimization methods (Zhou et al., 2023; Pryzant et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Yuksekgonul et al., 2024) iteratively refine a single global prompt by evaluating candidates on a held-out validation set. Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) instead critique and revise outputs at test time, while LEAP (Zhang et al., 2024b) mines errors from zero-shot predictions and distills them into principles prepended to every prompt. V ERB -ICL differs on three axes. First, feedback is generated once at annotation time from the labeled examples, so no additional inference is required per test query. Second, each note stays attached to the demonstration that produced it, so different feedback surfaces depending on what is retrieved, rather than being aggregated into a single prompt. Third, V ERB -ICL requires no held-out validation set; as §5 shows, the per-example validation criterion used in prior work is too strict to improve on using all feedback. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We presented V ERB -ICL, which makes better use of a fixed annotation budget for ICL-based structured prediction along two axes: which examples to annotate, and what those anno-tations contain. Coverage-driven selection captures the fine-grained patterns these tasks predict over, while error feedback codifies task-specific conventions that cannot be acquired through pretraining or input-output demonstrations alone. Across six datasets, V ERB -ICL improves consistently over strong baselines, its specific feedback design matters, and both components reduce structural violations. These results suggest that when annotation is limited, what an annotation contains deserves as much attention as which examples are annotated. 10 Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026

GeoQuery (Zelle & Mooney, 1996) The GeoQuery dataset consists of 879 natural-language questions about U.S. geography, such as “Which states have cities named Austin?”. The task requires mapping each question to a corresponding logical form. 

ATIS (Hemphill et al., 1990) The ATIS dataset focuses on airline travel queries, where the goal is to parse natural-language utterances into logical forms expressed in lambda calculus. An example query is “What is the earliest flight from Washington to Atlanta leaving on Wednesday, September fourth?”, which can be represented as (argmin $0 (and (flight $0) (from $0 washington : ci) (to $0 atlanta : ci) (day $0 wednesday : da) (day_number $0 4 : dn) (month $0 september : mn)) (departure_time $0)) .

MTOP (Li et al., 2021) The MTOP dataset parses natural-language utterances into struc-tured semantic frames consisting of an intent , which captures the user’s overall goal, and multiple slots , which represent specific arguments needed to fulfill that intent. ## B Baselines 

K-Means Clustering K-Means sampling selects representative instances by partitioning the embedding space into k clusters. Each instance is first embedded into a d-dimensional space, after which K-Means is applied to identify cluster centroids. For each cluster, the instance with the highest cosine similarity to its centroid is selected, yielding a diverse set of representatives that approximates the global data distribution. 

IDDS (Tsvigun et al., 2022) IDDS incrementally constructs a labeled set by balancing diversity within the remaining unlabeled pool and dissimilarity from already selected instances. At each iteration, candidates are scored using a weighted difference between 20 Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 their average similarity to unlabeled examples and their average similarity to labeled ones. This greedy strategy promotes broad in-domain coverage while reducing redundancy. 

LDCAL (Li et al., 2024) LDCAL selects instances based on their marginal coverage gain beyond the current labeled set. It explicitly accounts for redundancy through the Coverage Excluding Redundancy (CER) score and evaluates candidates using the Average CER Gain (ACERG). A validity constraint ensures that selected instances contribute sufficient new coverage, yielding a principled balance between informativeness and redundancy control. 

vote-k (Su et al., 2023) vote-k is a two-stage selection method that combines graph-based voting with LLM-based uncertainty estimation. In the first stage, a directed k-NN graph is constructed in the embedding space, where each node votes for its nearest neighbors. A greedy voting process selects a diverse seed set by favoring nodes supported by many distinct neighbors while penalizing redundant contributions. In the second stage, the seed set is used as in-context examples to estimate uncertainty over remaining candidates, and the remaining budget is allocated via stratified sampling across uncertainty bins to balance coverage and informativeness. 

IDEAL (Zhang et al., 2024a) IDEAL formulates data selection as an influence maximization problem over a similarity graph. Examples are connected to their nearest neighbors with edge weights proportional to normalized similarity. Starting from a high-degree seed node, the algorithm greedily selects instances that maximize expected influence under the Independent Cascade diffusion model, estimated via Monte Carlo simulation. This approach explicitly models information propagation to identify globally influential examples. 

CoverICL (Mavromatis et al., 2024) CoverICL targets difficult instances for in-context learning by explicitly covering hard examples in an embedding graph. After estimating uncertainty, the least confident half of the unlabeled pool is identified as the hard set. Each candidate defines a coverage region consisting of hard instances reachable within two hops in a k-NN graph. The final selection is obtained via greedy set cover, prioritizing examples that jointly maximize coverage of hard instances.

## E Token-level Selection Baselines 

VERB -ICL selects examples using contextual token-level coverage. Two simpler strategies test whether this is necessary. BoW-coverage applies the same greedy coverage objective of Algorithm 1, but represents each example as a bag-of-words vector rather than a sequence of contextual embeddings, so coverage is measured over vocabulary overlap alone. Longest-

n ignores coverage entirely and selects the M examples with the most words. Both use SET-BSR for demonstration retrieval at test time, as in all other experiments, so only the annotation selection differs. Table 15 reports accuracy alongside the average length of the selected examples. Neither simpler strategy matches V ERB -ICL, and the margin widens from NCBI to ATIS, where compositional logical forms require distinguishing between structurally similar inputs that share vocabulary. Longest-n falls below Random on both datasets despite selecting the longest examples available, which indicates that the recall-oriented coverage objective does not succeed by favoring length: V ERB -ICL selects examples roughly half as long as Longest-n and outperforms it by 4.1 and 11.1 points.

Grounding. What the feedback is anchored to. Gold-grounded feedback compares a prediction against its reference label, so the model is shown what the correct output was and can diagnose the gap. Label-free feedback asks the model to critique its own output without a reference. Grounding is available only when errors are collected on labeled data, so test-time methods are necessarily label-free. 

Feedback selection. Which notes a given query sees. Global approaches show every collected note to every query. Retrieval-selected approaches show only the notes attached to the demonstrations retrieved for that query, so the notes vary across queries and the added prompt length is bounded by the retrieval depth K rather than by the size M of the annotated set. 

Positioning. Where the notes appear in the prompt. A global block places all notes together, typically as a prefix preceding the demonstrations. Inline positioning places each note with the demonstration it was derived from, preserving the association between an error and the example that produced it.
