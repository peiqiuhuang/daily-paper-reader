Abstract 

Stance detection remains challenging for large language models (LLMs) due to implicit opinions, sarcasm, and context-dependent expressions of stance. Al-though automatic prompt optimization of-fers an alternative to fine-tuning with-out updating model parameters, exist-ing structured approaches are not specif-ically designed around stance-label seman-tics and class-specific ambiguities. We introduce MoPrO-SD , a stance-specific prompt optimization framework that de-composes prompts into functional seman-tic modules and refines them sequentially using LLM-based feedback. We study three refinement strategies—instruction-only optimization ( I), instruction with rea-soning ( I+R ), and instruction with per-example reasoning ( I+R+E )—using class-conditioned errors for stance-label refine-ment. We also introduce VaccineStance ,a new human-annotated benchmark for vac-cine stance detection. Experiments across four datasets and five open-weight LLMs show that MoPrO-SD improves upon the human-written Base prompt in many set-tings. On SemEval-2016, class-conditioned feedback achieves higher mean Macro-F1 than pooled feedback for four of five back-bones. Both reasoning-based strategies, I+R and I+R+E, achieve strong perfor-mance, with their relative effectiveness vary-ing across models and datasets, while re-maining competitive with recent prompt op-timization methods. The MoPrO-SD code and VaccineStance dataset are available on GitHub. 1

1 Introduction 

Stance detection identifies the attitude to-ward a target or claim, typically categorized

as In-Favor , Against , or Neutral (Mohammad et al., 2016). It supports applications includ-ing misinformation detection, political opinion analysis, public health monitoring, and social media analytics (Küçük and Can, 2020; AL-Dayel and Magdy, 2021). Despite advances in large language models (LLMs), stance de-tection remains challenging due to sarcasm, implicit opinions, and context-dependent ex-pressions (Yakura, 2024; Gole et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Prompting offers an alternative to fine-tuning without updating model parameters, but LLMs are sensitive to prompt wording, demonstrations, and example ordering (Zhao et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2024). Se-mantically similar prompts can yield different performance (Kojima et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023), and effective prompts are often task- and model-dependent (Chen et al., 2023a), moti-vating automated prompt optimization (Chang et al., 2024). Recent methods include OPRO (Yang et al., 2024), EvoPrompt (Guo et al., 2024), and CAPO (Zehle et al., 2025), while UniPrompt (Juneja et al., 2025) and SCULPT (Kumar et al., 2025) optimize struc-tured prompt components. Reasoning-based prompting has also shown promise for stance detection (Ma et al., 2024; Gül et al., 2024; Aiyappa et al., 2024). However, existing opti-mization frameworks are largely task-general rather than organized around stance-specific class structure, where guidelines, label defi-nitions, and output constraints serve distinct roles. We introduce MoPrO-SD (Mo dular 

Pr ompt Optimization for Stance Detection), which decomposes a stance prompt into six functional modules and refines them sequentially. We study instruction-only ( I), arXiv:2610.13386v1 [cs.CL] 9 Oct 2026 instruction with reasoning ( I+R ), and instruc-tion with per-example reasoning ( I+R+E )optimization. Reasoning-based strategies use misclassification feedback for targeted refinement, with class-conditioned errors informing stance-label modules. We evaluate MoPrO-SD on SemEval-2016 (Mohammad et al., 2016), COVID-19-Stance (Glandt et al., 2021), Gun-Stance (Gyawali et al., 2024), and VaccineS-tance using five open-weight LLMs, com-paring against OPRO (Yang et al., 2024), CAPO (Zehle et al., 2025), EvoPrompt-GA/DE (Guo et al., 2024), UniPrompt (Juneja et al., 2025), and SCULPT (Kumar et al., 2025). MoPrO-SD improves over the human-written base prompt across many model– dataset settings. We also introduce VaccineS-tance , a new human-annotated benchmark constructed from vaccine-related social media posts collected between 2013 and 2022. Our contributions are: • We introduce MoPrO-SD , combining stance-specific modular refinement with class-conditioned error feedback. • We evaluate it across four datasets and five LLMs against established baselines, with analyses of modularity, optimization bud-get, cost, module contributions, few-shot prompting, and external-reasoner transfer. • We introduce VaccineStance , a new human-annotated benchmark. 

2 Related Work 

Prompting for Stance Detection. LLM-based stance detection uses prompting and reasoning strategies (Pangtey et al., 2025). Chain-of-Stance (Ma et al., 2024) and Stance Reasoner (Taranukhin et al., 2024) use ex-plicit reasoning, while other work explores rationale-augmented prompting (Lee et al., 2024) and zero-shot instruction and decoding strategies (Aiyappa et al., 2024). Supervised adaptation (Gül et al., 2024) can achieve strong performance but requires additional training. In contrast, we optimize the stance prompt without updating the target LLM. 

Automated and Structured Prompt Op-timization. Automatic prompt optimization spans instruction generation, feedback, search, reinforcement learning, and evolutionary ap-proaches (Ramnath et al., 2025). Represen-tative methods include APE (Zhou et al., 2023), ProTeGi (Pryzant et al., 2023), RL-Prompt (Deng et al., 2022), MAPO (Chen et al., 2023b), PRewrite (Kong et al., 2024), Auto Evol-Instruct (Zeng et al., 2024), Promp-tAgent (Wang et al., 2024b), and Align-Pro (Trivedi et al., 2025). OPRO (Yang et al., 2024) uses evaluated solutions to guide opti-mization, EvoPrompt (Guo et al., 2024) com-bines LLMs with evolutionary algorithms, and CAPO (Zehle et al., 2025) incorporates opti-mization cost. Structured approaches include Decomposed Prompting (Khot et al., 2023), modular LLM programs in DSPy (Khattab et al., 2024), structure-aware optimization in Sym-bolic Prompt Program Search (Schnabel and Neville, 2024), content–format optimization in CFPO (Liu et al., 2025b), and section-wise modular prompt optimization (Sharma and Henley, 2026). DS2 (Pang et al., 2025) and DeLLMa (Liu et al., 2025a) explore data-or decision-aware selection, while Prompt Se-lection and Augmentation (Wu et al., 2024), BRIDGE (Wan et al., 2025), APT-Pipe (Zhu et al., 2024), and Mixture of Demonstrations (Wang et al., 2024a) study demonstration se-lection and organization. Related work includes UniPrompt (Juneja et al., 2025), SCULPT (Kumar et al., 2025), and modular prompt optimization (Sharma and Henley, 2026). While these methods opti-mize structured prompt components, MoPrO-SD specializes this paradigm to stance detec-tion through fixed functional modules, sequen-tial refinement, and class-conditioned misclas-sification feedback for stance-label modules.

3.1 Stance Detection Prompt Modules 

The base prompt consists of six modules: task definition , guidelines , favor definition , against definition , neutral definition , and output format .This design follows prior work on decomposing long prompts for more stable and controllable optimization (Kumar et al., 2025; Khot et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2025). The task module spec-ifies the stance detection objective; guidelines provide general decision criteria, including sar-casm, implicit stance, and context-dependent expressions; the three label-specific modules define favor , against , and neutral and may in-clude representative examples to reduce label ambiguity (Mohammad et al., 2016); and the output module enforces label-only predictions. The complete modular base prompt is provided in Appendix A.1. 

3.2 Proposed MoPrO-SD Framework 

Starting from a manually designed six-module base prompt, MoPrO-SD first evaluates the full prompt on a validation set using Macro-F1 and then refines the modules sequentially in a fixed order: task definition , guidelines , favor definition , against definition , neutral definition ,and output format . During each update, only the target module is rewritten using feedback determined by the optimization strategy below. After all six modules are processed, the full prompt is re-evaluated on the validation set. One pass over the six modules constitutes an iteration, and the prompt with the highest val-idation Macro-F1 across iterations is selected for final test evaluation. This procedure re-sembles coordinate descent in prompt space, preserving prompt substructures while refining individual modules. In the main experiments, the optimizer/reasoner is the same backbone as the stance classifier; we separately evaluate a stronger external optimizer. 

3.3 Prompt Optimization Strategies Instruction-only Optimization (I). All six modules are refined using instruction-only updates. For each module, the optimizer uses its previous variants and the validation Macro-F1 recorded for their corresponding iterations to propose a new version, without explicit analysis of misclassified examples (Zhou et al., 2023). 

Instruction with Reasoning Optimiza-tion (I+R). The guidelines and three label-3specific modules are refined using aggregated reasoning over misclassified training examples, while the task definition and output format use instruction-only refinement. For each label-specific module, only errors whose gold label corresponds to that module are used; for ex-ample, the favor definition uses errors with gold label favor . For the guidelines module, errors from all three stance classes are con-sidered. The aggregated reasoning identifies problems in the current module and guides its refinement (Shin and Kim, 2025). 

Instruction with Per-example Reason-ing Optimization (I+R+E). For the three label-specific modules, misclassified training examples are analyzed individually. Following Tang et al. (2025), the optimizer generates an explanation for each error, summarizes these explanations, and uses the feedback to revise the corresponding module. The task definition ,

guidelines , and output format continue to use instruction-only updates within the same opti-mization loop. If no relevant errors are avail-able for a label-specific module, that module remains unchanged for that iteration. Example meta-prompts are provided in Appendix A.2. I+R and I+R+E are treated as alternative configurations, as they differ in both reasoning granularity and the treatment of the guidelines 

module. 

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Stance Detection Datasets 

We evaluate MoPrO-SD on three existing stance detection benchmarks— SemEval-2016 Task 6 , GunStance , and COVID-19-Stance —and our new human-annotated VaccineStance 

benchmark. 1. SemEval-2016 Task 6 (Mohammad et al., 2016) contains tweets labeled as 

Favor , Against , or None across multiple controversial targets. We exclude Donald Trump because it has no training instances and map None to Neutral for consistency across datasets. 2. GunStance (Gyawali et al., 2024) con-tains stance-labeled tweets on gun-control debates, including the targets Banning Guns and Regulating Guns .3. COVID-19-Stance (Glandt et al., 2021) contains stance-labeled tweets on COVID-19 measures, including face masks, school closures, and stay-at-home orders. 4. VaccineStance is a new human-annotated stance detection benchmark introduced in this work. We curated 2,000 vaccine-relevant tweets from the corpus of Gyawali et al. (Gyawali et al., 2025) and obtained human annotations for Favor , Against , and Neutral , with Fleiss’ κ = 0 .778. The dataset comprises 500 training, 500 validation, and 1,000 test instances. Detailed dataset statistics, VaccineStance construction and preprocessing, and split dis-tributions are provided in Appendix B. 

4.2 Large Language Models 

We evaluate five LLMs spanning different model families and scales: Phi-4-14B (Ab-din et al., 2024), GPT-OSS-20B (OpenAI, 2025b), Mistral-Small-3.2-24B (Mistral AI, 2025), Gemma-3-27B (Gemma Team et al., 2025), and Llama-3.3-70B (Meta, 2024). Ta-bles abbreviate them as Phi, GPT-OSS, Mis-tral, Gemma, and Llama. 

4.3 Baselines 

We compare MoPrO-SD with the origi-nal zero-shot Base prompt and several prompting baselines built on the same Base prompt: chain-of-thought (Base+CoT) (Wei et al., 2022), few-shot prompting ( k = 3; Base+FS) (Brown et al., 2020), their combi-nation (Base+CoT+FS), and Chain-of-Stance (Base+CoS) (Ma et al., 2024). We also compare against automated prompt optimiza-tion methods OPRO (Yang et al., 2024), CAPO (Zehle et al., 2025), and EvoPrompt with differential evolution (DE) and genetic algorithm (GA) variants (Guo et al., 2024). Additionally, on SemEval-2016, we compare with the structured prompt optimization meth-ods UniPrompt (Juneja et al., 2025) and SCULPT (Kumar et al., 2025) using Mistral, Gemma, and Llama. 

4.4 Implementation Details 

All experiments optimize prompts without fine-tuning model weights. MoPrO-SD uses 10 4optimization iterations, with the six modules processed sequentially in a fixed order. After each iteration, the full prompt is evaluated on the validation set, and the prompt with the highest validation Macro-F1 across iterations is selected for final test evaluation. All meth-ods in Table 1 were run in our experimental pipeline using fixed dataset splits and three independent runs per method. Across runs, validation subsampling, training-example sam-pling, and stochastic LLM generation can vary. Optimization budgets vary across methods. Unless otherwise specified, the same back-bone serves as the stance classifier and op-timizer/reasoner. We additionally evaluate GPT-5 as a stronger external reasoner (Ope-nAI, 2025a). In frozen-module ablations, one module remains fixed while the others are op-timized. 

4.5 Evaluation Metrics 

We use three-class Macro-F1, computed as the unweighted mean of the F1 scores for Favor ,

Against , and Neutral , as the primary evaluation metric. This differs from the official SemEval-2016 metric, which averages F1 over Favor and 

Against only. Prompt selection is based on validation Macro-F1, and final test results are reported as mean ± standard deviation across three independent runs. 

5 Results and Discussion 

We evaluate MoPrO-SD from both perfor-mance and diagnostic perspectives. We first examine optimization dynamics and aggregate Macro-F1 performance across datasets and models, followed by comparisons with struc-tured prompt optimization methods, external-reasoner effects, computational cost, modular versus full-prompt optimization, and module-level ablations. 

5.1 Optimization Dynamics 

Figure 2 shows validation Macro-F1 trajec-tories across 10 optimization iterations using I+R+E. Most models improve over the Base prompt during the early iterations, while later iterations show smaller gains and greater fluc-tuations. Some trajectories exhibit sharp drops followed by recovery, particularly on VaccineS-tance, indicating variation across optimization iterations. The shaded regions also show run-to-run variability in validation performance. Overall, the trajectories show that useful refine-ments are often identified early and that contin-ued optimization does not guarantee monotonic improvement. 5.2 Main Performance Results 

Table 1 summarizes Macro-F1 across datasets, backbone models, and prompting strategies, with per-target results provided in Appendix E. I+R+E improves over the human-written Base prompt in 18 of 20 dataset–model combi-nations. The improvements are neverthe-less model- and dataset-dependent: I+R out-performs I+R+E in several settings, indicat-ing that the preferred reasoning-based refine-ment configuration depends on the model and dataset. The largest I+R+E gains over Base are ob-served for Phi on GunStance (39 .55 → 59 .74, +20 .19 Macro-F1), Phi on COVID-19-Stance (50 .36 → 69 .97, +19 .61), and Gemma on Vac-cineStance (37 .78 → 55 .12, +17 .34). Across all methods, CAPO with Llama obtains the highest SemEval score (74.87), while I+R+E achieves 74.78. I+R+E with Mistral also performs strongly on GunStance (65.01) and achieves the highest score on COVID-19-Stance (78.03), while I+R with Mistral obtains the highest VaccineStance score (68.96). Static prompting also yields substantial im-provements in some settings. For example, Base+FS increases Mistral from 53.90 to 64.69 on SemEval and from 55.58 to 65.24 on Vac-cineStance. MoPrO-SD frequently improves further, suggesting that iterative module refine-ment can provide gains beyond static prompt augmentation. However, the variation across models, datasets, and refinement strategies in-dicates that no single optimization strategy dominates in every setting. Additional zero-shot and few-shot comparisons are provided in Appendix F.

External Reasoner Comparison. We eval-uate GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025a) as an external reasoner for the three label-specific I+R+E modules, while retaining the target backbone for classification and other module updates. This tests whether stronger reasoning during optimization improves prompts without replac-ing the target classifier. Table 3 shows gains in 17 of 20 dataset–model combinations, with substantial variation across settings. The largest gains occur for Gemma on Vac-cineStance (+11.64), Llama on GunStance (+9.42), and Llama on COVID-19-Stance (+9.23). However, Mistral on SemEval declines by 5.82 points, showing that stronger reasoning does not always improve optimization. Over-all, reasoning quality can substantially affect optimization outcomes, but its benefits remain model- and dataset-dependent.

6 Conclusion 

We introduced MoPrO-SD, a modular prompt optimization framework for stance detection that refines prompt components without model fine-tuning. Across four datasets and multiple LLM backbones, MoPrO-SD improves over the Base prompt in many settings, although perfor-mance varies by model and refinement strategy. Class-conditioned error feedback achieves higher mean Macro-F1 than pooled feedback for four of five backbones on SemEval-2016, supporting the value of label-specific refine-ment. Modular optimization also outperforms full-prompt optimization for several models, with varying module contributions. Despite higher development-time costs, MoPrO-SD re-quires no additional optimization at inference. 8Limitations 

MoPrO-SD has several limitations. First, al-though it improves performance across mul-tiple stance detection datasets, its generaliz-ability to other NLP tasks remains unexplored. Second, optimized prompts are model- and domain-dependent, so transferring them across backbone models or datasets may require re-optimization. Third, the current framework uses a fixed modular structure tailored to stance detection, with explicit favor , against ,and neutral definition modules; extending this design to tasks with larger label spaces or more complex reasoning requirements remains fu-ture work. Fourth, MoPrO-SD incurs addi-tional optimization-time cost due to repeated validation and optimizer calls, especially for reasoning-based strategies. Finally, experi-ments involving API-based LLMs may be af-fected by model updates over time, which can impact reproducibility.

A.1 Complete Prompt Template 

Complete Base Prompt for Stance Detection 

1. Task Definition 

Stance detection is a task of determining the attitude or position expressed by an author in a text regarding a particular topic or claim. Your task is to classify the stance of a tweet toward the target “ {target} ” as either Favor, Against, or Neutral. 

2. Guidelines 

1. Stance Indicators: Look for keywords, phrases, emoticons, and tone that indicate a favor, against, or neutral attitude toward {target} .2. Implicit vs. Explicit Stance: Consider both implicit and explicit expressions of stance. If a tweet implies a stance without directly stating it, use the context and language to determine the intended stance. 3. Sarcasm and Irony: Be aware of sarcasm and irony, which can be used to express an opposing stance. Look for inconsistencies between the literal meaning and the tone or language used. 

3. Favor Definition 

Classify the tweet as favor if it: 1. Shows an explicitly positive stance: openly supports {target} 

2. Uses pro-{target} keywords: languages that frames {target} in positive light 

4. Against Definition 

Classify the tweet as against if it: 1. Shows an explicitly negative stance: openly opposes {target} 

2. Uses against {target} keywords: languages that frames the target {target} in negative light 

5. Neutral Definition 

Classify the tweet as neutral if it: 1. Shows no clear stance: it neither supports nor opposes {target} 

2. Uses neutral or tentative language: e.g., simply asking a question or stating opinion that signals indifference/uncertainty 

6. Output Format 

Strictly follow the guidelines above and classify the tweet if it is Favor, Against, or Neutral to the target “{target} ”. Reply with the classification label only, without any additional text or explanation. 

Tweet: {tweet} Stance: [Favor/Against/Neutral]

B.2 VaccineStance Construction and Annotation 

We construct VaccineStance from the vaccine-discourse corpus of Gyawali et al. (Gyawali et al., 2025), comprising public English-language posts collected from X (formerly Twit-ter) using the Full Archive Search API between January 2013 and December 2022. Preprocess-ing retained original posts, limited users to one post per day, removed reposts, URLs, emoti-cons, non-ASCII characters, and user mentions, and filtered references to the band “The Vac-cines” using Sentence-Transformer similarity. The resulting corpus contained approximately 18.7 million posts from 5.8 million users. From this corpus, we sampled 240,000 tweets (2,000 per month) and used Llama-3.3 (Meta, 2024) to identify posts relevant to vaccine stance detection. We then selected 2,000 tweets for human stance annotation. Each tweet was independently labeled by three annotators as 

Favor , Against , or Neutral , achieving Fleiss’ 

κ = 0 .778. The resulting benchmark contains 724 Favor (36.2%), 464 Against (23.2%), and 812 Neutral (40.6%) instances, divided into 500 training, 500 validation, and 1,000 test instances. All tweet identifiers and texts are unique, with no exact overlap across splits.

C.1 Implementation Details 

All LLM calls are performed through re-mote APIs, with OpenRouter as the primary provider. Stance classification uses a tempera-ture of 0.2, whereas reasoning-generation steps use a temperature of 0.7. For few-shot evalua-tion, the final optimized I+R+E prompts are kept frozen and augmented at inference time with training-set demonstrations retrieved by embedding similarity. We evaluate k = 1 and 

k = 3 demonstrations per stance class.
