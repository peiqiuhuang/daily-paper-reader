RANGE-GRPO: POLICY OPTIMIZATION VIA PAIRWISE
RELATIONS AMONG REWARD INTERVALS
Ryunyi Lee, Kangjun Noh, Somin Kim, Heedong Kim, Kyungwoo Song∗
Yonsei University
ABSTRACT
As the use of large language models (LLMs) expands, post-training has be-
come increasingly important for adapting them to downstream tasks.
How-
ever, obtaining reliable supervision remains costly, especially in domains with-
out reference answers or executable verifiers. LLM-as-a-Judge provides scalable
pseudo-rewards for unlabeled responses, but a single point score does not ex-
plicitly represent reward uncertainty. This motivates representing pseudo-rewards
as conformally calibrated reward ranges. We propose Range-GRPO, a semi-
supervised post-training framework that combines limited labeled data with unla-
beled prompts. In Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), learning signals
depend on relative reward comparisons within each rollout group. The proposed
objective compares reward ranges pairwise rather than reducing them to point re-
wards, allowing interval uncertainty to affect both the magnitude and direction of
these signals. Our theoretical analysis characterizes this distinction and shows that
the proposed objective recovers the Dr.GRPO advantage when all reward ranges
collapse to points. Empirically, Range-GRPO achieves the highest in-distribution
and out-of-distribution average performance among the evaluated semi-supervised
methods while requiring fewer training resources.

INTRODUCTION
Adapting large language models (LLMs) to new tasks and domains requires reliable supervision,
yet obtaining it at scale remains difficult. Existing benchmarks provide limited coverage of task
distributions (Kiela et al., 2021) and may suffer from data leakage (Roberts et al., 2024; White et al.,
2025). These limitations motivate semi-supervised post-training methods that use limited labeled
data to learn from large collections of unlabeled prompts. LLM-as-a-Judge (LaaJ) offers a scalable
way to assign pseudo rewards to unlabeled responses (Lee et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2022). However,
a judge may assign high scores to flawed reasoning and low scores to valid but atypical responses.
It may also react to surface cues unrelated to correctness (Ye et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2023; Wu &
Aji, 2025), but a single scalar score does not express the uncertainty of that evaluation. Conformal
calibration offers a promising approach to representing this uncertainty through reward intervals
(Sheng et al., 2025). In this setting, it measures the discrepancies between predicted and reference
rewards on a small labeled calibration set and uses the empirical error distribution to construct
intervals for new responses. Under exchangeability, these intervals cover the reference reward with
marginal coverage of at least 1−α (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023). This guarantee provides statistical
support for the calibrated intervals, but does not specify how to construct effective policy learning
signals from them.
We therefore seek an objective that uses calibrated intervals directly to determine relative credit
among responses. Some existing approaches predict task outputs as intervals and derive rewards by
comparing these intervals with known reference values (Lin et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wu et al.,
2026). These comparisons cannot yield rewards for unlabeled responses without the corresponding
reference information. More closely related, Uncertainty-Aware Reward Modeling (UARM) follows
a reweighting approach for Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), using
the widths of calibrated intervals to scale advantages computed from point rewards (Pan et al., 2026).

However, this rescaling can adjust advantage magnitudes but cannot change the direction determined
by the point rewards, even when those rewards provide a misleading learning signal.
We address this gap with Range-GRPO, a semi-supervised post-training framework that learns from
unlabeled prompts using conformally calibrated reward intervals. The proposed objective aggregates
pairwise midpoint differences into group-relative advantages, using the widths of both reward inter-
vals to weight the contribution of each comparison. By changing the balance between positive and
negative pairwise contributions, this weighting can alter both advantage magnitudes and signs even
when the interval midpoints remain fixed. When all reward intervals collapse to points, this objective
recovers the credit assignment of Dr.GRPO as a special case (Liu et al., 2025).
As the policy learns, its response distribution can drift from the initial labeled calibration distribu-
tion, motivating weighted conformal inference to account for this shift. To construct reward intervals
under this drift, we adapt Robust Weighted Conformal Inference (RoWCI), which performs shift-
aware LaaJ calibration in a low-cardinality judgment state space (Noh et al., 2026).
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We propose Range-GRPO, to our knowledge the first semi-supervised GRPO framework
to construct on-policy learning signals through pairwise comparisons of conformal reward
intervals.
• We theoretically show that pairwise interval comparisons exploit range information beyond
scalar reduction to determine both the magnitude and direction of learning signals, while
retaining Dr.GRPO as the point-reward special case.
• Empirically, Range-GRPO outperforms the evaluated range-based methods on the synthetic
task. On real reasoning benchmarks, it achieves the highest ID and OOD average accuracies
among the evaluated semi-supervised methods, with relative improvements of 5.2% and
7.0% over the strongest baseline in our comparison, respectively.

RELATED WORK
2.1
REWARD SUPERVISION
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) typically derives rewards from ground truth
answers (Guo et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025) or executable verifiers. Prior work also combines out-
come rewards with signals derived from internal model states (Sun et al., 2026). Methods based
on majority voting use agreement among unlabeled rollouts to construct pseudo labels (Wei et al.,
2025; Zuo et al., 2025). Another line of work uses LaaJ to assign rewards according to explicit
rubrics (Zhou et al., 2026; Gunjal et al., 2026). TraPO, a semi-supervised RLVR framework, uses a
small labeled set to select unlabeled samples whose learning trajectories align with those of labeled
samples (Yang et al., 2026). Together, these approaches broaden the scope of available reward su-
pervision beyond direct outcome verification. However, consensus rewards may reinforce incorrect
answers, while point rewards from LLM judges may reflect biases unrelated to response quality.
Range-GRPO uses judge estimates with limited labeled calibration data to construct conformal re-
ward intervals that represent reward uncertainty on unlabeled rollouts.
2.2
CONFORMAL CALIBRATION
Conformal calibration is a promising approach to quantifying uncertainty in LLM judge evaluations
(Sheng et al., 2025). It constructs prediction intervals from labeled calibration data and provides
marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023). However, pol-
icy updates can shift the response distribution away from the calibration distribution, potentially vi-
olating the exchangeability assumption underlying these guarantees. Weighted Conformal Inference
(WCI) addresses covariate shift by reweighting calibration samples using density ratios (Tibshirani
et al., 2019). RoWCI, which we adopt, mitigates weight instability due to nuisance variation in
LLM judge inputs (Noh et al., 2026). Recent studies have integrated conformal prediction into LLM
training. CONST selects prompts for annotation based on conformal prediction set size (Xiao et al.,
2026). CFA uses conformal prediction sets to estimate answer reliability and reweight preference

ward interval widths to rescale GRPO advantages derived from point rewards. However, its interval
calibration procedure does not explicitly correct for shifts between the calibration and response dis-

conformal reward intervals for unlabeled rollouts.
2.3
RANGE-BASED LEARNING SIGNALS
Several studies derive scalar rewards by comparing predicted ranges with task targets. BAGEN re-
wards narrow ranges that contain the realized cost of the remaining trajectory (Lin et al., 2026).
REC-RL rewards predicted count ranges that contain the target, assigning larger rewards as both
bounds approach it (Liu et al., 2026). TempR1 uses the overlap between predicted and ground-truth
temporal intervals as a reward signal (Wu et al., 2026). Other studies use ranges to account for
uncertainty in training signals. In its interval setting, Variance-Aware PPO samples scalar rewards
uniformly from a predicted reward range and uses the corresponding variance to regularize policy
updates (Banerjee et al., 2025). IRPM-Interval constructs Student’s t confidence intervals over cho-
sen and rejected score groups to define binary rollout rewards (Song et al., 2026). UARM uses
the median quantile as a point reward and the width of a conformally calibrated reward range to
reweight response advantages without changing their signs (Pan et al., 2026). Range-GRPO instead
assigns relative credit within each rollout group through pairwise comparisons of conformal reward
intervals, allowing reward uncertainty to influence both advantage magnitudes and signs.

procedure that integrates this objective with shift-aware conformal reward calibration, as illustrated

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
TOY EXPERIMENT
Setup. We adapt a synthetic task from prior work (Lee et al., 2026) into a candidate selection task.
GPT-2-small (Radford et al., 2019) serves as the policy, and the prompt does not include the ranking
rule. The rank of the selected candidate determines the true reward. Accuracy measures how often
the policy selects the candidate with rank 1, which receives the highest reward. The labeled training
pool serves only to fit the state scorer and calibrate reward intervals.
We compare Range-GRPO with LB, Midpoint, UB, and UARM. LB, Midpoint, and UB have sep-
arate variants using the GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) advantage for-
mulas. All methods share the data allocation and use the same on-policy reward range construction
described in Section 4.3. This controlled comparison focuses on how the methods derive learn-
ing signals from reward ranges. We use the true rewards of unlabeled responses only to compute
empirical coverage.

5.2
MAIN EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate Range-GRPO on established reasoning benchmarks to complement the synthetic toy
experiment. These experiments examine whether Range-GRPO can effectively learn from unla-
beled prompts with limited labeled supervision and generalize beyond the labeled source domain.
Appendix C provides implementation details.
Baselines. We adapt the training and evaluation setting used for Table 2 in Yang et al. (2026),
without increasing the data or policy rollout budgets. The comparison includes the semi-supervised
results reported in that table for TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025), Self-certainty (Zhao et al., 2026), and the
Token-level Entropy and Sentence-level Entropy baselines (Agarwal et al., 2025). We additionally
reproduce TraPO under our experimental setting and implement UARM (Pan et al., 2026) with a
quantile reward model as a direct baseline.
Training Setup. For policy training, we use Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Yang et al., 2024) and generate
G = 8 responses per prompt. The training data comprise 1,024 labeled mathematics prompts from
OpenR1-Math-220k (Hugging Face, 2025) and 1,024 unlabeled MMLU-Pro prompts (Wang et al.,
2024). For Range-GRPO and UARM, we reserve part of the training data for their auxiliary com-
ponents, including reward estimation, calibration, and density ratio estimation (DRE) where appli-
cable. Training follows the TraPO schedule of ten labeled warmup epochs followed by five epochs
8


Preprint
using both labeled and unlabeled prompts. Labeled updates use binary correctness rewards based on
reference answers, while each method defines its own learning signals for unlabeled updates.
Reward Range Construction. After labeled warmup and before unlabeled policy updates begin,
we use the resulting policy to generate the source and calibration responses for reward range con-
struction. A frozen Qwen3-4B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) judge extracts judgment states for these
responses. We then fit a lightweight XGBoost scorer on the source states and compute calibration
nonconformity scores. The scorer and calibration scores remain fixed during subsequent training,
while density ratios are updated at each iteration using fresh target responses from the current policy.
For each unlabeled response, we combine its scorer prediction with the weighted calibration scores
to construct a calibrated reward range with α = 0.1.
Evaluation. We evaluate performance on both in-distribution (ID) mathematical benchmarks and
out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks outside the labeled mathematics domain. The ID bench-
marks include AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC, MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2024), Minerva
(Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024), whereas the OOD benchmarks
include ARC-c (Clark et al., 2018), GPQA-diamond (Rein et al., 2024), and MMLU-Pro. We report
avg@32 on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC, and pass@1 on the remaining benchmarks.
Quantitative Analysis. Table 2 shows that Range-GRPO achieves the highest ID and OOD averages
among semi-supervised methods. It also achieves the best accuracy on eight of the nine individual
benchmarks. Both averages exceed those of our TraPO reproduction with an entropy coefficient of
0.001. Notably, Range-GRPO attains this performance while reserving part of the same training
prompt pool for scorer fitting, calibration, and density ratio estimation rather than policy optimiza-
tion. Despite this auxiliary allocation, its total response budget, including both policy and judge
outputs, is approximately 18.2% smaller than that of TraPO.
Detailed prompt allocation and response accounting are provided in Section C. Range-GRPO also
achieves higher ID and OOD averages than UARM, which applies uncertainty weights at the re-
sponse level. This result is consistent with the benefit of preserving interval-dependent pairwise
structure when constructing learning signals. Range-GRPO also remains competitive with the fully
supervised 2K setting, exceeding its ID average while remaining close on OOD. Together, these
results support the effectiveness of Range-GRPO for semi-supervised post-training across the eval-
uated benchmarks.

CONCLUSION
We present Range-GRPO, a semi-supervised post-training framework that learns from unlabeled
prompts with limited labeled supervision. The proposed objective assigns relative credit to each
9


Preprint
response through pairwise comparisons of reward intervals. Our theoretical analysis shows that
Range-GRPO includes Dr.GRPO as a special case under zero-width intervals, while its pairwise
credit assignment allows range information to affect both the direction and magnitude of the learning
signal. Empirically, Range-GRPO outperforms the other semi-supervised methods in both ID and
OOD average performance and achieves the best result on eight of the nine individual benchmarks.
Together, these findings highlight the value of using interval relations to determine not only the
strength of a learning signal but also its direction. Beyond the evaluated tasks, future work could
extend this framework to domains where reward supervision is uncertain and expert annotations are
limited.

B.1
DATA AND TRAINING
Task. Following SSPO (Lee et al., 2026), we construct a synthetic dataset using words from the
Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) word list (Loper & Bird, 2002). We define a task-specific ranking
rule for the candidate words. Each prompt contains ten candidate words labeled A–J, and the policy
selects one letter. We first sort the words by increasing length, preserving the input order for words
of equal length. We then reorder only the three shortest words alphabetically, treating uppercase

B.2
LEARNING SIGNALS
Advantage construction. All methods in this toy comparison use the interval construction proce-
dure in Section B.3 but apply different rules to compute advantages from the resulting intervals. The
scalar baselines use the lower bound (LB), upper bound (UB), or midpoint of each interval as the
reward and compute either GRPO or Dr.GRPO advantages. Range-GRPO uses the objective defined
in Section 4.1 with λ = 0.2 and no additional advantage normalization.
We adapt UARM (Pan et al., 2026) by using the midpoint of each interval as the reward estimate
for the corresponding response. The shared interval construction procedure replaces the original
quantile reward model so that all advantage rules use the same source of uncertainty. We retain the
published advantage formula and use a signal variance stabilizer of 10−6.

B.3
RANGE CONSTRUCTION
Response features. To simplify state construction in the toy task, we compute Z directly from word
features rather than using an LLM judge. For each selected word, Z contains five fixed features:
Brown corpus frequency, Brown genre count, WordNet synset count, WordNet noun status, and
surprisal of the first token under GPT-2 after a fixed end-of-sequence context. We apply log(1 + x)
to the first three features and use fixed standardization for all five.
Scorer and calibration scores. We adapt the RoWCI scorer (Noh et al., 2026) to the toy task using
an XGBoost regressor that predicts rewards from Z. Using responses from the initial pretrained
policy, we fit the scorer and compute calibration nonconformity scores on their respective prompt
partitions. The fitted scorer and calibration scores remain fixed for the rest of training. All methods
share the fitted scorer, calibration scores, and DRE source data.
Updates during training. At each update using unlabeled responses, a random forest estimates den-
sity ratios from a fixed source feature set and features extracted from new target responses sampled
by each method’s current policy on the shared target prompt pool. These ratios reweight the fixed
calibration scores to account for changes in the response distribution without new target reward la-
bels. For numerical stability, we cap the estimated density-ratio weights at their 95th percentile. The
weighted cutoff also accounts for the weight of the query response. We clip intervals to [0, 1] and use

and use them for coverage diagnostics at a nominal level of 90%.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
We adapt the experimental setting of Table 2 in TraPO (Yang et al., 2026) for both Range-GRPO and
UARM (Pan et al., 2026). For all methods, we initialize the policy from Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Yang
et al., 2024). Following TraPO, we select the checkpoint with the highest mean validation accuracy
across AIME, AMC, MATH, Minerva, and OlympiadBench. Results under our experimental setting
are reported from a single training run per configuration.
C.1
TRAPO
We reproduce TraPO using its official implementation. The entropy coefficient differs between the
paper and the official implementation: the paper uses 0.01, whereas the released configuration uses
0.001. We therefore evaluate both entropy coefficients while keeping all other settings fixed. As
shown in Table 9, an entropy coefficient of 0.001 outperforms 0.01 on all benchmarks except GPQA,
improving the average ID and OOD performance by 2.6 and 1.2 percentage points, respectively. We
therefore report the results with an entropy coefficient of 0.001 in the main table.
For budget accounting, we follow the TraPO training procedure, which uses a warmup of 10 epochs
followed by 5 epochs that combine labeled and unlabeled signals. TraPO generates rollouts for
both labeled and unlabeled prompts in every training epoch. During warmup, only labeled roll-
outs contribute to policy updates, while unlabeled rollouts accumulate pass rate trajectories. In the
subsequent stage, TraPO uses these trajectories to select reliable unlabeled samples for policy up-
dates. We therefore count both labeled and unlabeled rollouts across all 15 training epochs. Table 10
summarizes the resulting response budget.
C.2
UARM
Reward Model Training.
We use a frozen Qwen2.5-Math-7B checkpoint (Yang et al., 2024) as
the RM backbone and train only a quantile head. The quantile head jointly predicts multiple reward
quantiles for each prompt-response pair. For each prompt reserved for RM training, validation,
or calibration, the initial policy generates 140 responses. Correctness checks against the ground
truth provide binary reward targets: one for a correct answer and zero otherwise. Within each
configuration, we retain the RM checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Among configurations,
we select the one with the highest mean validation AUC of the predicted median rewards. We use a
separate labeled subset for calibration. Both the RM and calibration parameters remain fixed during
policy training.

For the main experiments, we follow the range construction procedure in Algorithm 1 with two
practical implementation choices. First, to align with the TraPO warmup schedule, we treat the
policy immediately after labeled warmup as π0. At this stage, π0 generates the source and calibration
responses for scorer fitting and calibration. We keep the fitted scorer and calibration nonconformity
scores fixed throughout subsequent training.
Second, we normalize the calibration nonconformity scores so that differences in scorer predictions
are reflected more clearly during range construction. For a scorer prediction sZ(Z) ∈[0, 1], we
define

For the same positive prediction error, the normalized nonconformity score increases as the scorer
prediction approaches 0 or 1, until ψ(v) reaches its floor of 0.05. This makes the nonconformity
score sensitive to the scorer prediction in addition to the absolute prediction error.
We compute the weighted cutoff bqα,t(Z) from the normalized nonconformity scores and rescale it
for each query response as bτα,t(Z) = ψ(sZ(Z)) bqα,t(Z). Here, ψ(sZ(Z)) sets the query-specific
error scale, while bqα,t(Z) provides the corresponding normalized conformal cutoff. We then use
bτα,t(Z) in the range construction of Algorithm 1. Together, normalization and query-specific rescal-
ing allow the constructed ranges to reflect differences in scorer confidence more directly. At each
unlabeled update, we refit the DRE classifier using the fixed source states and fresh target states
