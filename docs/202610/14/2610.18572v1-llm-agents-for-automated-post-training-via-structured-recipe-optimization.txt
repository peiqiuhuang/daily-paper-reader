ABSTRACT 

Automated post-training transforms pre-trained large language models (LLMs) into models capable of handling diverse domains by automatically constructing and optimizing a recipe of coupled decisions over data sources, data processing, cross-domain composition, and training configuration under a limited experimen-tal budget. Existing automation either searches predefined recipe spaces that en-able systematic optimization but remain fixed rather than adapting their available choices to the target tasks, or uses open-ended agents that discover task-specific choices but leave them implicit in trial-level edits, limiting their systematic reuse and recombination across trials. To fill this gap, we introduce optimization over a task-adapted recipe space , a novel paradigm that can adapt the recipe space itself to the target tasks while representing its choices explicitly for systematic reuse, recombination, and joint optimization. We instantiate this paradigm with Struc-tured Recipe Optimization (SRO) , comprising an LLM-based Recipe-Space De-signer that constructs the task-adapted recipe space and a Multi-Domain Recipe Optimizer that uses structured trial evidence to guide LLM candidate construc-tion and multi-objective Bayesian trial selection. Extensive experiments show that SRO achieves the highest average score in all evaluated target-model and agent-LLM combinations. In joint post-training across three capability domains and six benchmarks, SRO reaches 41.1 versus 15.4 for the strongest baseline on Qwen3-1.7B with GPT-5.4, and 53.8 versus 40.8 on Qwen3-4B with DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Our code is available at https://github.com/RuohLiuq/SRO .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Post-training transforms pre-trained large language models (LLMs) into instruction-following mod-els capable of handling user requests across diverse domains, such as mathematical reasoning, cod-ing, and knowledge-intensive question answering (Ouyang et al., 2022; Tie et al., 2025). This pro-cess involves a sequence of coupled decisions over data processing, cross-domain composition, and training configuration, whose interactions can largely affect the resulting model (Tie et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2026). Coordinating these decisions to form a post-training recipe requires substantial expert engineering (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), while evaluating each candidate recipe requires actual training and multi-domain evaluation, making experimentation expensive. These considerations motivate automated post-training methods that automatically optimize for effective recipes under a limited experimental budget.

Figure 1: Three paradigms for automated post-training. Existing methods automate the overall post-training pipeline with two broad paradigms. First, op-timization over a predefined recipe space specifies a recipe space in advance and systematically searches over the resulting alternatives for any given task. For example, AutoPipe applies Bayesian optimization to post-training pipeline configurations (Chwa et al., 2026). While a predefined recipe space enables systematic comparison and optimization, it may exclude useful task-specific choices, such as data sources and processing methods, and thereby limit achiev-able post-training performance. Second, optimization over open-ended recipe space instead dele-gates more of the design process to LLM agents, allowing them to discover data, construct training interventions, and revise recipes using evaluation feedback for each specific task (Rank et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). This enables greater task-specific adaptation, but newly dis-covered interventions are typically introduced within trial-level edits without being consolidated into explicit, reusable choices. Consequently, evidence from previous trials cannot be systemati-cally reused to compare and recombine individual recipe decisions, making subsequent search less sample-efficient and reducing the likelihood of identifying high-performing recipe combinations un-der a limited training budget. Thereon, existing paradigms trade off task adaptability against opti-mization effectiveness . Predefined recipe spaces support effective optimization but can omit choices needed for the target task, while open-ended agents can use such choices but do not organize them into a space that can be efficiently optimized across trials. Both leave substantial post-training per-formance unrealized. To fill this gap, we explore a novel paradigm termed optimization over a task-adapted recipe space : constructing searchable choices for the target tasks and jointly optimizing their combinations. This paradigm introduces two key requirements. First, the recipe space must be task-adapted and structured : it should incorporate task-relevant alternatives for data sources, data processing, cross-domain composition, and training configuration, while representing them as explicit, reusable, and composable choices whose combinations remain executable and comparable across trials. Second, optimization over this space must be joint and sample-efficient . Since recipe components interact and each end-to-end evaluation is costly, search should reason over their combinations and use accumulated experimental evidence to prioritize promising recipes under a limited training budget. To realize this paradigm, we propose Structured Recipe Optimization (SRO) with two key com-ponents. A Recipe-Space Designer decomposes post-training into components for data sources, data processing, cross-domain composition, and training configuration, and asks the LLM agent to populate each component with choices based on the given task. We provide skills to guide this construction together with a fixed workflow for data preparation, training, evaluation, and valid-ity checks, allowing newly designed choices to be incorporated without sacrificing executability or comparability. A Multi-Domain Recipe Optimizer records executed recipes and their benchmark scores, organizing the results by domain. Selective retrieval provides relevant trial records and ex-ploration statistics to guide LLM construction of multiple candidate recipes by recombining choices across components. Multi-objective Bayesian optimization fits domain-wise surrogate models to 2Preprint the trial history and uses Pareto-aware acquisition to select one candidate for the next training trial. Figure 1 contrasts our proposed SRO with previous automated post-training paradigms. Our main contributions are: 

• We introduce optimization over a task-adapted recipe space as a novel paradigm for automated post-training, representing task-specific designs as explicit, reusable, and composable choices to support joint search over data and training decisions. 

• We propose SRO to instantiate this paradigm. The Recipe-Space Designer constructs the search space, while the Multi-Domain Recipe Optimizer organizes trial history to support LLM candidate construction and multi-objective Bayesian trial selection. 

• Extensive experiments show that SRO achieves the highest average score in all evaluated base-model and agent-LLM combinations. For joint post-training across three capability domains and six benchmarks, SRO reaches 41.1 versus 15.4 for the strongest baseline on Qwen3-1.7B with GPT-5.4, and 53.8 versus 40.8 on Qwen3-4B with DeepSeek-V4-Flash. 

2 RELATED WORKS 

Post-Training. Post-training adapts a pre-trained language model to follow instructions and acquire desired capabilities across diverse downstream domains (Tie et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2026). Mod-ern post-training can be organized into three coupled stages: data preparation, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL) (Tie et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025). Data preparation constructs the supervision used by subsequent training stages by collecting, synthesiz-ing, filtering, and mixing instruction–response pairs, reasoning trajectories, preference annotations, and task-specific feedback (Longpre et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Cui et al., 2024). SFT trains the pre-trained model on curated samples to acquire instruction-following behavior and specific task capabilities (Wei et al., 2022; Sanh et al., 2022; Longpre et al., 2023). RL further optimizes the SFT model toward desired behaviors and capabilities through reward signals (Zhang et al., 2025). For example, RL with human feedback (RLHF) improves alignment with human preferences, while RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) strengthens reasoning ability by rewarding correct solutions and intermediate steps (Ouyang et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025). LLM post-training involves designing a recipe over coupled decisions in data processing and training configurations, which demand sub-stantial human expert engineering (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Tie et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2026). Since each candidate recipe requires costly training and evaluation, automated methods are proposed to construct effective recipes automatically under limited experimental budgets. 

Automated Post-Training. Existing work on automated post-training spans from component-level to pipeline-level automation. Component-level methods automate decisions within a fixed training procedure, such as data generation, selection, and mixture optimization for data preparation (Wang et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2024; Zhang, 2026), sampling and resource allocation during SFT (Shin et al., 2026; Gualdoni et al., 2026), and domain balancing during RL (Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Ramesh et al., 2026). More recent work moves toward pipeline-level automation, which is the focus of our work and broadly follows two paradigms. Optimization over a predefined recipe space 

systematically searches over pipeline choices specified in advance. For instance, AutoPipe applies Bayesian optimization to a predefined configuration space with base-model choice and SFT/RL hy-perparameters (Chwa et al., 2026). Optimization over an open-ended recipe space instead delegates broader pipeline design to LLM agents. For instance, PostTrainBench gives agents full autonomy to discover training data and design and execute post-training strategies for target benchmarks (Rank et al., 2026). However, existing approaches trade off task adaptability against optimization effective-ness: predefined recipe spaces enable systematic search but may omit task-specific choices, whereas open-ended agents can discover such choices but struggle to organize and optimize them across trials. The Designer first constructs the initial task-adapted recipe space R0. The Optimizer then iteratively uses trial history to propose and select a recipe, executes and evaluates it, and updates the trial history and recipe space for the next iteration. Execution workflow and design skills. Reimplementing data loading, formatting, training, and evaluation across trials wastes compute and introduces implementation variance. We therefore pro-vide a fixed executable workflow that performs these operations, validates recipes, enforces com-pute constraints, and excludes evaluation data from training. To guide choice construction, we also provide reusable skills covering dataset discovery, generation-prompt design, cross-domain compo-sition, and training configuration. The workflow handles execution and validation, the skills provide design guidance, and the agent can thus focus on instantiating concrete recipe choices. 

Choice construction and expansion. Using the component schema and design skills above, the LLM agent instantiates discrete choices for each searchable component. For data sources , the agent identifies datasets relevant to each target domain, inspects examples, and specifies the field mappings required by the executable workflow. Each validated dataset becomes a data-source choice. For 

data processing , we provide two data-generation templates for expanding capability coverage and addressing model deficiencies. For each instantiated template, the agent specifies a source dataset and generation prompt; generation runs only when selected by a recipe, strictly excludes evaluation data. Validated generated datasets are subsequently registered as new data-source choices. For 

cross-domain composition , the agent specifies mixture ratios and assembly modes under a fixed training-sample budget. For training configuration , the agent constructs configuration choices for post-training algorithms such as SFT and GRPO. The validated choices populate the component-wise sets {V j }Jj=1 , whose valid combinations define the initial recipe space R0.3.2.2 MULTI -D OMAIN RECIPE OPTIMIZER 

The Multi-Domain Recipe Optimizer searches the current recipe space using feedback from previous trials. At iteration t, Domain-Structured Trial Memory retrieves a compact summary St−1 from the trial history Ht−1 and current recipe space Rt−1. An LLM proposer uses this summary to construct candidate recipes Qt, and multi-objective Bayesian optimization selects the next recipe rt using the complete trial history. The selected recipe is then executed and evaluated, updating the trial history and, when new data-source choices are produced, the recipe space for the next iteration. 

Domain-Structured Trial Memory. This memory retains each completed trial as a structured record ξi = ( ri, s(Mi)) , containing the executed recipe and its scores on all target domains. The scores are organized by domain to preserve differences in cross-domain performance, and the history before iteration t is denoted by Ht−1. The history supports optimization in two paths. The Bayesian optimizer uses the history to fit its surrogate models, while the LLM proposer receives a compact summary obtained by selective retrieval: St−1 = Retrieve( Ht−1, Rt−1). The summary includes Pareto-optimal or otherwise strong trials, lower-performing trials, aggregate statistics over explored 5Preprint choices, and coverage statistics identifying under-explored choices. Retaining recipe configurations alongside domain-wise results allows the proposer to compare historical recipes and their effects across domains rather than relying only on aggregate scores. 

LLM-Augmented Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization. Recipe optimization separates can-didate construction from trial selection . Since proposing recipes with an LLM agent is inexpen-sive relative to data preparation, model training, and evaluation, we use the agent to construct multiple candidates and Bayesian optimization to select which candidate to execute. At itera-tion t, the LLM receives the current recipe space Rt−1 and retrieved summary St−1, producing 

Qt = GLLM (Rt−1, St−1). It fills structured recipe fields with admissible choices rather than im-plementing a new training pipeline. The proposer compares recipe configurations and their domain-wise results across retrieved trials, together with choice-coverage statistics. These comparisons guide the reuse of promising configurations, the recombination of existing choices, and the explo-ration of under-explored options within the current recipe space. The Bayesian optimizer uses the complete history Ht−1 to predict each candidate’s domain-wise outcomes and uncertainty. The mean benchmark score within each domain defines an objective, modeled by a separate surrogate using executed recipes and their observed outcomes. A Pareto-aware acquisition function combines the predictive distributions to identify candidates expected to improve the current cross-domain trade-offs. We use expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI) and select rt = arg max r∈Q t αt(r), where αt(r) is the acquisition score. Intuitively, EHVI mea-sures the expected increase in dominated hypervolume relative to a reference point, accounting for predictive uncertainty. A warm-up strategy is used when too few completed trials are available to fit the surrogates. Appendix B.4 describes surrogate fitting and acquisition computation. 

Trial execution and update. The executable workflow executes the selected recipe rt, performing data preparation, training, and evaluation to obtain Mt and its benchmark scores. Data generation uses the sources and prompts from the Recipe-Space Designer. Correction uses the latest trained model to identify failed examples in its separate source dataset and generate corrective training data. While these failed examples are used to prompt data generation, the examples themselves are not retained in trial memory. After evaluation, the completed trial is added to the history Ht =

Ht−1 ∪ { ξt}. Validated generated datasets are registered as new data-source choices, producing the updated recipe space Rt. The updated states are the inputs to the next optimization iteration. 

4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate SRO through three questions concerning overall performance, explicit recipe-space optimization, and the mechanisms used to search the constructed space. 

Research Questions 

1. RQ1: How does SRO compare with existing automated post-training methods? 2. RQ2: How does our Recipe-Space Designer support recipe optimization? 3. RQ3: How does our Multi-Domain Recipe Optimizer support recipe optimization? 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Tasks and evaluation. We evaluate SRO in single-domain and multi-domain settings using the benchmarks and evaluation protocols provided by PostTrainBench (Rank et al., 2026). Each single-domain run targets one of GSM8K, GPQA Main, or HumanEval. Each multi-domain run jointly targets six benchmarks across three domains: mathematical reasoning (AIME 2025 and GSM8K), coding and tool use (BFCL v3 exec simple and HumanEval), and science and health (GPQA Main and HealthBench-Easy). The multi-domain aggregate score is the mean of the three domain scores, each computed by averaging its two benchmarks. All scores are reported as percentages. 

Models and agent LLMs. We post-train Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-4B-Base using DeepSeek-V4-Flash and GPT-5.4 as agent LLMs through the OpenCode CLI. Within each base-6Preprint model and agent-LLM configuration, all methods use the same agent LLM and CLI scaffold. We additionally report the official instruction-tuned models as reference points rather than competing methods because their post-training budgets are not matched. 

Baselines. We compare against PostTrainBench (Rank et al., 2026) and AutoTrainess (Yu et al., 2026), two end-to-end agent-based methods that automate data acquisition, training, and evalua-tion. These methods align with our focus on the agent’s ability to automate the complete post-training pipeline without requiring any predefined training data. PostTrainBench allows the agent to autonomously design and revise its training approach, while AutoTrainess provides a structured workflow. Both baselines run through OpenCode with the same base model, task specification, eval-uation interface, agent LLM, and compute budget as SRO. We additionally evaluate optimization over a predefined recipe space on GSM8K, whose official training split provides a task-specific training source. Appendix D.1 describes this supplementary baseline and its results. We also evaluate four variants with DeepSeek-V4-Flash: w/o structured recipe search replaces explicit recipe-space construction and its associated executable workflow with direct open-ended agentic recipe design; w/o LLM proposer uniformly samples candidate recipes from the same recipe space without using LLM proposals; w/o EHVI selection omits surrogate fitting and EHVI, selecting LLM-proposed candidates by self-reported confidence; and w/o structured memory replaces selec-tive retrieval with the full history of recipes and raw benchmark scores, without domain-structured summaries or choice-coverage statistics. In the last variant, the history used for surrogate fitting is unchanged. 

Training and compute budget. All runs start from the provided base model. The training-configuration space includes LoRA-based SFT and, for single-domain runs, an optional GRPO phase. Each run uses one GPU with a wall-clock budget of 10 hours for single-domain experi-ments and 20 hours for multi-domain experiments. All methods follow the evaluation protocols and data-contamination constraints provided by PostTrainBench. We select the checkpoint with the highest aggregate score during recipe optimization and report its full-set evaluation outside the time budget (Appendix B.1). 4.2 RQ1: O VERALL EFFECTIVENESS

A LIMITATIONS 

Dynamic recipe-space evolution. SRO constructs a task-adapted recipe space before optimization and can expand it during search when newly generated datasets become reusable choices within existing components. However, the set of searchable components and their organization remain fixed within each run: optimization does not introduce new component types or modify the com-ponent hierarchy. Building on SRO’s structured recipe representation, future work could extend this flexibility from choice-level expansion to schema-level evolution, allowing new components or hierarchical structures to be introduced as experimental evidence accumulates. 

Continual post-training across tasks and models. SRO uses accumulated trial evidence to guide recipe optimization for a fixed set of target tasks and a given base model. While this enables reuse across trials within a run, we do not study a continual setting in which new post-training tasks or base models are encountered sequentially and experience from earlier runs is carried forward. Future work could extend SRO to reuse recipe choices, trial evidence, or optimization priors across successive post-training problems, reducing repeated exploration and enabling increasingly efficient automated post-training over time.

B.2 COMPUTE BUDGET AND INFRASTRUCTURE 

Each multi-domain run (SRO and both baselines) receives a 20-hour wall-clock budget that covers discovery, data generation, training, and search-time evaluation. Each single-domain run (Table 1) receives a 10-hour budget. Final independent evaluation is conducted outside this budget. All meth-ods receive the same budget, task description, evaluation interface, and hardware. Each run has 13 Preprint exclusive access to one GPU with 16,896 CUDA cores, 132 streaming multiprocessors, and 141 GB of memory. Every method—SRO, the PostTrainBench baseline, and the AutoTrainess baseline—drives the same CLI agent scaffold (OpenCode) with one of two agent LLMs, DeepSeek-V4-Flash or GPT-5.4, served through a fixed gateway. In SRO the same agent LLM serves as (i) the discovery agent that constructs the recipe space, (ii) the in-loop recipe proposer, and (iii) the framework’s auxiliary data-generation calls (Rewrite / Correction workers). A SRO run consists of (i) a recipe space design phase in which the CLI agent explores sources and methods and submits the executable handoff, and (ii) a recipe optimization phase in which it executes at most iterative rounds of proposal, acquisition, training, and evaluation, until reaching wall-clock budget. B.3 TRAINING CONFIGURATION 

SRO runs start from the provided base model, which is never replaced or merged with external model weights. Warmup ratio is fixed at 0.05. Qwen3-1.7B-Base uses per-device batch size 8 with gradient accu-mulation 4; Qwen3-4B-Base uses batch size 2 with accumulation 16 (effective batch size 32 in both cases). The SFT loss is applied to the assistant target only. In single-domain runs, after three consecutive completed SFT trials with neither realized Pareto-hypervolume growth nor any improvement in domain-wise historical-best scores, the search enters a GRPO phase initialized from the best SFT checkpoint and continues LoRA training. Training prompts come from discovered sources with reference answers or executable tests. Rewards use the verification rules of the target benchmark to check answer correctness or unit-test success; evaluation examples never enter the training prompt pool. None of the reported multi-domain runs entered this phase within the time budget; all results in Table 2 therefore come from SFT trials. For GRPO, the proposer searches five components: learning rate ∈ { 5×10 −7, 10 −6, 2×10 −6}, KL coefficient β ∈ { 0, 0.02 , 0.04 }, group size G ∈ { 8, 16 }, training budget ( short or long ), and prompt selector ( all or unstable ). The short and long budgets correspond to one and two passes over the prompt pool, respectively. The unstable selector retains only prompts for which the current model’s sampled answers disagree; all uses the complete pool. The remaining settings are fixed. Rollout temperature is 0.8 and top-p is 0.95. Maximum completion length is 512 tokens, and maximum prompt length is half the model’s sequence-length limit. Per-device batch size is half the SFT value, with gradient accumulation adjusted so that the effective batch size is divisible by G.Training uses bf16 precision. Competition mathematics remains difficult. Across the four multi-domain runs, the final se-lected models achieve AIME scores of 0.0–6.7, substantially below the instruction-tuned reference scores of 26.7 for Qwen3-1.7B and 53.3 for Qwen3-4B. With only 30 evaluation problems, each additional correct answer changes the score by about 3.3 points. These results show that a high aggregate score does not imply strong performance on every capability. 

Recipe validity does not guarantee capability preservation. During the DeepSeek-V4-Flash run on Qwen3-1.7B, some candidate recipes produce severe regressions in BFCL performance. Such failures show that recipes assembled from admissible choices can still perform poorly on an individ-ual benchmark. Recipe validation checks executability but does not guarantee preservation of every capability. The observed scores alone do not determine whether these regressions arise from data composition, training settings, or output-format failures. 23 Preprint 

E CONTAMINATION SAFEGUARDS AND RUN HYGIENE 

Because automated post-training systems have been shown to engage in reward hacking when given the opportunity (Rank et al., 2026), all reported runs enforce the following safeguards. These apply identically to SRO and both baselines. 

Task-level rules. Every run receives the contamination rules reproduced in Appendix C.4: bench-mark questions, answers, solutions, tool-call targets, rubrics, and transformations of them are re-served for evaluation and must not appear in training sources, Rewrite inputs, diagnostic sources, generated-data prompts, or examples. 

Structural guards in SRO. Beyond the task-level rules, the SRO workflow enforces contami-nation safety structurally, since the search space is machine-checked rather than free-form code: (i) every declared source passes a benchmark adapter that canonicalizes rows and validates required supervision (explicit numeric targets for math, four choices plus an A–D gold label for GPQA, executable tests plus reference solutions for HumanEval, schema-valid tool calls for BFCL, com-plete multi-turn structure for HealthBench), and rejected sources cannot appear in recipe choices; (ii) HealthBench rows containing official rubrics , ideal_completions_data , or canary 

fields are rejected outright; (iii) Rewrite supervision is generated by an answer stage that never sees the source target, and Correction supervision is verified against the diagnostic source’s own oracle rather than against any benchmark artifact; (iv) diagnostic sources are held out from training-data roles and used only to surface model failures for Correction; (v) the handoff records a content digest of every source and method contract, so the exact training data of every reported run is reproducible and auditable after the fact. 

Model substitution. Only the assigned base model may be fine-tuned; checkpoints must derive from the base model. SRO satisfies this by construction (the workflow trains only from the assigned base checkpoint), and the baselines receive the same rule in their task prompt. Final evaluation loads each submitted final_model through the same benchmark entrypoints used during the run, with evaluation scripts and templates immutable from the agent’s perspective. 24
