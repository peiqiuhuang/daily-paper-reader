ABSTRACT 

Automatic prompt optimization (APO) has shown strong effective-ness for text-based LLMs and LLM systems, but its application to audio-visual understanding systems requires suitable textual in-terfaces through which multimodal reasoning can be exposed to text-based optimizers. We investigate how the design of textual interfaces affects prompt optimization for multimodal reasoning, on the audio-visual question answering task. We compare three interface designs, including a direct inference system consisting of a single omni-modal large language model (OmniLLM), a captioner-reasoner system, and an agentic architecture in which a primary agent orchestrates subagent OmniLLMs. Experiments with Qwen3-Omni and Gemini show that direct prompt optimization provides model-dependent gains; the captioner-reasoner system and the agen-tic system expose more interpretable optimization interfaces but are harder to optimize, where error analysis of cases affected by opti-mization is conducted. Our study highlights textual interface design as an important factor in making prompt optimization effective for omni-modal reasoning systems. Our implementation details are specified in GitHub, with code also being released there 1.

Index Terms — Prompt Optimization, Multimodal Reasoning, Omni-modal Large Language Models, Audio-Visual Question An-swering, Textual Interface 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal reasoning requires integrating evidence from multiple modalities to support a decision. In audio-visual question answer-ing (AVQA) task, for example, this may involve identifying relevant evidence from both audio and video, and reasoning over the evi-dence to answer a question, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Prior work has improved multimodal reasoning by enhancing chain-of-thought in MLLMs [1, 2], while another approach is to verbalize multimodal evidence into text for subsequent language-based reasoning, where rich and faithful textual descriptions, combined with additional rea-soning by text LLMs, have been shown to improve downstream per-formance [3, 4, 5, 6]. These findings motivate viewing text as an explicit interface through which multimodal reasoning can be struc-tured and improved.

Recent omni-modal large language models (OmniLLMs), which jointly process audio, video, and text, have achieved strong perfor-mance on audio-visual multimodal reasoning tasks [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. However, their outputs can be sensitive to how the task is instructed. Different prompt instructions for the same audio-visual input may elicit different perceptual details or organize the resulting evidence differently [12, 13], which may make manual prompt design brit-tle. Automatic prompt optimization (APO) addresses this problem by using task feedback to iteratively search for refined instructions. Given proper optimization signals and evidence, they usually pro-pose prompt that performs better on downstream tasks than heuristic designs [14, 15, 16]. APO methods optimize textual prompts based on text feedback [14, 15, 16]. Recent multimodal APO methods [13, 17] may addi-tionally incorporate multimodal feedback, but the optimized prompts themselves are still represented in text. Therefore, applying APO to a multimodal reasoning system requires the system to expose a tex-tual optimization interface, specifying which textual instructions the optimizer can modify and what feedback or intermediate informa-tion is available to guide prompt updates. The designs of vision-language interfaces for prompt optimiza-tion have been studied in previous works [17, 13], but less on omni-modal reasoning over audio-visual information. To this end, we raise arXiv:2610.20085v1 [cs.AI] 14 Oct 2026 Fig. 2 . Overview of the three systems we investigated and com-pared for instruction optimization .the research question: does exposing intermediate multimodal rea-soning through explicit textual interfaces make APO more effective than optimizing an end-to-end OmniLLM instruction directly? Thus we explore two types of audio-visual reasoning systems consisting of multi-LLMs, providing different explicit textual optimization inter-faces, 1) a captioner-reasoner system that externalizes multimodal evidence into a textual caption before a separate language model pro-duce an answer to the downstream task; 2) an agentic system where a text-based LLM agent raises queries to OmniLLM subagents to collect multimodal evidence for downstream task. And we compare them with the direct inference system, where a single OmniLLM di-rectly consumes multimodal input to conduct the downstream task. We study these questions through AVQA benchmarks, where a model must answer questions that depend jointly on the auditory and visual streams of a video [18, 19, 20]. AVQA provides a nat-ural testbed because it requires both retrieving sparsely distributed multimodal evidence and reasoning over the retrieved evidence. Both systems we study are established paradigms for video QA (VQA) or AVQA. Previous works [3, 12, 21] conducted caption-based VQA/AVQA focusing on properly aggregating captions from frames, inducing question-conditioned descriptions, etc. Agentic video systems instead acquire question-relevant evidence iteratively through OmniLLM observation and tool use [22, 23, 24, 25], achiev-ing strong performances. We therefore study captioner-reasoner and agentic systems as representative interfaces for static evidence ex-ternalization and dynamic evidence acquisition , respectively. While prior work primarily designs such systems to improve task performance, we instead study how effectively their intermedi-ate textual interfaces interact with APO, compared with direct Om-niLLM inference. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic study of textual interface design for APO in audio-visual reasoning sys-tems. Through controlled experiments and error analyses of reason-ing trajectories and action traces, we identify distinct limitations in captioner-reasoner and agentic systems, providing insights into fur-ther investigation on design and optimization of textual interfaces for audio-visual reasoning. 

2. METHOD 

In AVQA, the audio-visual reasoning system aims to predict the cor-rect answer y, given a video v with audio a, a question q, and an-swer options o. This section introduces three system architectures that differ in how audio-visual evidence is exposed through textual interfaces and, consequently, where textual instructions can be opti-mized. All LLM parameters are kept frozen. Three systems consist of OmniLLMs and optionally text-only LLMs. In following sec-tions, M denotes an OmniLLM and T denotes a text-only LLM, with subscripts indicating their functional roles. ϕ denotes each op-timizable prompt, belonging to its corresponding LLM component constructing different systems. 

2.1. Direct OmniLLM Inference 

We first consider the simplest setting, where an end-to-end (e2e) OmniLLM directly performs AVQA. The model jointly receives the video v, audio a, question q, and answer options o, and makes pre-diction ˆy = Me2e (v, a, q, o ; ϕe2e ). Me2e denotes the OmniLLM used for end-to-end inference and ϕe2e is its textual task instruction, informing the model that it should conduct the AVQA task and op-tionally the procedures and rules to follow. This system exposes the direct-inference textual interface ϕe2e .

2.2. Captioner-reasoner System 

We next consider a two-stage captioner-reasoner system. The cap-tioner produces c = Mcap (v, a ; ϕcap ), without knowing the ques-tion and options. Mcap denotes the OmniLLM captioner, and ϕcap 

is its textual instruction. A text-only reasoner then predicts ˆy =

Trea (q, o, c ; ϕrea ), where Trea denotes the reasoning LLM and ϕrea 

its instruction. This system exposes two optimizable instructions: the captioner instruction ϕcap and the reasoner instruction ϕrea .

2.3. Agent-Subagent System 

We further consider an agent-subagent system where the text-based LLM agent interactively acquires audio-visual evidence from Om-niLLM subagents, and then makes the answer decision. The pri-mary agent cannot inspect the raw video or directly answer the ques-tion without calling a subagent. However, it provides longer context window length not consumed by the large amount of multimodal in-put, allowing itself to memorize information collected from multiple interaction turns with subagents and reason from such memory. We adopt one subagent for captioning and another for respond-ing to multimodal evidence-collection queries issued by the text agent. The captioner summarizes globally salient events and pro-vides temporal information about these events, while details omitted from the caption can be further collected through targeted queries, conditioned on the current evidence in captions. These two types of tools are widely adopted in agentic audio and video understanding systems and have demonstrated their effectiveness [25] . At each interaction step t, the text agent Tagt takes an action based on q, o, and interaction history ht. The workflow always begins with a captioning call to obtain a description of the audio-visual content. At each subsequent step, Tagt decides its next action: 

zt = Tagt (q, o, h t; ϕagt ). The action zt can be requesting another caption, raising a follow-up query ut to a query-response component to collect information useful for answering the original question, or producing the final answer once adequate evidence has been col-lected. Both the captioning component and the query-response com-ponent are OmniLLMs, just playing different roles. The captioner component is defined as ct = Mcap (v, a ; ϕcap ), and the follow-up query responder component as rt = Mresp (v, a, u t; ϕresp ).This system exposes multiple textual prompts for optimization, most notably ϕagt . The perception-side prompts ϕcap and ϕresp are also optimizable, and can be adaptively raised by the text agent. 

2.4. Automatic Prompt Optimization 

During optimization, the system is executed on training examples, whose ground truths are available, using the current instruction ϕ.The optimizer F, which is a text-based LLM, observes optimization evidence e together with the predicted answer ˆy and ground-truth answer y, and proposes a revised instruction, 

ϕ′ = Fopt (ϕ, e, y, ˆy). (1) The evidence may include the model response, intermediate reason-ing trajectory, or, for the agent-subagent system, the interaction trace containing agent actions and subagent observations. The optimizer iteratively searches over candidate instructions using the training examples. We use QA accuracy as the task-level optimization objective for all APO algorithms. Candidate instruc-tions produced during the search are evaluated on the validation set, and the instruction with the highest validation accuracy within the specified optimization budget is selected as the final optimized in-struction, to be evaluated on the held-out test set. 

3. EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate APO for AVQA on Daily-Omni [18], comparing direct single-MLLM inference, captioner-reasoner pipelines, and agentic system, particularly their compatibilities with an APO algorithm GEPA [16], with brief reference experiment on APO algorithm COPRO [15]. 

3.1. Evaluation Dataset 

We evaluate on Daily-Omni [18], which contains 1,197 audio-visual multiple-choice QA examples spanning six task categories. Since APO requires examples for prompt search and validation-based can-didate selection, we stratify the benchmark into 125 training, 125 validation, and 947 held-out test examples, preserving the overall task-category distribution. The training set is used during prompt optimization, the validation set for selecting candidate instructions, and the held-out test set as the primary measure of optimization ef-fectiveness. We additionally report full-benchmark accuracy in case for comparison. 

3.2. Models and Systems 

For direct single-MLLM inference, we evaluate Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B in both its Instruct 2 and Thinking 3 variants, as well as Gemini-2.5-Flash 4. Qwen3-Omni is served locally, while Gemini-2.5-Flash is accessed via the Google API. For the captioner-reasoner system, Gemini-2.5-Flash generates an audio-visual caption and GPT-4.1 5

performs text-only reasoning. The agent-subagent system similarly uses GPT-4.1 as the primary text agent and Gemini-2.5-Flash for audio-visual perception. We initially tested Qwen3-Omni variants as captioners, but did not carry them forward to these two systems

3.3. APO Algorithms 

We evaluate two APO algorithms: COPRO [15] as a simpler APO for reference method and GEPA [16] as the primary reflective opti-mizer. GPT-4.1 serves as the optimizer LLM for both methods. To make their search effort comparable, we use the same optimization budget of 2,500 trials for COPRO and GEPA. Candidate instruc-tions are evaluated on the validation set, and the best validation-performing instruction across the corresponding search runs is se-lected for held-out evaluation. Full optimization and inference con-figurations are provided in our GitHub repository 6.

The results of experimenting APO on the captioner-reasoner system are shown in Table 3. The captioner-reasoner decomposition itself incurs a substantial performance drop compared with direct Gemini inference. Using the same Gemini-generated captions, both Gemini and GPT-4.1 reasoners obtain around 5% accuracy less than the di-rect inference. The discrepancy between the reasoner and captioner models did not lead to further accuracy drops. The drop is possibly because caption forms an information bottleneck between perception and reasoning: the captioner must compress the audio-visual input to text descriptions without knowing which details are key to the ques-tion. The downstream reasoner has to make decision on ambiguous observations. Moreover, providing additional textual interfaces, from only

4.3. APO on the Agent-subagent Interface 

We implemented the agent-subagent system as a ReAct-style pipeline [26], and applied GEPA. As shown in Table 4, its per-formance remains comparable to direct Gemini inference, while GEPA does not improve held-out accuracy. To understand the behavior of the agentic interface, we inspect samples whose correctness changes between direct inference and ReAct. Among direct-correct but ReAct-wrong cases, the domi-nant failures are conflicting perception evidence (61.7%) and incor-rect reasoning over otherwise useful evidence (27.3%). Conversely, ReAct improvements mainly come from retrieving additional useful evidence or richer captions. Thus, active evidence acquisition in the agent system can recover missing information, but can also introduce extra failure points. When applying GEPA on the agentic system, it succeeded in finding an optimized prompt with improved validation accuracy dur-ing prompt search, but the improvement does not transfer to test. This discrepancy suggests that the agentic interface is difficult to op-timize reliably. Table 5 further shows that GEPA acts on different scopes in the two interfaces. The optimized direct prompt mainly constrains evidence grounding at the final decision, whereas the opti-mized ReAct prompt changes a longer chain involving caption inter-pretation, query formulation, evidence acquisition, and integration. Accordingly, GEPA substantially changes the interaction strategy, reducing the calls to query-responder by 35.8% and total tool calls by 15.0%, yet yielding no gains in held-out accuracy. There are few potential explanations. One is that final-answer correctness alone may provide a weak optimization signal for a multi-stage agentic interface. Or the perception and reasoning ability of the LLM com-ponents set limits on how the agentic system can perform. 

5. CONCLUSIONS 

We study whether externalize audio-visual reasoning of OmniLLM-based systems through explicit textual interfaces makes APO more effective than directly optimizing an OmniLLM instruction,which we found is not uniformly the case. APO substantially improves di-rect Gemini inference, reaching 79.62% overall accuracy on Daily-Omni, despite it not producing much gain on Qwen3-Omni models. In contrast, the captioner-reasoner and agentic systems expose more interpretable optimization interfaces but obtain no reliable improve-ment from APO. Our error and trajectory analyses reveal interface-specific failure modes, including information loss in captioning, con-flicting perception evidence, and downstream reasoning errors. Our results suggest that explicit textual interfaces make multimodal rea-soning easier to inspect and steer, but not necessarily make APO more effective.
