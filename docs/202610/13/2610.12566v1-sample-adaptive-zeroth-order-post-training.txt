## ABSTRACT 

Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization offers a memory-efficient alternative to backpropagation for fine-tuning large language models. However, existing ZO methods typically sample training examples uniformly, overlooking their evolving importance and leaving avoidable estimation variance. To address this limitation, we propose Sample-Adaptive Zeroth-Order Optimization (SAZO), which learns sample importance online and adaptively allocates queries to accelerate convergence. SAZO ex-tracts gradient-norm information from existing directional differences to estimate sample importance without access to true gradients. Furthermore, to track evolving importance from sparse minibatch feedback, SAZO maintains per-example exponential moving averages that aggregate observations across updates. The method requires no additional model evaluations and only O(N ) additional scalar state compared with MeZO. We prove that SAZO asymptotically matches the leading conver-gence term of the minimum-variance sampling oracle, and achieves a strictly faster convergence rate than standard methods. Extensive experiments across four model families demonstrate improved average downstream performance and up to 6× faster convergence to the same training loss in terms of query count. 

## 1 Introduction 

Fine-tuning adapts pre-trained large language models (LLMs) to diverse downstream tasks, including sentiment analysis, natural language inference, and question answering (Devlin et al., 2019). However, standard fine-tuning incurs substantial memory overhead from stored activations, gradients, and optimizer states (Li et al., 2026), requiring up to 12 × the inference memory in the settings evaluated by Malladi et al. (2023). Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), including prefix tuning (Li and Liang, 2021) and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2021), reduces trainable parameters but still stores activations for backpropagation. To overcome this remaining activation-memory bottleneck, zeroth-order (ZO) methods such as MeZO bypass backpropagation and estimate updates from two forward evaluations, achieving a memory footprint close to inference (Malladi et al., 2023). This memory advantage comes with an optimization challenge: the resulting gradient estimates remain noisy, limiting the convergence speed of ZO fine-tuning (Nesterov and Spokoiny, 2017). Inspired by first-order adaptive optimizers such as Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015), mainstream approaches to accelerating ZO optimization focus on adaptation in parameter space. HiZOO (Zhao et al., 2025c) and PaZO (Zhao et al., 2025b) use Hessian-informed preconditioning, ZO-AdaMM (Chen et al., 2019) and R-AdaZO (Shu et al., 2025) adapt coordinate-wise update scales using historical moment estimates, and LOZO (Chen et al., 2025) exploits low-rank perturbation subspaces. However, ZO methods face a fundamental information bottleneck: each directional query reveals only a scalar projection rather than a full gradient. Identifying an unrestricted d-dimensional adaptation vector requires Ω( d)

such observations, so the cost of learning reliable parameter-wise adaptation can offset its intended acceleration. We therefore ask:

Since each update uses only a small subset of the training data, its effectiveness depends in part on which examples are selected. Our empirical analysis identifies an untapped source of information: the evolving importance of individual training examples. Their gradient norms differ substantially and change during training (Figure 1(a); Appendix E.5), suggesting that queries need not be equally valuable across examples. Uniform sampling overlooks this heterogeneity, leaving avoidable estimation variance. To use this information for query allocation, a natural approach is importance sampling, inspired by first-order methods (Katharopoulos and Fleuret, 2018; Borsos et al., 2018; El Hanchi and Stephens, 2020): allocate more queries to examples with larger gradient norms. Although sampling heterogeneity also exists in first-order methods, we argue that zeroth-order algorithms are more sensitive to it, since the estimated gradient variance of zeroth-order methods using only function-value access is much larger than that of first-order methods 2.However, applying importance sampling principle to ZO fine-tuning presents two challenges. First, sample importance is not directly observable. The variance-minimizing allocation depends on per-example gradient norms, p⋆i,t ∝∥∇ fi(xt)∥, whereas ZO queries reveal only random directional measurements. We therefore use the scaled magnitudes of existing directional differences to estimate these norms; their expectations approach the corresponding gradient norms as the perturbation radius vanishes, requiring no additional queries. Second, evolving importance must be tracked from sparse minibatch feedback. Each update reveals importance information only for examples in the sampled minibatch, while the importance of all examples may change as the model evolves. We therefore maintain per-example exponential moving averages to combine newly observed information with historical estimates, allowing importance tracking across intermittent observations. Uniform exploration provides opportunities to refresh infrequently sampled examples. Together, these designs yield Sample-Adaptive Zeroth-Order Optimization (SAZO) . The learned scores guide sampling, while inverse-probability weighting preserves the expected full-data finite-difference update. SAZO requires no additional model evaluations and only O(N ) scalar state for N training examples. The benefit of tracking rather than fixing this allocation is visible in Figure 1(b): SAZO incurs lower relative cumulative sampling regret than uniform sampling and an oracle allocation fixed at initialization. This improved allocation also translates into faster training-loss reduction in Figure 1(c), connecting the recovered sample information to optimization progress. Our main contributions are: • We propose SAZO, which estimates sample importance from existing directional differences without extra model evaluations and tracks its evolution from sparse minibatch feedback using per-example exponential moving averages, requiring only O(N ) scalar state.

## 2 Related work 

Sample-adaptive first-order optimization. Online variance minimization learns importance distributions from sampled feedback (Bouchard et al., 2016; Namkoong et al., 2017; Salehi et al., 2017). Static-regret analyses treat feedback as general loss sequences (Borsos et al., 2018, 2019); subsequent work connects sampling to Adam (Liu et al., 2020), federated convergence (Zeng et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025a), and joint convex optimization (Aolaritei et al., 2025). Avare exploits gradient continuity for dynamic-regret guarantees (El Hanchi and Stephens, 2020). SAZO addresses the ZO setting, where gradients are unavailable and directional feedback remains noisy, while accounting for the coupling between sampling and optimization. 

Accelerating ZO post-training. MeZO establishes memory-efficient language-model fine-tuning using forward evaluations (Malladi et al., 2023). Subsequent approaches accelerate optimization through curvature-based precondi-tioning (Zhao et al., 2025c,b), low-rank perturbations (Chen et al., 2025), variance reduction (Gautam et al., 2024), and directional alignment (Ma and Huang, 2025). Other methods improve update normalization (Dang et al., 2026), memory-efficient optimizer adaptivity (Dbouk et al., 2026), or adaptive layer selection (Wang et al., 2026). These studies focus on constructing parameter updates and allocating perturbations within the model, where learning reli-able parameter-wise adaptation is intrinsically difficult in high dimensions; SAZO instead adapts query allocation across examples.

We initialize each mi by averaging its directional-magnitude observa-tions over four complete, stable-order burn-in passes without replacement, using ordinary MeZO updates; these updates and queries count toward the training budget. Recommended hyperparameters. We recommend β = 0 .05 and ε = 0 .1, which are used for all main SAZO results. This single setting performs robustly across tasks and datasets and across masked and autoregressive models of different scales; the controlled sweeps in Section 4.4 further show low sensitivity over broad ranges of both parameters.

## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate SAZO from five complementary perspectives: downstream performance on masked and generative language models, convergence across model scales, scaling with the training-population size, computational effi-ciency, and robustness to its sampling hyperparameters. Appendix E provides the detailed experimental settings and additional diagnostics.

We first examine whether SAZO’s optimization strategy translates into downstream accuracy. 4.3 Memory and wall-clock efficiency 

We finally examine whether the gains above preserve the computational advantages of ZO optimization. SAZO retains MeZO’s memory footprint. Across the OPT configurations in Figure 4, the measured GB values for SAZO match those of MeZO. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose SAZO, a sample-adaptive zeroth-order optimizer that reuses existing directional differences to estimate sample importance without additional model evaluations. Per-example exponential moving averages track how this importance evolves from sparse minibatch feedback and require only O(N ) scalar state. Our analysis asymptotically matches the variance-optimal dynamic oracle’s leading convergence term, yielding a smaller convergence coefficient than uniform sampling when sample importance is heterogeneous.
