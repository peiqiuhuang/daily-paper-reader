## Abstract 

Recent advances in video generative models have enabled flexible conditional gen-eration and editing pipelines, such as text-to-video, image-to-video, and reference-guided synthesis. However, these workflows are inherently one-directional, map-ping conditions to videos without providing a mechanism to invert this process. In complex media production settings, the ability to recover controllable textual conditions from a given video is highly desirable, yet remains underexplored. While inverse prompting has been studied for image generation, directly extending these approaches to video is challenging due to the need to capture temporal dy-namics beyond static appearance, as well as the substantial computational cost of per-video optimization. In this work, we propose the first practical framework for efficient video inverse prompting. Specifically, we formulate inverse prompting as a meta-learning problem and introduce Meta Video Inverse Prompting (MVIP), which learns a meta-prompt optimized over temporal dimensions to enable fast and effective prompt inference. Our approach amortizes the inversion process, elimi-nating the need for costly iterative optimization at test time. Experimental results demonstrate that MVIP recovers higher-quality inverse prompts with significantly improved reconstruction fidelity compared to image-based inversion and iterative captioning baselines, while achieving substantial gains in efficiency. Code and video results are available at https://ueriniuno.github.io/mvip-page/ .

## 1 Introduction 

Video generative models have rapidly advanced to produce high-quality and temporally coherent videos, approaching realistic visual fidelity across diverse scenes and motions [62, 60, 70]. These models are increasingly deployed across a wide range of applications, including content creation [44, 57], editing [68, 74, 15, 36], simulation [7, 69], and world modeling [2, 7, 11]. To support such applications, modern video generation frameworks support flexible conditional pipelines such as text-to-video [55, 62, 70], image-to-video [5, 9, 60], and reference-guided synthesis [31, 75]. However, despite this progress, existing workflows to access video generative models are inherently “unidirectional”; viz ., while conditions can effectively guide video synthesis, they lack a clear mechanism to invert this process, e.g., for fine-grained semantic controls. For example, a creator may generate a video from a prompt and later wish to modify it, such as changing lighting or motion while preserving the scene. However, the original prompt is often unavailable or insufficient to reproduce the video due to the non-unique and model-dependent nature of prompt-video mappings, forcing users to rely on trial-and-error prompt engineering. This motivates the problem of inverse prompting , which aims to recover a textual prompt that can faithfully reproduce a given target video under a generative model. Prior work on inverse prompting has largely focused on images [65, 38, 50], primarily relying on gradient-based optimization or iterative captioning strategies. However, these approaches do not scale well to video. Gradient-based methods [65, 38] require repeated forward and backward passes during target-specific prompt optimization, which becomes prohibitively expensive for long video sequences. Contributions. In this paper, we propose Meta Video Inverse Prompting (MVIP), the first practical framework for video inverse prompting, to the best of our knowledge. Our key idea is to unify gradient-based optimization and captioning-based approaches within a meta-learning [16, 39] formulation, achieving both high reconstruction fidelity and efficiency. Instead of optimizing prompts from scratch for each video, we cast inverse prompting as a meta-learning that learns a shared prompt initialization across videos. During meta-training, MVIP performs gradient-based prompt optimization over diverse videos, allowing the meta-prompt to capture common spatiotemporal structures required for faithful reconstruction. At inference time, this learned initialization enables rapid prompt inference without costly per-video optimization, effectively behaving as a reconstruction-aware captioning mechanism. In this way, MVIP combines the reconstruction fidelity of gradient-based methods with the efficiency of captioning-based approaches. We evaluate MVIP on both real and synthetic videos, including videos generated from diverse video generative models, to assess its generality across different video distributions and genera-tion pipelines. Since video inverse prompting remains largely unexplored and lacks established benchmarks or baselines, we construct practical comparison methods based on recent image inverse prompting approaches, including PEZ [65] and ARPO [50], as well as captioning-based methods. The results demonstrate that MVIP consistently achieves superior reconstruction performance across diverse video-video and video-text alignment metrics. Notably, our method significantly outperforms iterative captioning approaches despite requiring substantially lower computational cost, showing that high-quality inverse prompting cannot be achieved by simply increasing inference-time iterations. Qualitative comparisons further show that our meta-learned initialization produces prompts that better capture temporal dynamics and fine-grained scene details, resulting in more faithful and controllable video reconstructions. 

## 2 Related Work 

Video generative models. Recent advances in video generation have been largely driven by text-to-video (T2V) frameworks, where diffusion models [6, 62, 60], transformer architectures [21, 20], and flow-matching objectives [28, 44] have enabled high-quality video synthesis from textual descriptions. Alongside open research models [9, 28, 33, 60, 70], commercial systems [40, 52, 19] have further established video generation as a practical tool for content creation. This progress has extended video generation beyond T2V to conditional interfaces such as image-to-video generation [72, 35, 22, 37], reference-guided synthesis [26, 67], and text-driven editing [46, 34, 61]. Prompt optimization and inversion. Recent video generative models have achieved substantial progress, yet their outputs remain highly sensitive to input conditions, including textual prompts and visual anchors. Minor variations in these conditions can lead to substantially different visual outputs [25, 73]. Existing prompt inversion methods have mainly focused on images, including pseudo-word embedding learning [18], model fine-tuning with unique identifiers [51], gradient-based hard-prompt optimization [65, 38], and black-box prompt search [50]. While effective for static visual content, these methods are not directly suited to video, where the recovered prompt must account for temporal evolution in addition to appearance. In this paper, we address this inverse problem through a reconstruction-driven framework that recovers prompts directly from target videos. 

Meta-learning. Meta-learning aims to extract transferable knowledge from a distribution of tasks so that a model can rapidly adapt to new tasks with limited data or optimization. Beyond metric-based methods [59] that learn shared embeddings and model-based approaches [54] that leverage memory, optimization-based meta-learning is a prominent paradigm. Notably, MAML [16] and Reptile [39] focus on learning an optimal initialization for rapid gradient-based adaptation. This paradigm has been widely adopted in domains where task-specific adaptation is costly, such as personalized federated learning [14], few-shot image classification [32, 53], and natural language classification [4]. A similar initialization-based view has also been explored in prompt learning and prompt optimization. Meta prompt tuning learns transferable initializations for cross-task generalization [47], while system prompt optimization uses a meta-learning framework to learn system prompts that generalize across diverse user inputs and unseen tasks [13]. These studies highlight the benefit of transferring task-level experience through learned prompt initializations. Building on this, we formulate video inverse prompting as a meta-learning problem, leveraging shared initializations to facilitate precise, reconstruction-oriented adaptation for each target video. 

## 3 Method 

In this section, we introduce Meta Video Inverse Prompting (MVIP), the first practical framework for video inverse prompting, to the best of our knowledge. Our key insight is that gradient-based prompt optimization for video reconstruction can be lifted into the instruction-prompt space within a vision-language model (VLM) and amortized via meta-learning, enabling it to directly generate reconstruction-aware prompts from unseen videos in a fast, captioning-like manner. We first formalize our problem of interest, viz ., video inverse prompting, in Section 3.1. In Section 3.2, we provide a brief overview of the meta-learning framework underlying our approach. 3.2 Background: Meta-learning 

Meta-learning aims to acquire transferable knowledge that enables a learner to quickly adapt to new tasks. It consists of meta-training and meta-testing. During meta-training, the learner uses multiple tasks to learn transferable knowledge across tasks. At meta-test time, the learned knowledge is used to facilitate adaptation to an unseen task. ## 4 Experiments 

In this section, we evaluate MVIP on both real and synthetic video datasets. We compare MVIP with caption-based and image inverse prompting baselines to assess reconstruction fidelity, and analyze how meta-learned initialization affects the efficiency and quality of the reconstruction prompt. We also analyze the iterative refinement baseline to examine whether feedback-based updates provide additional gains over one-shot prompt generation. 

Implementation details. We use a Wan2.2 [60]-based first/last-frame conditioned video generator for all reconstruction experiments. Long-form videos are split into scene-consistent segments with PySceneDetect, and first/last-frame conditioning is applied to each segment. This segment-level protocol is shared by all methods to keep generation and evaluation consistent. We use Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct [3] as the VLM for captioning and comparison, while keeping both the VLM and video generator frozen. Full implementation details are provided in Appendix B. 

Datasets. We evaluate on both real-world and synthetic videos. The real-world split consists of ActivityNet [8] and MSR-VTT [66], covering long human-activity videos and short everyday web videos, respectively. The synthetic split is built from VidProM [63], a large-scale text-to-video prompt-gallery dataset containing videos generated by Pika [43], VideoCrafter2 [9], and ModelScope [62], along with Wan2.2 [60] videos generated using VidProM prompts. We additionally evaluate on Kinetics-400 [27] and DAVIS-2017 [42, 45] to assess generalization beyond the meta-training datasets. 

Baselines. We compare MVIP with four baselines. FLF-Only uses boundary-frame visual anchors without text. PEZ [65] applies image inverse prompting to four sampled frames and concatenates the recovered prompts. Captioning uses the VLM instruction “Describe this video in detail.” Caption-ing+Refine adapts ARPO [50], an image reverse prompting method, to video by refining prompts through comparisons between sampled target and generated frames. 4.2 Downstream Application: Prompt-Based Video Editing 

To evaluate the effectiveness of MVIP in downstream video editing, we consider the subject-motion editing tasks of IVEBench [12] as a representative downstream editing benchmark. We follow its evaluation protocol and compare MVIP with two video editing methods: RF-Edit [61], an inversion-based editing framework for rectified-flow models, and Wan-Edit [30], a training-free video editing method for the Wan model family. For MVIP, we use GPT-4o [24] to minimally rewrite the recovered inverse prompt according to the edit instruction and check whether the requested motion is reflected in the edited prompt. The edited prompt and only the first frame of the source video are then used for video generation, without access to the benchmark target prompt or any editing-specific training. 

Quantitative results. As shown in Table 6, despite requiring no editing-specific training, the MVIP-based prompt editing pipeline achieves the highest Instruction Satisfaction and Content Fidelity while remaining competitive across the other metrics. In particular, MVIP remains competitive with RF-Edit [61], which is built upon a substantially larger video model with an RF solver, and Wan-Edit [30], which is specifically designed for editing within the same model family. These results show that inverse prompts recovered for reconstruction can also serve as effective control interfaces for downstream video editing. |Φ|. Too few tokens limit the meta-prompt capacity, while longer prompts bring little additional benefit. Inner-loop adaptation depth. We also vary the number of inner-loop adaptation steps K during meta-training. Table 9 shows that increasing K from 1 to 3 does not translate into better overall performance. Compared with K = 1 , K = 3 slightly lowers ViCLIP V-V and CLIP-I and worsens DreamSim. Moreover, as detailed in Appendix F, using three inner-loop steps takes about 3 × longer per outer update than using one inner-loop step. Since this additional cost does not lead to clear performance gains, we use K = 1 as our default configuration. 

## 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we have presented MVIP, a meta-learning framework for recovering inverse prompts from videos. While prior inverse prompting methods focus on images, we show that video inverse prompting requires a different approach due to temporal dynamics and the high cost of video optimiza-tion. Through comparisons with baselines, we show that MVIP improves video reconstruction using only a compact set of meta-learned soft tokens, without updating the VLM or the video generator. 15 A Limitations and Broader Impact 

Limitations. While our work demonstrates the feasibility of practical video inverse prompting, several aspects remain beyond the scope of the current framework. In particular, the proposed method assumes a first-last-frame (FLF) conditioning setup, where the boundary frames of the target video are provided. This design choice allows the model to focus on learning temporally coherent inverse prompting beyond static appearance reconstruction, and is also motivated by the fact that many recent public video generative models support FLF-conditioned generation. Nevertheless, the current framework does not address fully text-only video inverse prompting, which remains a challenging open problem due to the severe ambiguity of prompt-video mappings in the video domain. Despite this limitation, the proposed approach can still serve as a practical component within broader media generation pipelines when combined with existing image-based inverse prompting and editing methods. In addition, further developments may be required for deployment in real-world media production workflows. For example, learning more structured and editable captioning schemas through meta-learning could improve controllability and downstream editing flexibility, which we view as a promising direction for future work. 

Broader impact. This work can provide positive societal impacts by improving the controllability of video generative models, which benefits applications such as content creation, media production, and simulation. In particular, enabling reconstruction-aware inverse prompting may reduce the manual trial-and-error process currently required for prompt engineering, improving accessibility and efficiency for non-expert users. At the same time, the framework could potentially be misused to facilitate unauthorized reproduction or manipulation of generated media, including malicious editing through video generation pipelines. Furthermore, recovering prompts from videos may raise concerns regarding intellectual property when applied to proprietary or copyrighted generative content. While our work focuses on improving controllable generation workflows rather than bypassing model safeguards or recovering hidden prompts exactly, we acknowledge these potential risks and encourage future research on responsible deployment, access control, and misuse monitoring for inverse prompting systems.

Segmentation and conditioning. All videos are split into scene-consistent segments using PySceneDetect with a threshold of 27. Datasets.

For prompt generation, we uniformly sample 8 frames as input to the VLM, while PEZ uses 4 uniformly sampled frames. • Kinetics-400. We randomly sample 30 clips with seed 42 from the first shard of the Kinetics-400 validation split [27], which contains approximately 1,000 videos. We segment each clip using PySceneDetect with a threshold of 27 and a minimum scene length of 15 frames. Scenes longer than 5 seconds are further divided into shorter segments, resulting in 75 evaluation units in total. Video generation follows the same settings as for DAVIS-2017. 

Meta-learning hyperparameters. MVIP uses a prefix-based soft prompt consisting of 16 learnable soft tokens. The soft prompt is initialized from the token embeddings of the instruction “Describe objects, actions, camera motion, and temporal changes.” During meta-training, we use Best-of-N

prompt sampling with N = 4 , an inner learning rate of α = 10 −3, a meta-step size of η = 0 .1, and 100 outer-loop optimization steps. 

Compute infrastructure. Our experiments are conducted on compute cluster instances equipped with a single NVIDIA B200 GPU, each with 183GB of VRAM.

## D Baselines 

All baselines are evaluated on the same segmented video set as MVIP. For each segment, we use the same first-last-frame conditioning, generation seed, video length cap, resolution, and inference budget. Generated videos are evaluated against the same reference segments using the same metric pipeline. • FLF-Only. This baseline evaluates how much reconstruction can be achieved from the video conditioning signal alone, without using any text description of the target video. For 18 each video segment, we use the same first–last frame conditioning as MVIP, where the first and last frames of the segment are provided to the video generator. The text prompt is set to an empty string. The video is then generated using the same T2V model, resolution, length, and inference budget as MVIP. This baseline isolates the contribution of first–last frame conditioning from the contribution of inverse prompt recovery. • Captioning. We next consider a direct describe-then-generate pipeline, which provides a straightforward text prompt for each target video segment. For each segment, we feed the target video to Qwen2.5-VL and ask the model to describe the visible scene, subjects, actions, and temporal progression in natural language. The generated caption is then used as the text prompt for the video generator, together with the same first–last frame conditioning as MVIP. No prompt optimization, iterative rewriting, or learnable soft prompts are used. • PEZ. PEZ [65] is originally proposed as an image inverse prompting method, while our task requires video-level reverse prompting. Since there is no directly comparable video inverse prompting baseline, we adapt PEZ to the video setting as follows. For each video segment, we uniformly sample four frames across the temporal span of the segment. We then apply PEZ independently to each sampled frame to recover an image-level prompt. The recovered prompts are concatenated in temporal order and used as the text condition for video generation. • ARPO-based refinement. ARPO [50] proposes an iterative feedback-and-rewrite strategy for image reverse prompting, where a prompt is refined by comparing the generated image with the reference image and using the resulting feedback to update the prompt. To adapt this idea to video reverse prompting, we introduce a Captioning+Refine baseline. Starting from a VLM-generated caption using the instruction “Describe this video in detail,” we generate an initial reconstruction video. We then uniformly sample frames from both the target video segment and the generated video, and ask Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct to describe their concrete visual and motion differences. The resulting feedback is passed to GPT-4o-mini, which rewrites the current video prompt while preserving its correct content and structure. The refined prompt is then used to regenerate the video under the same first-last-frame conditioning and generation protocol as MVIP.
