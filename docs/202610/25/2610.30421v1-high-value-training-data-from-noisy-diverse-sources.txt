Abstract 

Training state-of-the-art models increasingly requires learning from large, heterogeneous datasets whose examples vary in quality, domain relevance, and source of supervision. Dataweighting—assigning different weights to training examples—offers a general way to control how such data influences training, but learning effective weights at scale remains challenging. We develop a scalable approach to learning dataweights: given a small, high-quality target dataset, we use metagradients through the training process to efficiently optimize training-example weights directly for performance on the target domain. To scale beyond a fixed dataset and model, we learn parametric weighting rules that generalize to new and streaming examples and transfer from smaller models to substantially larger ones. We study reward modeling as a prototypical application, where preference data may be noisy, adversarial, synthetic, or mismatched with the target domain. Learned dataweights improve held-out performance, automatically adapt reward models to specialized domains, and allow mixtures of human and synthetic supervision to outperform either source alone. 

# 1 Introduction 

In modern machine learning, we increasingly want to train reliable, specialized models using heterogeneous, general-purpose data. For example, large language models are trained on massive corpora scraped from the internet; vision models are trained on heterogeneous collections of web images. Domain-specific models are often adapted from such general-purpose models using much smaller, specialized datasets. This process poses several challenges, including low-quality or missing data, distribution shift, and mismatches between the training objective and the downstream evaluation criterion. Standard approaches to these challenges are often bespoke. For example, we might design heuristic filters to remove low-quality data [e.g., PMH+23]; synthesize labels where they are missing [e.g., XLH+20]; or explore data mixing strategies between general and target-specific data [e.g., XPD+23]. While these techniques can be effective, they require substantial problem-specific design and often rely on human judgments about what constitutes “good” or “bad” data—judgments that may themselves be unreliable or unavailable in many domains. As the need for customizable models and the amount of available general-purpose data grow, it is natural to seek a more scalable approach. An attractive general paradigm is to assign a weight to each training example [HGB+06; RZY+18; SXY+19]. We call this practice dataweighting . In theory, dataweighting can reduce the influence of outliers and low-quality examples and reshape the effective training distribution to better align with the downstream task. In practice, however, choosing the right weights is difficult, particularly when little is known a priori about the relationship between the training data and the target domain. In this paper, we therefore ask: 

Is there a scalable way to learn dataweights for training on diverse, heterogeneous data? 

Contributions. In this work, we revisit dataweighting as a unified, scalable way of reducing both the human effort and subjective decision-making involved in training specialized models. Our central premise is that 1arXiv:2610.30421v1 [stat.ML] 21 Oct 2026 a training sample’s utility should be determined by its impact on the performance of the resulting model, rather than by external heuristics. Instead of predefining quality criteria, we infer data usefulness directly from the learning process itself, and reweight the data’s impact appropriately. We provide algorithms and principles for instantiating dataweighting at scale . We leverage tools from data attribution (and specifically metadifferentiation [IE25; EIC+25]) to quantify how individual training examples influence a model’s behavior. In this way, data reweighting becomes an adaptive procedure that is directly aligned with the target objective. We show how to go beyond a fixed training dataset and training architecture by implementing a parametric dataweighting model and showing that dataweights learned with a smaller model can transfer to substantially larger ones. We identify reward modeling as a prototypical application of the challenges we study. Preference data is heterogeneous and may contain spam, bot-generated responses, inconsistent judgments, or subjective preferences. Large pretrained language models offer an attractive source of auxiliary supervision, but their labels can introduce additional uncertainty and bias. Moreover, the reward models’ training objective (Bradley-Terry loss) is often not aligned with the downstream uses that we care about, such as providing accurate model rankings. Our experiments with human and synthetic preference data support four main takeaways, validated at scale with models whose size is on the order of billions of parameters: 1. Improved performance with heterogeneous, noisy data. Learned dataweights improve model perfor-mance by upweighting helpful data points and downweighting harmful ones, such as outliers and adversarial examples. 2. Domain customization. When the target dataset comes from a specialized domain such as coding or STEM question answering, dataweights automatically identify relevant examples within diverse training data. 3. Effective integration of synthetic supervision. When data consists of a mix of human and synthetic labels, dataweights can automatically adapt to the quality of the synthetic labels, upweighting the helpful ones and downweighting the harmful ones. This results in improved performance over both (a) using real human data alone, and (b) using all human and synthetic data while treating them equally. 4. Alignment with downstream objectives. Dataweighting allows us to close the gap between the training loss and the downstream task we care about; in the reward modeling case, we can optimize dataweights for model ranking accuracy while retaining the standard Bradley–Terry training objective. Finally, in Section 4 we provide a few stylized examples illustrating these same benefits of dataweighting in theory, with the goal of providing further intuition about how dataweights work. Our work builds on validation-guided example reweighting [RZY+18; SXY+19; CFK+25] and scalable metagradient computation [EIC+25; IE25]. We investigate how these tools can improve training through continuous reweighting over long training segments, transfer of learned weighting functions to new data and larger models, and optimization of downstream ranking objectives; Section 5 gives the detailed comparisons.

## 2.2 Scalable dataweighting 

We now describe our procedure for approximating (2) at the scale of modern models and datasets. Acore primitive in our method is computation of the metagradient ∇w L′(A(w, θ0, k)) , the derivative of the target objective with respect to the data weights (i.e., through the algorithm A). Despite the complexity of computing this quantity, a line of work in data attribution [KL17; GZ19; IPE+22; GBA+23; PGI+23; IGE+24] offers efficient ways to compute approximate metagradients, and recent work shows how to compute these gradients exactly for moderate-sized models and datasets [EIC+25; IE25]. Using metagradient computation as a primitive, we describe a modular framework for learning optimal dataweights.

# 3 Applying scalable dataweighting to reward models 

We identify reward modeling as an ideal testbed for training specialized models from heterogeneous data. In re-ward modeling, our input is preference data : a collection of prompts, each paired with two candidate responses and a label indicating which response is preferred. The goal is to learn a model that takes a prompt and a re-sponse and assigns a numerical score, allowing us to compare new responses by predicting which one would be preferred. We train the model with the Bradley-Terry objective [BT52; CLB+17], which encourages higher scores for preferred responses. Formally, each training example is zi = ( prompt i, pref_resp i, unpref_resp i)

and the corresponding training loss for a reward model rθ is 

ℓ(zi; θ) := − log σ (rθ (prompt i, pref_resp i) − rθ (prompt i, unpref_resp i)) .Reward modeling is an excellent candidate for dataweighting: preference data is noisy, often based on LLM judges, and comes from diverse sources. We demonstrate the efficacy of dataweighting on real human preference data in a variety of settings. We begin by giving an overview of the training and target datasets we use, as well as our reward model training procedure.

## 3.2 Establishing the efficacy of dataweighting 

We start by establishing the efficacy of our proposed method for dataweighting relative to existing ideas in the literature on data-centric model adaptation. Specifically, we compare our method to: • Attribution-based data selection methods (approximate influence [KL17], TracIn [PLK+20], and TRAK [PGI+23]), which assign a score to each training example based on its estimated contribution to the target objective, and then keep only the highest-scoring examples; • Metagradient-based methods over a short horizon (as in [RZY+18] and others), where we attempt to optimize dataweights w by differentiating through single inner optimization steps—in other words, our method but with K = T.• Fine-tuning , where we leverage the high-quality target data by training directly on it, instead of using it to optimize dataweights for the training data. Specifically, we study a method which first pre-trains 

on the n datapoints from the heterogenous training set, and then fine-tunes on the n′ target samples. We also evaluate the unweighted baseline, which sets all weights wi equal to one.

## 3.4 Parametric dataweights and larger-scale transfer 

So far our dataweights have used the identity parameterization described in Section 2. The number of learned weights therefore grows with the dataset size n, which might be prohibitively expensive for large training sets; moreover, this rules out direct transfer of the dataweights to new training datasets. We now study the parametric variant (6): a small head hψ maps fixed example features ϕ(zi) to nonnegative weights 

wψ 

> i

Cross-scale transfer. A key practical question is whether dataweights learned with a small reward model can be reused when training larger models. We therefore learn dataweights at the 0.5B scale, freeze them, and apply them unchanged when training 1.5B, 3B, and 7B reward models for a single weighted epoch. This

These results suggest that dataweighting can be amortized: the expensive meta-optimization can be performed at a smaller scale, and the resulting weighting rule can be deployed directly in training runs of a larger model. 

Generalizing to unseen training examples. A limitation of per-sample dataweights is that they only assign weights to examples seen during meta-optimization. The linear parameterization removes this restriction: because it maps an example embedding to a weight, it can be evaluated on new training examples without relearning a separate scalar for each one. We test this by applying a linear head learned on the smaller 45k-row base to the full Human-155k training set. This transfer requires only computing embeddings and a forward pass through the learned weighting rule, rather than running metagradient optimization on the larger dataset. Figure 5(c) shows that the resulting weights continue to improve over the unweighted baseline across model scales. Thus, parametric dataweights provide a way to amortize data curation across datasets: a weighting rule learned on a manageable subset can be deployed directly on larger pools containing previously unseen training examples.

# 5 Related work 

We discuss prior approaches to leveraging heterogeneous and noisy data sources, particularly focusing on reweighting methods and aggregating heterogeneous preferences. Closest to our work are papers on automatic data selection using data attribution methods [GZ19; XMG+24; XPD+23] and metagradient descent [EIC+25; CFK+25; DKE+25], as well as prior work on learning to reweight data [RZY+18]. 

Reweighting methods. Our dataweights approach relates to a number of existing studies proposing reweighting strategies for deep learning [HGB+06; RZY+18; BL19; LWS18; WCH+21], often motivated by particular models of distribution shift such as covariate shift and label shift. Most related is the work of Ren et al. [RZY+18], who similarly assume a clean target set and aim to optimize training data weights, although their update differentiates through a single SGD step. Also relatedly, Meta-Weight-Net [SXY+19] and DataRater [CFK+25] learn parametric weighting functions using meta-objectives. Our implementation 13 computes exact metagradients within long training segments (typically 5–10% of total training), using scalable attribution methods that support differentiation through general optimizers. As described in Section 2, these segment-level updates approximate differentiation through the full training run. Despite mixed evidence on the effectiveness of reweighting [WCH+21; SKH+19], we find it effective for model customization and for combining human and synthetic preferences. We also study how a parametric weighting function transfers to unseen examples and larger reward models, and how dataweights can be optimized for downstream ranking objectives rather than the standard Bradley-Terry loss. 

Data attribution. Data attribution methods [KL17; GZ19; IPE+22; GBA+23; PGI+23] seek to quantify the influence of individual training data points on the model’s downstream behavior. Compared with methods that use attribution scores or metagradients to select training examples [EIC+25; DKE+25; XMG+24], we optimize continuous example weights directly in the training loss. We build on the R EPLAY algorithm [EIC+25; IE25] to compute exact “metagradients” of the model’s performance on the clean target data with respect to dataweights, allowing us to automatically discover—and thus downweight—noisy and irrelevant parts of the training data. 

Distributional robustness and domain adaptation. A central issue with heterogeneous data is that training examples may come from a mixture of domains that differ from the test distribution. A large body of work studies how to learn predictors that robustly generalize under such shifts via domain adaptation [BBC+06; BBC+10; ZZW+18], distributionally robust optimization (DRO) that optimizes worst-case performance over groups [SKH+19], and invariance-based objectives such as invariant risk minimization [ABG+19]. Rather than seeking a single representation that is invariant across environments, we directly use a small, high-quality target set to reweight heterogeneous training data toward the target distribution. Recent work in language model pretraining similarly uses robust or minimax objectives to tune mixture weights over data domains (as opposed to per-example weights) to improve downstream performance [XPD+23]. 

Robust statistics. Heterogeneity is often caused by corrupted observations—e.g., mislabeled examples or adversarial outliers. Robust statistics formalizes this through contamination models and offers robust estimators that take the contamination into account [Hub64; DKK+19]. A parallel literature in machine learning studies learning with label noise [NDR+13; LSH20]. All these works aim to detect corruption from internal inconsistency; our work is different in that we exploit an additional, high-quality target sample that specifies the desired test distribution, and we use it to determine how much each training example should influence the learned model. 

Synthetic data. An increasingly common cause of heterogeneity is mixing real and synthetic data. Synthetic data has proven useful for improving power in statistical inference [ABF+23; HSR+20], training better machine learning models in domains such as robotics [TFR+17] and computer vision [ZPI+17], and improving efficiency in language model evaluations [ZCS+23]. A key challenge with synthetic data is knowing which examples are useful and which are potentially even harmful. Our method can be seen as learning how to weight each synthetic example based on how it improves performance on the target set. 

Reward modeling, RLHF, heterogeneous preferences. Reward modeling and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) operationalize “alignment” by learning a reward function from human-labeled comparisons and then optimizing a policy against that reward [CLB+17; OWJ+22]. Humans may provide noisy feedback, or even vote adversarially. By optimizing against a clean target dataset, our method downweights such noisy and adversarial votes. At a high level, our work relates to social choice theory [BCE+16; FG06; AOW03], which studies principled ways for aggregating heterogeneous preferences. Some recent works proposed data selection algorithms for reward model training [FKZ+25; SST25]; however, these approaches primarily focus on reducing data volume. # B Dataset construction and reward model training 

Training data. We use two public preference datasets, arena-human-preference-100k and arena-human-preference-140k [CZS+24]. From these datasets, we filter for single-turn, non-tie examples, yielding a combined dataset of 155,577 rows of human-labeled pairwise preference data—we hereby refer to this dataset as Human-155k . We also obtained 10, 000 examples of bot and adversarial activity flagged by Arena in their preference data, henceforth referred to as Bot-10k .Using the prompts and response pairs found in Human-155k , we generate a synthetic preference dataset by replacing the human label with an LLM-as-a-judge label [ZCS+23]. Specifically, we prompt Gemini 3 Flash to pick the best response given the prompt and response pair. Measured against the original human labels, the LLM-as-a-judge labels reached an accuracy rate of 0.6812. We will refer to this synthetic dataset as 

Gemini-155k .From Human-155k and Gemini-155k we randomly subsample to derive the dataset pairs ( Human-45k ,

Gemini-110k ) and ( Human-77k , Gemini-77k ). Sampled data rows are disjoint within each pair. The first dataset pair illustrates a setting in which collecting synthetic labels is much cheaper than collecting real human labels, while the second dataset pair keeps the sample size fixed so we isolate the effect of using noisy labels. 

Target datasets. We evaluate dataweighting on two different target datasets: Expert and STEM . Expert 

is sourced from Arena’s arena-expert-5k : a heavily filtered subset of Arena’s preference data aimed at identifying sophisticated, domain-specific prompts. Around 5.5% of Arena’s preference data is labeled as “expert.” Since Expert is a subset of Arena’s general human preference data, we use it to evaluate in-distribution performance gains of dataweighting, albeit a niche part of the distribution. 

STEM is sourced from both HelpSteer3 and Tulu2.5 preference datasets [WZD+25; IWL+24]. From HelpSteer3, we filter for only rows labeled as “stem” or “code” and for rows where the responses were not tied. These preference rows contain a prompt, two responses, and an overall preference score labeled by ScaleAI annotators noted to have a relevant degree and/or software engineering work experience [WZD+25]. From Tulu2.5, we filter for the prm800k_pairs_phase2 subset, where chosen and rejected preference pairs 18 have been selected based on mathematical correctness [IWL+24; LKB+23]. Since STEM annotations come from ground truth verifiers and/or hired human rubric labelers with relevant domain expertise, rather than crowdsourced organic usage like in Human-155k , we use this dataset to evaluate out-of-distribution performance of dataweighting. For each domain, we use a meta-optimization target split and a held-out test split, in addition to the separate checkpoint selection set described below. The target split supplies the objective in Section 2; the test split is used only for evaluation. Expert has a target/test split of 3000/547. STEM has a target/test split of 6796/6796. 

Reward model training procedure. We adopt a standard reward model training procedure where we minimize the Bradley-Terry objective, as discussed in Section 3. Unless specified otherwise, the base pre-trained model we start from is Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct . The 1.5B experiments use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct ;the transfer experiments also include 3B and 7B reward models (Section 3.4). When training a reward model, we remove the vocab_head and replace it with a linear reward_head that takes in the hidden dimension output from a <|cls|> token and outputs a scalar reward. We use the same separate 100-example checkpoint selection set across methods for early stopping. This simple protocol lets us evaluate dataweighting; we leave alternatives such as cross-validation to future work.

## E.1 Evaluation protocol and scorer configurations 

Every scorer produces one score per training example (higher = keep) for the same pool/target pair. For each keep fraction k ∈ { 10, 20, . . . , 90, 95, 99 } we retain the top-scoring k% and tile the kept rows back to the original pool size, so every arm performs an identical number of optimizer steps regardless of k

(selection is therefore not confounded with a reduced compute budget). Each arm trains with the same reward-model training procedure as all other experiments, split into ten segments; we select the segment with the lowest loss on the checkpoint selection set and report the loss on the disjoint held-out test split, so checkpoint selection does not use the held-out test set. Every keep fraction has a random-drop control, and the unweighted baseline is replicated over three seeds to establish the noise band. Target and test splits were verified disjoint from each other and from the training pools. Individual keep-fraction points are single-seed.

# F Experimental setup 

Tables 3–5 summarize reward-model training, dataweight optimization, and the parametric weighting model. One training pass is one epoch over the pool. K denotes segments per pass, O the configured number of outer passes, and I the inner repeats per segment ( inner_repeats ). Each inner repeat replays the segment from its starting model state, computes a metagradient, and updates the dataweights. Thus I = 1 means one meta-optimization traversal per segment, not one model-optimizer step; each traversal can contain multiple optimizer steps. Forward-only training uses I = 0. This appendix gives the formal results underlying Section 4. The constructions isolate three benefits of target-guided dataweighting: correcting systematic synthetic-label bias, filtering severe corruption, and adapting the training distribution to a specialized target. They concern global optimization over fixed-size subsets, a restricted family of dataweights; they do not assert that a particular metagradient algorithm finds the optimum.

Connection to the informal statements. For each sufficiently large n, choose d(n) > n + n′ so that the finite-dimensional expected risk of dataweighting is at most RDW (n) + 1/ n, and each baseline’s expected risk is within 1/ n of its D → ∞ limit. Such a choice follows from the definition of lim sup and the convergence of the baseline risks proved above. The limits as n → ∞ then give the statements in the main text. For bias correction, taking λ = 1 gives cn′ = 1/ [2π(1 + n′)2].For covariate shift, an orthogonal rotation of the two signal coordinates maps the unit vector θ∗ to e1,giving bf = f ∗ = x1. The source signal covariance remains I2, while the target signal covariance rotates with the coordinates. Minimum-norm fitting, gradient-flow fine-tuning, subset selection, and population risks are preserved by this change of basis. 38
