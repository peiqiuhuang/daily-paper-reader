Abstract 

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has be-come a widely adopted approach for aligning vision-language models (VLMs), yet the se-curity of multimodal preference data remains largely unexplored. Existing preference poison-ing attacks either corrupt explicit supervision signals or require visible triggers, making them detectable under human inspection. We intro-duce DPOison-VL, the first clean preference poisoning attack against VLM-DPO, which ma-nipulates model alignment solely through im-perceptible perturbations on a small fraction of training images, while leaving all prompts, responses, and preference annotations semanti-cally intact. We formulate poison construction as a bilevel optimization and derive a gradient alignment surrogate that efficiently steers im-age perturbations toward the attacker-specified preference. We further introduce a preference displacement score for principled poison selec-tion with provable first-order optimality under a fixed poison budget, and derive a sufficient condition for preference inversion. Extensive experiments across diverse attack tasks, differ-ent VLM architectures and scales, and base-line comparisons demonstrate that DPOison-VL achieves up to 98% ASR with only 2% poisoned data and exhibits cross-model trans-ferability, while largely preserving clean accu-racy and general utility. Our findings reveal a critical vulnerability in VLM-DPO, where im-perceptible image perturbations in preference training data can silently redirect alignment. 

1 Introduction 

Vision-language models (VLMs) have achieved re-markable capabilities in multimodal reasoning, vi-sual question answering, and image-grounded dia-logue (Liu et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Hurst et al., 2024). Despite these advances, VLMs remain susceptible to well-documented fail-ure modes, including hallucination (Guo et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024a), harmful content gener-ation (Zhang et al., 2025), and poor calibration of helpfulness (Yu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024c). To address these failures, Direct Preference Optimiza-tion (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) has emerged as a widely adopted post-training paradigm for lan-guage model alignment, and has been recently ex-tended to VLMs to mitigate hallucinations (Yang et al., 2025) and improve trustworthiness (Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). However, the effectiveness of DPO also makes preference data a critical attack surface. Prior poi-soning attacks on VLMs have focused on super-vised multimodal training, either implanting back-doors triggered at test time (Lyu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2026; Jiao et al., 2025) or injecting stealthy image-text pairs (Xu et al., 2024), leaving the preference alignment stage unexplored. Recent work on preference learning in LLMs ma-nipulates alignment through label flipping (Kusaka et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Pathmanathan et al., 2025), ranking corruption (Wang et al., 2024), or poisoned preference pairs (Baumgärtner et al., 2024), but all corrupt explicit supervision signals and remain detectable under human inspection. Ta-ble 1 summarizes these two lines of work along five key properties, and none of them jointly targets the VLM-DPO stage, poisons through images alone, and keeps preference annotations clean. This raises an underexplored question in VLM-DPO: 

Can an attacker manipulate VLM-DPO using semantically valid preference pairs? 

In this work, we propose DPO ISON -VL, a clean preference poisoning attack against VLM-DPO that jointly satisfies all five properties in Table 1. To the best of our knowledge, DPO ISON -VL is the 

first attack to demonstrate that an adversary can manipulate VLM preference alignment by adding imperceptible perturbations to images, while keep-ing all preference annotations clean. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We introduce a new poisoning threat against VLM-DPO. To the best of our knowl-edge, DPO ISON -VL is the first clean pref-erence poisoning attack on VLMs. Unlike existing attacks that corrupt supervision sig-nals or require explicit triggers, DPO ISON -VL shows that imperceptible image perturba-tions alone can redirect DPO alignment to-ward attacker-specified behaviors. • We develop a principled poison crafting pipeline. 2 Related Work 

Preference Alignment for VLMs. Preference alignment has become an important post-training paradigm for improving the helpfulness, factuality, and safety of VLMs. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) simplifies RLHF by directly optimizing over pref-erence data, making it attractive for multimodal alignment. Recent VLM studies have adopted preference optimization to mitigate hallucinations, strengthen visual grounding, and improve safety and trustworthiness (Xie et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025, 2026). These works demonstrate the effectiveness of pref-erence data for VLM alignment but generally as-sume their trustworthiness. We show that a small number of clean preference pairs with impercepti-bly perturbed images can compromise VLM-DPO. 

Poisoning Attacks on Preference Alignment. 

Recent studies have shown that preference align-ment can be vulnerable when poisoned supervi-sion is incorporated into training. Existing attacks manipulate the alignment pipeline through inject-ing poisoned preference pairs (Baumgärtner et al., 2024; Pathmanathan et al., 2025), corrupting re-ward or ranking feedback (Wang et al., 2024), flip-ping preference labels (Kusaka et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026), or implanting backdoors through poi-soned human feedback (Rando and Tramèr, 2024). All these attacks corrupt explicit supervision sig-nals and target LLMs rather than VLMs, leaving the multimodal preference alignment unexplored. 

Clean-Label Poisoning and Backdoor Attacks on VLMs. Clean-label poisoning has been widely studied in machine learning and computer vision, where attackers preserve label correctness while inducing targeted model behavior (Biggio et al., 2012; Muñoz-González et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2020; Geiping et al., 2021; Souri et al., 2022). Recent work has extended such threats to VLMs. Shadowcast (Xu et al., 2024) injects visu-ally indistinguishable image-text pairs to manipu-late VLM responses after visual instruction tuning. TrojVLM (Lyu et al., 2024) and BadToken (Yuan et al., 2025) investigate backdoor attacks against VLMs, while CBV (Guo et al., 2026) introduces clean-label VLM backdoors using diffusion models to generate triggered images. However, these meth-ods do not investigate poisoning at the multimodal preference alignment stage. In contrast, DPO ISON -VL targets VLM-DPO through imperceptible im-age perturbations alone, while preserving semanti-cally valid preference pairs and inducing attacker-specified responses on clean target images. 

3 Methods 

Notation. We use I ∈ I for an image, x ∈ X for a prompt, and y ∈ Y for a response. A preference example is z = ( I, x, y+, y−), where y+ and y−

are the chosen and rejected responses. The VLM policy πθ is initialized at θ0, with reference policy 

πref . For each task, we use four disjoint image splits. Dcraft base provides base images for constructing clean preference candidates, while Dcraft target provides target examples for defining the attack objective. The held-out splits Dtest base and Dtest target evaluate clean behavior and attack effectiveness, respectively. 

3.1 Threat Model 

In this subsection, we describe the threat model of our study, including the attacker surface, attacker’s goal, capabilities, knowledge and constraints. 

Attack Surface. Motivated by prior studies of VLM data poisoning and DPO preference poison-ing (Xu et al., 2024; Pathmanathan et al., 2025), we study training-time data poisoning against VLM-DPO, targeting the preference data collection or curation stage. The attacker contributes a small number of poisoned preference pairs to the victim’s DPO training pipeline, with adversarial modifica-tions restricted to imperceptible image perturba-tions. The attack requires no inference-time trig-gers or perturbed inputs at inference time. 

Attacker’s Goal. The attacker aims to steer the VLM, after poisoned DPO training, to generate an attacker-specified response yadv instead of the cor-rect response ycorr on clean target images, while preserving correct responses on benign base im-ages. For example, in Task I, the attacker seeks to make the model misidentify Donald Trump as Joe Biden while still correctly identifying Biden images. Attack success and clean accuracy are evaluated by free-form generation on the held-out 

Dtest target and Dtest base , respectively. 

Attacker’s Capabilities and Knowledge. We consider DPO post-training of an open-weight VLM using multimodal preference data collected from multiple sources (Li et al., 2024). When such training data include externally contributed sam-ples, they may provide an entry point for data poi-soning attacks (Wan et al., 2023). In this setting, the attacker can contribute multimodal preference pairs to the DPO training pipeline. As in prior clean-label poisoning (Shafahi et al., 2018), we con-sider white-box access to the initial model. Since the initial VLM weights are publicly available, the attacker can obtain the same checkpoint θ0 and compute gradients for poison construction. The attacker can use a target crafting set, but cannot modify the original training data Dtrain or perturb images at inference time. 

Clean Preference and Stealth Constraint. The attacker can not construct poisoned samples that are obviously misaligned with human preference. Fol-lowing the clean-label poisoning principle (Shafahi et al., 2018), each contributed preference pair must remain semantically valid under human inspection: the preferred response is genuinely better than the dispreferred one given the perturbed image, and all preference annotations remain correct. The adver-sarial signal is embedded in imperceptible image perturbations, making poisoned samples difficult to detect through human inspection.

Models and DPO Training. We evaluate DPO ISON -VL on four VLMs: Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), InternVL3.5-1B and InternVL3.5-8B (Wang et al., 2025b). Unless otherwise stated, we train the vic-tim models with standard DPO for 2 epochs using LoRA fine-tuning. The selected poisoned prefer-ence set Dp is mixed with the clean preference set 

Dc for DPO training. Further details on poison crafting, training hyperparameters, and implemen-tation are provided in Appendix B.4.

5.3 Ablation Study 

To evaluate the contributions of our key design choices and compare DPO ISON -VL against alter-native poisoning attacks, we conduct experiments on Qwen3-VL-2B with clean DPO, two ablation variants, and two attack baselines: (1) No Perturba-tion serves as the clean DPO baseline without any perturbations or preference corruption; (2) Random Perturbation replaces crafted perturbations with random ℓ∞-bounded ones; (3) Random Selection 

selects candidates randomly instead of by ψ(z); (4) Preference Flipping (Kusaka et al., 2026) swaps chosen and rejected responses on clean target craft-ing images without perturbations; and (5) Feature Collision (Xu et al., 2024; Shafahi et al., 2018) crafts poisons by colliding with clean features ex-tracted by the VLM visual encoder. 

First, explicit preference corruption is not nec-essary for a strong attack. Unlike Preference Flipping, which directly swaps chosen and rejected responses, DPO ISON -VL keeps preference annota-tions correct and perturbs only the image, making it a much stealthier attack. Although preference corruption should intuitively carry a stronger sig-nal, DPO ISON -VL still surpasses Preference Flip-ping in ASR on Task A1, showing that a carefully crafted image perturbation can achieve higher ASR. 6 Conclusion 

We present DPO ISON -VL, the first clean prefer-ence poisoning attack against VLM-DPO, which manipulates model alignment through impercepti-ble image perturbations while preserving seman-tically valid preference annotations. DPO ISON -VL introduces a principled poison crafting and selection pipeline, with provable first-order opti-mality and a sufficient condition guaranteeing pref-erence inversion. Extensive experiments across four VLMs and three attack tasks demonstrate that small poisoning budgets can achieve high ASR while preserving clean behavior and general utility. These findings reveal a critical attack surface in VLM-DPO, where imperceptible image perturba-tions can compromise preference alignment with-out corrupting preference annotations. Limitations 

Our theoretical analysis relies on standard smooth-ness assumptions and gradient descent dynamics to characterize preference margin changes. Ex-tending these results to other optimization settings such as AdamW could provide further insights into practical DPO training. Although our experiments focus on standard DPO across multiple VLM archi-tectures and attack tasks, the underlying gradient alignment framework can also extend to DPO vari-ants or other preference optimization objectives. Future work could evaluate these extensions on additional multimodal preference datasets. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work investigates clean preference poisoning to reveal security vulnerabilities in vision-language models trained with Direct Preference Optimiza-tion. Such attacks could be misused to induce mis-leading model responses, potentially affecting ap-plications that rely on VLM-DPO. Our research aims to improve understanding of these risks and motivate the development of more robust multi-modal preference alignment methods. We also evaluate potential defenses, including image pre-processing and feature-space filtering, to inform fu-ture efforts to protect VLM-DPO training pipelines against data poisoning.

B Experimental Details 

B.1 Dataset Construction Task Data. We construct task-specific image splits for the three attack scenarios in Table 2. Task I follows the Biden–Trump identity data from Shadowcast (Xu et al., 2024), Tasks A1 and A2 are constructed from Visual Genome (Krishna et al., 2017) by metadata filtering and manual inspection. Since the clean DPO training set also contains im-ages from Visual Genome, we additionally remove any overlapping images between the task-specific splits and the clean training set to prevent data leak-age. For each task, the four image splits, Dcraft base ,

Dcraft target , Dtest base , and Dtest target , are mutually disjoint. Base crafting images provide poison candidates, while target crafting images are used to define the crafting objective. ASR and CA are measured on the held-out target and base test splits, respectively. 

Clean preference data. The clean preference set 

Dc, shared across all tasks, contains 12283 image-text preference pairs over 5392 unique images and comprises two types of clean preference data. The first type consists of 5172 description-style pref-erence pairs, including 3439 examples from the MiniGPT-4 visual alignment data (Zhu et al., 2023) and 1733 examples from the HA-DPO desc_data split (Zhao et al., 2023), whose images are drawn from Visual Genome (Krishna et al., 2017). Fol-lowing Meng et al. (2024), for each model check-point, we construct these description-style pairs in an on-policy manner: for each image, we sample five candidate responses from the initial VLM and use Skywork-VL-Reward-7B (Wang et al., 2025c) to select the highest-scoring response as chosen response and the lowest-scoring response as re-jected response. The second type consists of 7111 object-hallucination preference pairs derived from HA-DPO (Zhao et al., 2023) and normalized into the POPE-style yes-no format (Li et al., 2023), pro-viding preference supervision for mitigating object hallucinations. Together, these two components form the clean DPO training set Dtrain = Dc. For poisoned DPO training, we augment this clean set with the selected poisoned preference pairs Dp.

B.4 Poison Crafting and DPO Training Hyperparameters 

Poison images are optimized under an ℓ∞ pertur-bation budget ϵ = 8255 using 200 attack iterations, SGD with step size 0.5255 , batch size 1, and a learn-ing rate schedule with decay factor 0.5 at the mid-point of optimization following (Xu et al., 2024). The crafting objective maximizes the cosine align-ment between the poison DPO gradient and the target preference margin gradient, following Algo-rithm 1, and crafting is performed on 4 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. For downstream alignment, we train the victim VLMs with DPO for two epochs using LoRA fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022) and a frozen reference model. We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 2×10 −5, global batch size 64, micro-batch size 8, warmup ratio 0.05, min-imum learning rate ratio 10 −8, and DPO β = 0 .1,with DeepSpeed ZeRO Stage 2 (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) for distributed training. LoRA is applied to the {q, k, v, o, gate , up , down } projection mod-ules with rank 64, alpha 64, and dropout 0.05. To accommodate different software dependen-cies across models and training stages, our ex-periments use separate software environments for poison crafting and downstream DPO training. Poison crafting and attack evaluation are imple-mented using PyTorch 2.6.0, Transformers 4.57.6, PEFT 0.17.1, and Accelerate 1.9.0. For down-stream DPO training, we use PyTorch 2.9.1 and Transformers 4.57.0. The on-policy response pool is generated from the initial VLM using vLLM 0.14.0, with a sampling temperature of 0.9, top-p of 0.95, and top-k of 50. The sampled re-sponses are subsequently used to construct the pref-erence pairs for poison crafting. All experiments were conducted primarily on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The experiments reported in our main results re-quired approximately 600 GPU hours of computa-tion. B.5 Evaluation Details 

We use greedy decoding and a maximum response length of 128 tokens. We evaluate the attack suc-cess rate (ASR) and clean accuracy (CA) on the held-out test splits of the target and base image datasets, respectively. ASR is the fraction of im-ages in Dtest target yielding the attacker-specified re-sponse. CA is the fraction of correct responses on 

Dtest base . Neither test split is used in poison crafting. Since generated responses are free-form, mo-tivated by prior LLM-as-a-judge evaluation for open-ended generation (Zheng et al., 2023), we use gpt-6-luna as an LLM judge to deter-mine whether each response assigns the attacker-specified answer to the main entity in the image. Specifically, the judge checks whether the response identifies the main person as Joe Biden in Task I, the main car body as blue in Task A1, and the main active traffic light as green in Task A2. Figure 3 shows the judge prompt template. For general utility, following Xu et al. (2024), we evaluate on the VizWiz (Gurari et al., 2018) validation split and the GQA (Hudson and Man-ning, 2019) test-dev balanced split. The prompt asks the model to answer using a single word or short phrase. VizWiz is scored with standard soft consensus accuracy, while GQA is scored by ex-act match after lowercasing and light punctuation normalization.

C.4 Cross-Model Transferability 

We further evaluate the cross-model transferability of DPO ISON -VL by reusing image perturbations crafted on Qwen3-VL-2B to train three different victim models. To isolate the effect of visual pertur-bations, we also train each model using the same selected preference pairs with unperturbed images. As shown in Table 9, using perturbed images con-sistently achieves higher ASR than using unper-turbed images across all victim models and tasks. Notably, Task A2 achieves the largest ASR gains across all victim models, with an improvement of 58.5 percentage points on InternVL3.5-1B. Mean-while, CA remains largely comparable between the two settings, indicating limited additional im-pact on benign behavior. The relatively low CA on Task I for InternVL3.5 models is primarily at-tributable to their pre-existing tendency to refuse person-identification requests, rather than the poi-soning itself. These findings demonstrate that im-age perturbations crafted for one VLM can also influence the preference alignment of other VLMs, revealing a broader security risk in VLM-DPO even when preference annotations remain correct.
