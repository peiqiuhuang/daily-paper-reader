On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) trains a large language model using supervi-sion from a teacher instantiated from the same model as the student. The teacher is conditioned on privileged information (PI), such as verified solutions. The stu-dent, which does not observe PI, learns from the teacher’s next-token distribu-tions along its own sampled responses. However, PI can bias supervision toward shortcut reasoning with limited verification or reconsideration, accompanied by a sharp drop in the entropy of the output distribution. To address this challenge, we introduce self-distillation with verbal regularization (VRSD), which uses sys-tem prompts to regulate how the teacher uses PI, thereby encouraging rethinking and exploration to counteract premature commitment. Theoretically, we model prompting as attenuating the influence of PI on the teacher distribution and derive conditions for entropy preservation. Across three models and four tasks, VRSD improves Pass@1 over OPSD in 11 of 12 model–task settings, by 3.9 percentage points on average, while generally maintaining higher token entropy.

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Knowledge distillation transfers capabilities from a teacher to a student language model. In conven-tional off-policy distillation, the student is trained on fixed teacher or reference responses (Kim & Rush, 2016; Hsieh et al., 2023; Ho et al., 2023). Because these responses are generated indepen-dently of the student’s current policy, their prefixes may differ from those the student encountered during its own generation (Agarwal et al., 2024). On-policy distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024) reduces this mismatch by providing dense teacher supervision on the student’s own sampled responses. Its dependence on a separate teacher nevertheless adds training cost. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026a) removes this dependency by using the same model as both student and teacher under different conditioning. Given an input question, the student first gen-erates a response without access to privileged information (PI). The self-teacher then uses additional PI, such as a verified solution, to produce a next-token distribution at each position of the student response. The student is trained to match these teacher distributions. However, previous work (Kim et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026a) suggests that access to the PI can reinforce shortcut continuations while suppressing reconsideration. This influence is described through log-probability shifts, which measure changes in the model’s next-token log-probabilities when PI is added. These shifts indicate which tokens become more or less likely at the same position in the student’s response. Distilling such supervision may encourage the student to inherit both shortcut reasoning behavior and the associated concentration of its predictive distribution, limiting opportunities to explore alternative reasoning paths. The challenge is therefore to benefit from PI guidance while limiting its tendency to drive premature commitment. To tackle this challenge, we introduce self-distillation with verbal regularization (VRSD) . Rather than changing the model architecture, training objective, or PI itself, VRSD modifies only the self-teacher’s conditioning . Specifically, we introduce teacher-only system prompts that instruct the model to verify intermediate steps, consider alternatives, and reconsider the earlier reasoning be-fore committing to an answer. As illustrated in Figure 1, these prompts use the model’s existing instruction-following capability to regulate how strongly PI shapes the teacher’s token-level super-vision. The resulting method can therefore be incorporated directly into existing OPSD pipelines. In our theoretical analysis, we model this regulation as affine attenuation of the log-probability shifts induced by PI. We characterize when prompting can counteract teacher-side entropy collapse by deriving an exact condition for entropy increase. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce teacher-only verbal regularization for OPSD, using system prompts to regulate how the teacher uses privileged solutions without modifying the architecture or distillation objective. • We introduce an affine attenuation model of PI-induced log-probability shifts to explain when prompting counteracts teacher concentration reinforced by PI. • VRSD improves Pass@1 over OPSD in 11 of 12 model-task settings, while improving average Pass@1 and Worst@16 for all three models (Table 1). Further analyses indicate that VRSD mitigates over-concentration in output distributions (Section 5.3). Causal interventions in Sec-tion 5.6 indicate that, on average, VRSD students rely more on their reasoning chains to reach final answers compared to vanilla OPSD, indicating higher faithfulness in reasoning paths. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

OPD Knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015; Gou et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024) traditionally trains a student to match a teacher’s predictive distribution. For language models, distillation often uses token-level cross-entropy on hard teacher generated sequences (Kim & Rush, 2016; Hsieh et al., 2023; Ho et al., 2023). OPD (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025) trains the student on its own responses by matching the teacher’s next-token 2Preprint distributions at each step. This reduces the distribution mismatch of off-policy distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024) and provides more dense supervision than GRPO-like methods (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025). Building on these advantages, multi-teacher OPD (Core Team et al., 2026; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026; Kimi Team et al., 2026) has recently emerged as a practical paradigm for consolidating the complementary capabilities of multiple teachers into the student. 

OPSD Within the OPSD framework (Zhao et al., 2026a; H¨ ubotter et al., 2026; Sang et al., 2026), prior work has explored diverse sources of PI for teacher supervision. This privileged context may include reference solutions (Zhao et al., 2026a), successful (H¨ ubotter et al., 2026) or self-consistent responses (Li et al., 2026), and feedback from environments (H¨ ubotter et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) or humans (Wang et al., 2026b). Related approaches extend this context with reflections (Zhao et al., 2026c) or reusable skills (Jiang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026). More broadly, OPCD (Ye et al., 2026) distills behaviors specified by system prompts. Its system-prompt setting supplies general instructions without instance-specific reference solutions. However, in OPSD, conditioning the self-teacher on complete reference solutions can introduce shortcut bias (Kim et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026; Harne et al., 2026). To mitigate this bias, Puri-fied OPSD (Shen et al., 2026b) filters reference-driven shortcuts from training targets by comparing teacher predictions with and without the question. AntiSD (Shen et al., 2026a) instead combines GRPO with updates that discourage matching the privileged teacher. A complementary line of work modifies the PI supplied to the teacher: PS-OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026b) replaces solutions with struc-tured guidance, while ATESD (Han et al., 2026) adaptively controls how much reference reasoning is revealed. In this work, VRSD instead regularizes how the teacher uses PI: it retains complete ref-erence solutions and uses teacher-only system prompts to regulate their influence, aiming to mitigate shortcut bias and preserve the student’s exploration ability.

## 4 METHODOLOGY 

4.1 REGULARIZING PREMATURE TEACHER COMMITMENT 

OPSD uses PI to provide supervision signals. We first examine how PI can bias the self-teacher toward premature commitment, and how teacher-only system prompting can regulate this effect. 

PI can reinforce premature commitment At a student-generated prefix y<t , conditioning on PI changes the self-teacher’s relative preferences over possible next tokens. Following the conditional pointwise mutual information (PMI) interpretation of AntiSD (Shen et al., 2026a), we quantify the PI-induced log-probability shift for token v ∈ V as 

ut(v) := log qt(v)

pt(v) = PMI( v; c | x, y <t ). (3) When c contains PI such as verified solution, a positive ut(v) means that access to it makes v

more likely. Such support can guide reasoning, but it can also favor direct commitment to the supplied answer before the current reasoning has justified it. If PI primarily reinforces already-preferred conclusion tokens while suppressing tokens associated with verification or reconsideration, the resulting teacher distribution becomes more concentrated (i.e., low entropy) and assigns little probability mass to alternatives to premature commitment. Such concentration can propagate to the student because it is trained to match the teacher’s distribution. This preference transfer is reflected in the JSD gradient: −∇ θ Jα(sg[ qt]∥pt) =

Ev∼pt [At(ut(v)) ∇θ log pt(v)] , where At(u) = α−1 log[(1 − α) + αe u]. Since At(u) is strictly increasing in the PMI score u, tokens receiving larger positive PI-induced shifts (i.e., larger PMI scores) also have larger coefficients in the distillation gradient. Thus, when PI strongly favors pre-mature commitment, the student is explicitly trained to increase the probability of those continua-tions. If the student approaches a highly concentrated teacher distribution, competing continuations become less likely under the student’s own policy. As subsequent training uses student-generated rollouts, these alternatives become less likely to be sampled, limiting opportunities to explore dif-ferent reasoning paths. This link between teacher concentration and on-policy exploration motivates preserving probabilities on continuations that explore alternatives or reconsider intermediate steps. 

Regularizing teacher with system prompts We use the self-teacher’s instruction-following ca-pability to regulate how it uses PI. Specifically, we introduce a training-only system prompt d that instructs the teacher to verify intermediate steps, or consider alternative reasoning paths before com-mitting to an answer (see Appendix J for a behavioral analysis of the prompts’ regularizing effects on teacher reasoning). Formally, at the same prefix and with the same PI, the prompted teacher distribution is qdt (v) = πθ (v | d, x, c, y <t ) for v ∈ V . The student, by contrast, continues to gener-ate without access to either the system prompt d or the PI c. Training replaces the original teacher distribution qt with the prompted distribution qdt in Eq. (2), giving the per-prefix loss Jα(sg[ qdt ]∥pt),while leaving the JSD objective unchanged. The prompt introduces no additional learned module or regularization loss; it is absent from the student’s inference-time input. To quantify the effect of this prompt regularization, we next model how d changes the teacher’s token preferences. 

An affine model of PMI attenuation Using the same student distribution pt as reference, define the prompted PMI udt (v) := log[ qdt (v)/p t(v)] . Empirically, we find that the teacher-only system prompt reduced the PMI (i.e., the magnitude of PI-induced preference shifts) on average. Such an attenuation effect can be modeled as 

udt (v) = γtut(v) + κt, γt < 1, v ∈ V . (4) The affine coefficient γt controls the relative scaling of PMI differences across tokens, while κt

ensures normalization of qdt . We estimate the factor γt across models and tasks in Appendix A. Figure 2 shows mean estimates and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, indicating statistically sig-nificant PMI attenuation. This supports the hypothesis that teacher prompting weakens PI-induced token preference shifts. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

The experiments are presented in the following order. We first assess the task performance of VRSD in Section 5.2. Next, in Section 5.3, we examine student entropy to determine whether the entropy-preserving effect of teacher-side regularization carries over to the student. In Section 5.4, we then 5Preprint investigate which instruction choices contribute to the performance gains. Since our ultimate goal is to encourage the student to learn better reasoning behaviors, we next examine whether it exhibits the intended reasoning patterns. We present this qualitative study in Section 5.5. Finally, we extend this behavioral analysis with a quantitative assessment of reasoning faithfulness. A single VRSD teacher prompt improves OPSD, while multiple prompts yield further gains. 

As shown in Table 1, both variants improve average Pass@1 over OPSD on every evaluated model. VRSD (multi) improves average Pass@1 over OPSD by 7.2, 2.8, and 1.6 percentage points on Qwen3-4B, OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, and Qwen3-8B, respectively, yielding an average gain of approx-imately 3.9 percentage points. Furthermore, it achieves higher Pass@1 in 11 of 12 model–task settings. The benefits also extend beyond mean accuracy. VRSD (multi) matches or exceeds OPSD in Maj@16 across all settings and improves average Worst@16 on every model. These results show that VRSD improves both average accuracy and the frequency with which problems are answered correctly across all sampled responses. Compared to the single-prompt VRSD variant, the multi-prompt variant achieves higher average Pass@1 and Maj@16 on every model. 

VRSD remains effective even when OPSD underperforms the starting model. The benefits also extend to a setting where OPSD itself reduces accuracy.On Qwen3-4B SQL, OPSD’s Pass@1 falls below that of the starting model (69.8% versus 71.3%), whereas both VRSD variants exceed it, with VRSD (multi) reaching 81.0%. In this setting, changing the self-teacher’s conditioning turns a decline in student accuracy into an improvement over the starting model, demonstrating that VRSD can enable effective self-distillation even when OPSD does not yield an accuracy gain. 5.3 STUDENT ENTROPY 

Teacher-side regularization mitigates over-concentration in the distilled student. Our theoret-ical analysis identifies conditions under which prompting counteracts concentration in the teacher distribution. The student is trained to match this distribution, and it is the model ultimately used to perform the tasks. We therefore measure the student’s next-token entropy directly to assess whether the effect of teacher-side regularization carries over through distillation. As defined in Table 3, we assess students’ reliance on explicit reasoning using reasoning gain (RG). We also report the misleading-reasoning rate (MR) to measure how often full reasoning overturns an otherwise correct answer. 

VRSD students show stronger evidence of faithful reasoning. Retaining the full reasoning im-proves answer accuracy for both variants on all four tasks (Table 3). Averaged across tasks, RG reaches 5.8 percentage points for VRSD (single) and 5.0 for VRSD (multi), compared with 3.7 for OPSD. The effect is particularly clear on grading, where both variants more than double the RG of OPSD. The larger average RG is accompanied by lower MR than OPSD in seven of eight compar-isons across the two variants and four tasks. These interventions suggest that VRSD students rely 9Preprint more on their reasoning to reach correct answers than OPSD students do. Retaining their reason-ing improves accuracy more on average and less often turns a correct answer into an incorrect one. Together, these findings provide evidence of improved reasoning faithfulness. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced VRSD to improve OPSD by regulating how the self-teacher uses privileged solutions. The central idea is to retain solution guidance while encouraging verification and reconsideration, shaping the supervision through teacher conditioning. Our analysis connects this intervention to attenuation of solution-induced log-probability shifts and identifies conditions under which it coun-teracts teacher concentration. Experiments complement this analysis with improved accuracy and answer consistency, generally higher student entropy, and evidence of improved reasoning faithful-ness. These findings highlight teacher conditioning as a practical means of regulating privileged supervision. The model’s existing instruction-following capability can help preserve alternatives to premature commitment and improve the learning signal it provides to itself. Alongside these benefits, the current approach has several limitations that warrant further investiga-tion. The current approach relies on manually curated, task-specific prompts. This requires human judgment about which reasoning behaviors to encourage, and the resulting prompts may not suit every problem or remain equally useful as the student’s capabilities evolve during training. Future work could automate prompt selection and adaptation across problems and training stages. Beyond prompt design, verbal instructions control attenuation indirectly through the model’s response to their wording. This motivates methods that estimate and adjust PI-induced log-probability shifts directly. Such methods could use our entropy analysis to guide where and how strongly to attenuate the influence of privileged solutions, enabling more adaptive control of teacher regularization.

The system prompt acts as a regularizer of how the teacher uses c. Given c, the same prompts raise the student above OPSD on every task, keep the teacher as accurate as the OPSD teacher (Appendix F) and raise its average entropy on every task. The prompt thus does not add information about the answer; it shapes the distribution through which the answer in c is distilled, keeping the teacher from collapsing onto the solution and passing the verification and reconsideration steps to the student (Section 5.5). This is the role assumed by our analysis, in which d attenuates the influence of the privileged information on the teacher rather than supplying information of its own.

d2 You are an analyst who gets the join path right above all else. Before drafting the query, identify every table the answer must touch, and for each pair of them state explicitly the foreign-key columns that link them. Join only along those keys, and check that no join loses rows that should be kept or multiplies rows that should be unique. Prefer the shortest correct join path through the foreign keys; do not connect tables on columns that are not genuine keys. 

d3 You are an analyst who is meticulous about filtering and shaping the result. Before drafting the query, translate each condition in the question into the correct WHERE predicate, watching for value matching, text-vs-number comparisons, and NULLs. Then decide the aggregation and shaping deliberately: a "how many" needs COUNT, a "most / least / top-k" needs ORDER BY with LIMIT, a "per X" or "for each X" needs GROUP BY X, and a condition on an aggregate needs HAVING. Make the granularity of the output match exactly what the question asks for.
