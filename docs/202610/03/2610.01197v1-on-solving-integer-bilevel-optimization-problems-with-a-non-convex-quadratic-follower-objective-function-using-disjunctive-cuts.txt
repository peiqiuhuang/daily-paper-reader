Abstract 

In this work, we study bilevel optimization problems where all variables are integer, all con-straints and the leader objective function are linear, and the follower objective function is non-convex quadratic. Relying on bilevel-free sets derived from improving directions, we develop a disjunctive cut approach to exclude bilevel-infeasible solutions within a branch-and-cut algorithm. We show that our disjunctive cuts can be obtained by solving a cut generating linear program. Furthermore, we discuss conditions that allow the number of disjuncts in the cut generating linear program to be reduced, and we propose several strategies to identify improving directions and generate disjunctive cuts efficiently. We evaluate various aspects of the proposed branch-and-cut algorithm on both convex instances from the literature that fit our setting and new non-convex instances and compare the performance of our best approach with existing state-of-the-art approaches, which we significantly outperform. 

Keywords: bilevel optimization; branch-and-cut; nonlinear optimization 

# 1 Introduction 

Bilevel optimization problems (BOPs) arise naturally in settings where two decision makers act sequen-tially and hierarchically, with the so-called leader making decisions in the upper-level in anticipation of a 

follower’s optimal response in the lower-level . Such structures appear across a wide range of application domains, including energy market design, network interdiction, transportation planning, or pricing; we refer to recent surveys by Kleinert et al. [2021], Beck et al. [2023], Caselli et al. [2026] for introductions to the field. From a computational standpoint, BOPs are notoriously difficult. Already the linear-linear case, where both the leader and follower problem are continuous linear programs (LPs), is strongly NP-hard [Hansen et al., 1992]. The complexity increases fundamentally when convexity of the follower problem cannot be assumed. A particularly well-studied class of BOPs beyond the convex setting is that of integer linear BOPs, where non-convexity at the follower problem enters through integrality requirements. These BOPs are Σ p

> 2

-hard in general [Jeroslow, 1985], and solution approaches typically rely on branch-and-cut frameworks that alternate between branching on integer variables and generating cutting planes to remove solutions that are integer-feasible but violate bilevel-feasibility. Starting from the foundational work of DeNegre and Ralphs [2009], the field has developed rapidly, with contributions including the watermelon algorithm [Wang and Xu, 2017], intersection cuts [Fischetti et al., 2016, 2017,

It also means that, for a fixed leader decision, finding the optimal follower response is itself still an NP-hard problem, even without integer y.

## 1.1 Previous and related work 

Existing work on BOPs with non-convex integer follower problems is sparse. General-purpose frameworks such as those of Mitsos [2010] and Kleniati and Adjiman [2014b,a, 2015] can in principle handle such problems, but their broad applicability comes at a significant computational cost, limiting their practical reach to instances with very few variables and constraints. At the other end of the spectrum, recent work on integer nonlinear BOPs [Gaar et al., 2024] has demonstrated that exploiting DCs can lead to algorithms that scale to much larger instances, but this line of work assumes the objective function of the follower problem is convex quadratic. A very recent branch-and-cut approach by Horl¨ ander et al. [2025] also uses DCs to solve a larger family of integer nonlinear BOPs in which the leader and follower problems can contain nonlinear constraints as well. Contrary to traditional disjunctive cut approaches where DCs are derived from the convex hull of a disjunctive set, Horl¨ ander et al. [2025] solve a series 2of subproblems where each of them is optimized over one disjunct intersected with the integer lattice to obtain DCs. While this framework is capable of handling a more general problem class, its generality comes at a significant computational cost, as it is not tailored to exploit the structure present in our setting, namely the polyhedral constraints at the follower and a quadratic objective function. Regarding non-convex follower problems without integrality requirements on the variables, there is more existing literature. For example, Bomze et al. [2025] recently consider BOPs with a non-convex quadratic follower problem, proving Σ p

> 2

-hardness of this class and proposing an iterative lower- and upper-bounding scheme that is shown to be finitely convergent. We refer the interested reader to Bomze et al. [2025] and the references therein for more information regarding such problems without integrality requirements on the variables. To the best of our knowledge, no algorithm specifically designed for ILP-QILP BOPs with a non-convex follower objective function exists in the literature. We address this gap by developing a novel branch-and-cut (B&C) solution framework based on DCs. We exploit the algebraic structure of quadratic follower problems to derive polyhedral sets that contain no bilevel-feasible solutions in their interior. We denote such sets as bilevel-free. Contrary to the previous study by Gaar et al. [2024] where the follower objective function was a convex quadratic function, and the DCs were derived using a second-order cone programming (SOCP) based cut generating procedure, our new results show that DCs can be generated using LPs, even when the quadratic objective function of the follower is non-convex. 

## 1.2 Contribution and Outline 

The main contribution of this paper is the development of an exact solution algorithm for ILP-QILP BOPs, namely a B&C algorithm based on DCs. We also present enhancements of this algorithm and a computational study, which shows that our algorithm outperforms state-of-the-art algorithms for prob-lems with convex objective function and is also competitive for problems with non-convex objective (in the latter case, we compare with a solver which can tackle ILPs in the follower after using standard linearization techniques for the non-convex objective function). The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: In Section 2 we describe our bilevel-free set which is based on improving directions, and how to derive DCs based on it using a cut generating LP. We also describe how these cuts can be used to obtain a B&C algorithm to solve (1). In Section 3 we are concerned with obtaining improving directions which we need to obtain cuts. We also discuss techniques to detect and remove empty disjuncts to enlarge our bilevel-free set. In Section 4 we describe implementation details. Our computational study is presented in Section 5 and Section 6 concludes the paper. 

# 2 Disjunctive cuts for improving-direction bilevel-free sets 

In this section we first present basic notation in Section 2.1 and improving-solution bilevel-free sets used in earlier work in Section 2.2, before introducing improving-direction bilevel-free sets for our setting in Section 2.3. We then show how to use disjunctive arguments to derive improving-direction DCs and present the cut generating LP in Section 2.4. Finally, we present our B&C algorithm in Section 2.5, where we also show that we are guaranteed to obtain a violated cut for any bilevel-infeasible solution under a mild condition that is always satisfied in our case, and where we establish finite convergence of the B&C. 

## 2.1 Basic definitions and assumptions 

The optimistic BOP (1) has the so-called value function reformulation (VFR) min

is typically non-convex and non-continuous. Starting from the VFR (3), we obtain the single-level relaxation (SLR) by dropping (3e), i.e., the optimality condition on y for the follower problem. We denote the LP relaxation (i.e., replacing the integer constraint ( x, y ) ∈ Zn with the corresponding variable bound constraints) of the SLR as SLR. Given x ∈ Zn1 , let 

Y (x) = {y ∈ Zn2 : By ≥ f − Ax, ℓ ≤ y ≤ u}

be the feasible region of the follower problem for this leader decision x. Moreover, let 

F = {(x, y ) ∈ Zn : M x + N y ≥ h, y ∈ Y (x), q (y) ≤ Φ( x)}

be the feasible region of the VFR (i.e., equivalently, F is the feasible region of our original problem (1)). A solution ( x, y ) ∈ Rn is called (bilevel-)feasible if it is feasible for the original problem (1) (thus, if it is in F), otherwise it is called infeasible . Additionally, a solution ( x, y ) ∈ Rn is called bilevel-infeasible 

if it is feasible for the SLR, but violates (3e), so bilevel-infeasibility is a special case of infeasibility. In particular, if a solution is not feasible for the SLR, it is neither bilevel-feasible nor bilevel-infeasible. We note that these terms are not used uniformly in the literature and our definition of bilevel-infeasibility follows Fischetti et al. [2017], whereas, e.g., Gaar et al. [2024] call every solution that is not bilevel-feasible bilevel-infeasible. A set C ⊆ Rn is called bilevel-free if it does not contain any bilevel-feasible solution. Similar to Fischetti et al. [2016, 2017], Gaar et al. [2024], our aim is to design a B&C which starts with solving the SLR and where at any time a bilevel-infeasible solution is encountered, a DC is generated to cut off this solution. Note that this means we only need to be able to guarantee that we find a cut when we encounter a solution ( x∗, y ∗) which is integer during the B&C as continuous solutions are not feasible to the SLR per definition (and thus cannot be bilevel-infeasible). Standard branching within the B&C deals with the integrality constraints. Nevertheless, our DCs may also be separated when ( x∗, y ∗)is fractional, in this case the separation procedure is a heuristic. We make the following standard assumption for the rest of the paper (see e.g., Gaar et al. [2024] for a similar assumption). 

Assumption 1. The feasible region of SLR is a bounded polyhedron. 

## 2.2 Improving-solution bilevel-free sets and disjunctive cuts 

Before introducing our new bilevel-free sets which will be the starting point to deriving our new DCs, we first describe the bilevel-free sets and DCs used in our earlier work [Gaar et al., 2024]. The structure of the problem considered in that work is slightly different: while its leader problem is more general with also including conic constraints, its follower objective function is substantially more limited by allowing only convex quadratic objective functions by restricting R to be positive semidefinite. Furthermore, while we explicitly have bound constraints ℓ ≤ y ≤ u in the follower problem, they only have the constraint that y must be in a polyhedron. In particular, the approach of Gaar et al. [2024] is as follows. Given a bilevel-infeasible solution (x∗, y ∗) to be cut off, the construction of the proposed bilevel-free set starts from an improving solution 

ˆy ∈ Y (x∗), i.e., a follower solution that is feasible for the follower problem at x∗ and strictly better than y∗ (typically, an optimal follower solution for the follower problem at x∗). The corresponding 

improving-solution bilevel-free set is defined as 

S(ˆ y) = {(x, y ) ∈ Rn : Ax + B ˆy ≥ f, q (y) > q (ˆ y)}.

We note that any ( x, y ) ∈ S(ˆ y) cannot be bilevel-feasible, since ˆ y is a better follower response for x than 

y is. As for the derivation of DCs one needs sets with bilevel-free interior, the enlarged set 

S+(ˆ y) = {(x, y ) ∈ Rn : Ax + B ˆy ≥ f − 1, q (y) ≥ q(ˆ y)}

is used. The construction of this set exploits that all inputs and variables involved are integer. 4Applying disjunctive arguments to S+(ˆ y), the m2 + 1 disjuncts ¯D0(ˆ y) : q(y) ≤ q(ˆ y)¯Di(ˆ y) : Aix ≤ fi − Bi ˆy − 1 ∀i = 1 , . . . , m 2

are obtained, so one disjunct for the objective comparison and one disjunct for each of the m2 lower-level constraints. We note that in fact, the latter disjuncts ¯Di(ˆ y) will only appear for linking constraints, and not for the remaining lower-level constraints with Ai = 0 (and also not for the bound constraints), as the disjuncts corresponding to the remaining lower-level constraints are just trivially infeasible because ˆ y is a feasible follower solution. Since ˆ y is fixed, ¯D0(ˆ y) compares q(y) to the constant q(ˆ y) and is therefore a quadratic disjunct. This means it is convex only when R is positive semidefinite. Moreover, the separation problem in Gaar et al. [2024] thus is an SOCP. Note that this construction breaks down once R is not positive semidefinite, as ¯D0(ˆ y) then becomes a non-convex quadratic disjunct that is no longer amenable to an SOCP-based cut generating procedure. Closing precisely this gap is the starting point for the improving-direction bilevel-free sets we introduce next. 

## 2.3 Improving-direction bilevel-free sets 

In this section, we generalize the characterization of improving-direction bilevel-free sets , which was originally introduced by Wang and Xu [2017] for ILP-ILP BOPs. We start by repeating their result. 

Proposition 2 (Wang and Xu [2017], Fischetti et al. [2017]) . Consider problem (1) with R = 0. Then for any ∆ˆ y ∈ Zn2 such that gT∆ˆ y < 0 holds, the polyhedron 

¯T (∆ˆ y) = {(x, y ) ∈ Rn : Ax + B(y + ∆ˆ y) ≥ f, ℓ ≤ y + ∆ˆ y ≤ u}

does not contain any bilevel-feasible solution. 

Any vector ∆ˆ y ∈ Zn2 with gT∆ˆ y < 0 (as it occurs in Proposition 2) is called an improving direction :for any feasible follower solution y ∈ Y (x), moving into direction ∆ˆ y strictly improves the follower objective function, since gT(y + ∆ˆ y) < g Ty. Moreover, due to the definition of ¯T (∆ˆ y) we have that for any ( x, y ) ∈ ¯T (∆ˆ y) it holds that y + ∆ˆ y ∈ Y (x). This certifies that y + ∆ˆ y is a better feasible follower solution than y, thus y is not in Ω( x), and hence ( x, y ) is not bilevel-feasible. In the following, we expand Proposition 2 to the case when R̸ = 0. Note that compared to Proposi-tion 2, the condition on improving the objective function value actually becomes part of the definition of the considered set. We discuss this further after our theorem. 

Theorem 3. Consider problem (1) . Then for any ∆ˆ y ∈ Zn2 , the set 

T (∆ˆ y) = {(x, y ) ∈ Rn : Ax + B(y + ∆ˆ y) ≥ f, ℓ ≤ y + ∆ˆ y ≤ u, q (y + ∆ˆ y) < q (y)}

does not contain any bilevel-feasible solution. Proof. Due to q(y + ∆ˆ y) < q (y) we have that by moving from y to y + ∆ˆ y the follower objective function value is improved. Moreover, due to Ax + B(y + ∆ˆ y) ≥ f and ℓ ≤ y + ∆ˆ y ≤ u we have that the point (x, y + ∆ˆ y) is feasible for the follower problem. Hence, any ( x, y ) in T (∆ˆ y) cannot be bilevel-feasible, as (x, y + ∆ˆ y) is a better solution for the follower problem compared to ( x, y ). For R = 0, the requirement q(y + ∆ˆ y) < q (y) within the definition of T (∆ˆ y) in Theorem 3 reduces to gT∆ˆ y < 0, which is exactly the condition on ∆ˆ y in Proposition 2. Thus, for any ∆ˆ y not fulfilling this condition, the set T (∆ˆ y) is empty. Suppose the B&C terminates but returns a solution ( x∗, y ∗) that is bilevel-infeasible. Finally, suppose the B&C does not terminate. By Assumption 1, SLR is a bounded polyhedron and thus the SLR has a bounded feasible region, so branching on bounded integer variables produces a tree of finite depth. Whenever an integer solution which is not bilevel-feasible is encountered as solution at a node, Theorem 6 guarantees that the added DC is violated by the integer solution that generated it, thereby removing that solution from this node relaxation. Since the bounded node relaxation contains only finitely many integer solutions, the processing of each node terminates after finitely many separation rounds. Finite depth of the tree together with finitely many DC separation rounds per node in the tree contradicts non-termination. In our B&C described above fractional extreme points ( x∗, y ∗) of the current node relaxation P are not separated, as they can be handled by standard branching. However, in our implementation (see Section 4.1 for details), we also separate fractional extreme points to speed up the computation and strengthen the node relaxation P. For such extreme points our separation procedure is a heuristic in the sense that we are not guaranteed to find a violated DC. 

# 3 Separation of improving-direction disjunctive cuts 

In this section we discuss how to perform the IDG of our separation procedure, i.e., how to search for an improving direction ∆ˆ y for a given solution ( x∗, y ∗) or confirm its bilevel-feasibility. Before detailing the IDG, however, we note that it is often possible to reduce the size of the CGLP by dropping disjuncts that are not needed for the correctness of our approach. We first discuss such disjuncts in Section 3.1, since two of the procedures we later use for IDG are built directly around identifying such disjuncts. We then detail, in Sections 3.2.1 through 3.2.3, four different strategies for IDG. 

## 3.1 Redundant disjuncts 

For any k ∈ K , the disjunct Dk(∆ˆ y) can be dropped from D0(∆ˆ y) ∪ D (∆ˆ y) ∪ D ℓ(∆ˆ y) ∪ D u(∆ˆ y) and thus also from the CGLP without endangering the correctness of our approach, if no bilevel-feasible solution that could still improve the objective function value upon the current incumbent lies in P ∩ D k(∆ˆ y), because in this way we can guarantee to not lose any relevant bilevel-feasible solution. We call such a disjunct Dk(∆ˆ y) redundant . Removing redundant disjuncts reduces the size of the CGLP and may even strengthen the resulting cut. We will now discuss several options of removing redundant disjuncts from the literature. For the ease of readability, we will present these results already adapted to our setting. First, we present a basic result of Gaar et al. [2024] adjusted to our specific problem setting.

## 3.2 Improving-direction generation 

Next, we consider four different strategies for IDG, where three of them include solving a mixed-integer non-linear program (MINLP). Two MINLP-approaches based on producing many redundant disjuncts are presented in Section 3.2.1, and one MINLP-approach involving an improving follower solution is discussed in Section 3.2.2. Finally, one heuristic procedure, that is not guaranteed to produce an improving direction for a given bilevel-infeasible solution and that reuses previously found improving directions, is presented in Section 3.2.3. For ease of exposition, our description in the following focuses on the case where the solution ( x∗, y ∗)which we want to separate is feasible for the SLR, and therefore integer, as successful separation is required only in this case for the correctness of our B&C algorithm. We note that all IDG strategies can also be called for fractional solutions feasible for SLR as a heuristic. Even though fractional solutions are inherently infeasible due to violating integrality and will eventually be cut off via branching, they may still lie in a bilevel-free set defined by an improving direction, and thus can be cut off earlier by a DC.

3.2.3 Using a heuristic approach by utilizing previous improving directions 

The last approach we consider for IDG for a given solution ( x∗, y ∗) feasible for the SLR is a heuristic method in the sense that we are not guaranteed to find an improving direction if one exists, unlike all previously described IDG approaches, where such a guarantee holds. In our heuristic method we re-use previously generated improving directions ∆ˆ y for IDG, by employing an improving-direction pool , which we denote by C. Given a non-empty pool C, one can iterate through the directions of the pool to detect a feasible and improving one. For a direction ∆ y′ ∈ C , this involves 1. checking the feasibility of y∗ + ∆ y′ for the follower problem at x∗, and 2. verifying that ∆ y′ is an improving direction at ( x∗, y ∗), i.e., ∆ y′T

R∆y′ + 2∆ y′TRy ∗ + gT∆y′ ≤ − 1. Any direction that satisfies conditions 1. and 2. is a potential output of our IDG and can be employed to continue the separation procedure by solving (CGLP). If no such direction is present, then one can proceed with one of the exact approaches described previously and then add this newly found improving direction to the pool C. Also in this approach any improving direction is a certificate for bilevel-infeasibility of ( x∗, y ∗) and thus an explicit bilevel-feasibility check is not necessary.

• B: Solve the Binary-MINLP (9) to directly obtain ∆ˆ y.

• F : Solve the follower problem (2) to get ˆ y, and then calculate ∆ˆ y via (10). 14 For each IDG strategy, the associated MINLP (8), (9) or (2) can be solved either optimally or greedily (i.e., by stopping the exact solution process at the first solution found for (8), (9) and the first solution with better objective function value than y∗ for (2)). and G, respectively. Regarding the greedy variant, we note that any feasible solution to (8) or (9) yields an improving direction. When solving (2) greedily in FG , we impose an upper cutoff strictly smaller than q(y∗) on the objective function value to ensure that the obtained solution is improving. We also emphasize that, following preliminary experiments and the findings reported in Gaar et al. [2024], we do the DC separation procedure for both integer and fractional solutions ( x∗, y ∗) encountered during the B&C, even if for correctness we only need the separation procedure for integer solutions. In particular, at the root node of the B&C tree we continue adding DCs until no more significantly violated cuts can be found. Note that we employ a normalized violation threshold of 10 −5 throughout the algorithm. At all other B&C nodes, we limit the number of separation rounds to one for fractional solutions (and for integer solutions we of course have no limit to ensure correctness).

## 4.3 Constructing and solving the CGLP 

After performing the redundancy tests for the disjuncts, the next step is to build and solve the CGLP, which is based on the current B&C node relaxation P described in the beginning of Section 2.5. In particular, P can contain branching decisions at the current node and DCs added at ancestor nodes by incorporating them into P , Q and r within (7) when defining P. We note that, although including the DCs added at ancestor nodes in the CGLP through P is theoretically required to guarantee successful separation of bilevel-infeasible integer solutions, it is not mandatory for purely binary problems. In the latter case, any integer solution of the node LP is an extreme point of P, independent of whether P is defined with or without the DCs added at ancestor nodes. Conversely, for instances with general integer variables, it is in principle possible that a bilevel-infeasible integer solution ( x∗, y ∗) fails to be an extreme 15 point of P when previous DCs are not added to P. In such a situation, the CGLP may not yield a DC that is violated at ( x∗, y ∗). Preliminary computations indicated that, although incorporating previous DCs into P is numerically more robust and sometimes yields stronger DCs, it may adversely affect the overall performance due to the need to construct and solve substantially larger CGLPs. To balance these considerations, we adopt the following strategy: 

• All previously generated DCs are included in P in the CGLP at the root node of the B&C tree. 

• At other nodes with bilevel-infeasible solutions (recall that this means the solutions are integer), we first try separation via solving the CGLP without adding the previously generated DCs to P.Only if this separation fails and does not produce a violated DC, we call the separation again, but this time with the previously generated DCs added to P.

• At non-root nodes with fractional solutions, we try separation via solving the CGLP without adding the previously generated DCs to P. If this separation fails, we continue branching, unless we use option H and employ an improving-direction pool which contains other potential improving directions, in which case we proceed to the next feasible direction of the pool and try separation again. After having determined P, we are now ready to describe the details of the construction of the CGLP. For each disjunct that is identified as redundant, we remove the corresponding variables and constraints from the CGLP. Thus, we construct (CGLP) using only the non-redundant disjuncts in K and have to solve smaller LPs for deriving DCs. Note that in the following special cases, we skip constructing and solving the CGLP entirely: 

• Every disjunct is found to be redundant. In this situation, we prune the B&C node by inserting a dummy violated inequality (0 x + 0 y ≤ − 1). 

• All disjuncts except for the objective disjunct D0(∆ˆ y) are redundant. In that case, we directly introduce its inequality 2∆ˆ yTRy ≥ − ∆ˆ yTR∆ˆ y − gT∆ˆ y into the B&C node relaxation P.Finally, we want to briefly mention that in our B&C, we also try to separate integer solutions that are produced by the B&C MILP solver’s primal heuristics. Naturally, such solutions are not necessarily extreme points of P when considering integer (and not binary) instances, thus we are not guaranteed to find a violated DC for them. If our separation fails to produce a violated DC for such a solution even after including previously generated DCs in P, we reject it as an incumbent, to preserve the correctness of the B&C. 

## 4.4 Maintaining the improving-direction pool for heuristic DC generation 

In our computational study, an additional suffix H in a setting indicates an improving-direction pool 

C is employed for determining the improving direction ∆ˆ y for a given ( x∗, y ∗) heuristically. This pool 

C is initialized with ±1 unit vectors of length n2. Every time we solve an IDG problem, the pool is (possibly) expanded by the new improving directions ∆ˆ y found while solving the corresponding MINLP, i.e., (8), (9) and (2) for S, B and F, respectively. In particular, if the IDG problem is solved optimally in setting O, all the improving directions found during the solution procedure are used to expand the pool. Otherwise, if they are solved greedily in setting G, the first improving direction found feeds the pool. We use a capacitated pool having at most C elements. Once the capacity C is reached, the improving directions with the lowest utilization percentages (ratio of how many times this improving direction has produced a violated DC so far to the total number of trials) are replaced by the newly introduced ones. Moreover, we employ a diversification strategy for selecting directions from the pool C as ∆ˆ y: each time the heuristic IDG is called, the search begins from the improving direction immediately following the one that was most recently used to generate a DC. This proved helpful in preliminary computations, compared to e.g., always iterating through the pool from the start. For separating fractional solutions only the pool-based heuristic separation is executed without being followed by the computationally more costly exact separation. The only exception is the root node, at which we perform also exact separation once the pool has been exhaustively explored, even for a fractional solution. 16 An additional strategy we examine is the simultaneous addition of multiple DCs derived from differ-ent ∆ˆ y obtained within IDG for a single ( x∗, y ∗) at once, which will be denoted by the suffix M. Based on an initial analysis, we propose the following rules to be applied when the multiple-DC strategy is enabled: ( i) For the SO , BO and FO configurations, each time we solve the corresponding IDG problem, we introduce one DC for every distinct improving direction associated with the solutions stored by the MINLP solver; ( ii ) If the heuristic IDG option H is also enabled, the scanning of the pool is stopped once 

maxIntHeurCuts = 10 cuts have been added at integer nodes, and maxFracHeurCuts = 5 cuts have been added at non-root fractional nodes. At the root node of the B&C tree, no such bounds are enforced. 

# 5 Computational study 

In the following parts, we evaluate our B&C framework on 640 test instances across seven problem classes. The implementation was done in C++ using CPLEX 22.1 as the MILP and MINLP solver, with its optimality target parameter set to global optimality when solving MINLPs that include non-convex quadratic terms arising in IDG. All settings of CPLEX were left on their default values, except the feasibility tolerance parameter which is reduced from 10 −6 to 10 −8 while solving (CGLP), to mitigate numerical difficulties. The computations were performed on a single core of an Intel Xeon X5570 machine with 2.93 GHz processor and 8 GB of RAM, and the time limit for each run was set to 600 seconds. We first introduce the testbed in Section 5.1, tune key algorithmic components like IDG strategies, redundancy checks and DC generation on instances with convex follower objective function, and compare our best configurations against state-of-the-art methods from the literature in Section 5.2. Finally, we assess performance on instances with non-convex follower objective functions in Section 5.3.

## 5.3 Results for instances with non-convex follower objective function 

For the BNQKP, the non-convex follower objective function turns the IDG problems (8) and (9) of S and 

B into computationally heavy non-convex MINLPs. Conversely, the IDG strategy F reduces to a pure 0-1 quadratic integer program (QIP) with non-convexity restricted to the objective function. Furthermore, because BNQKP lacks leader constraints (1b), solving the follower problem to optimality in FO guarantees a bilevel-feasible solution that can immediately serve as an incumbent. In contrast, although the greedy variant FG achieved optimal solutions with a similar frequency in preliminary experiments not shown here, it frequently fails to produce valid incumbents for instances that terminate by timeout. This leads to misleadingly biased average upper-bounds ( UB ) and gaps. To ensure a consistent and reliable comparative evaluation, we therefore adopt FO as our main configuration for the current instance type, along with the RM strategy for redundancy checks based on the results in Table 3. The heuristic cut generation H and the addition of multiple cuts M are likewise activated. We evaluate FO against another benchmark method, MIX++ [Fischetti et al., 2017], across 180 BNQKP instances disaggregated over three matrix density levels p ∈ { 10% , 50% , 100% }. The previously used method from the literature, namely SOCP by Gaar et al. [2024], is inapplicable due to non-convexity of 

q(y), which requires MIX++ to rely on McCormick linearizations. The average results are presented in Table 6. Overall, FO outperforms MIX++ , solving 109 out of 180 instances with an average runtime of 279 .3seconds and a gap of 13 .0%, compared to 100 solved instances, 351 .5 seconds runtime, and an 18 .8% gap for MIX++ . The follower problem matrix density p highlights a clear structural trade-off. At low density (p = 10%), MIX++ performs better (51 vs. 38 solved) because sparse interaction matrices keep McCormick linearizations compact, which also explains its better overall Gap* . The high RGap of FO at this density stems from weak root incumbents rather than a weak root bound, as indicated by the similar RGap* 

values for both methods. However, as density increases to 50% and 100%, the proliferation of cross-product terms causes MIX++ ’s performance to degrade sharply, with runtimes increasing to 390 .0 s and 460 .3 s and solved instances dropping to 27 and 22, respectively. In contrast, FO scales far more robustly on dense instances, maintaining stable runtimes ( ∼ 276 s–288 s) and significantly lower optimality gaps (∼ 8.5%). 21 6 Summary and outlook 

In this paper, we study bilevel optimization problems where all variables are integer, all constraints and the leader objective function are linear, and the follower objective function is a quadratic function which can be non-convex. We develop a B&C algorithm, which uses DCs that exclude bilevel-infeasible solutions. These DCs are based on bilevel-free sets which generalize the set introduced in Wang and Xu [2017], Fischetti et al. [2017] for the case with linear follower objective function. Compared to the DC approach of Gaar et al. [2024], next to allowing for the non-convex case, which the bilevel-free set used in Gaar et al. [2024] could not handle, our DCs can be obtained by solving a cut generating LP based on an improving direction rather than a cut generating SOCP. We discuss conditions under which disjuncts are redundant and we propose and computationally compare several strategies to generate improving directions and to separate DCs efficiently, including a pool-based heuristic scheme. Our computational study confirms the practical value of the presented approach. On instances with a convex follower objective function, our best configurations consistently outperform the SOCP-based approach of Gaar et al. [2024] across all five benchmark instance sets considered: On QBCov-1 and 

QBCov-2 , our best setting solves all 80 instances to optimality with an average runtime of at most 12 seconds, whereas the approach of Gaar et al. [2024] fails to solve several instances within the time limit of 600 seconds. On the more challenging QBMKPInt set, our best setting solves 65 out of 99 instances, compared to only 40 for the approach of Gaar et al. [2024]. To assess the performance for follower problems with non-convex objective function, we introduce a new instance set BNQKP by modifying existing bilevel knapsack instances. Here, we compare with a state-of-the-art mixed-integer bilevel linear solver from Fischetti et al. [2017], which can be applied to these instances after linearizing the non-convex objective function. Our best setting solves 109 of the 180 instances compared to 100 instances for the approach of Fischetti et al. [2017]. Future work could address several directions. First, different improving directions can give rise to disjunctive cuts of different strength for the same bilevel-infeasible solution. A formal dominance analysis (in the spirit of the analysis Battista and Ralphs [2025] carry out for the mixed-integer bilevel linear case) between the cuts derived from two improving directions, and how such a result could be exploited algorithmically, e.g., to guide the selection among improving-direction generation strategies, is left for future research. Moreover, further redundancy checks and speed-ups for all parts of the separation procedure should also be an interesting topic. Finally, it can be worthwhile to investigate whether the improving-direction bilevel-free sets developed in our work extend to follower objective functions beyond the quadratic case.
